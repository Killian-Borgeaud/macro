Q1Boundary

The Solow steady state $k^*$ satisfies $sf(k^*)=(n+\delta)k^*$. Which is NOT implied by this condition alone?

Try answering in your head first.
A
Output per worker is constant at $y^*=f(k^*)$.
True at steady state — picked by readers who forget the question asks what is NOT implied.
B
Consumption per worker is at its maximum.
Confuses steady state with the Golden Rule (s = α). Tempting because both are 'special' s values.
C
Investment exactly offsets effective depreciation.
Literal restatement of the equation — clearly implied.
D
The capital stock $K_t$ grows at rate $n$.
True since $k = K/L$ is constant and $L$ grows at $n$ — implied.

The steady-state condition pins down where $k$ is constant. Maximizing consumption requires $s = \alpha$ (Golden Rule), a separate condition that the steady-state equation alone does not impose. A, C, D all follow directly from $\dot k = 0$.

Q2Spot the flaw

A student writes: 'Country A and country B have the same $s, n, \delta, \alpha$ but different initial $k_0$. Therefore they have different steady states.' What is wrong?

Try answering in your head first.
A
Nothing — different $k_0$ implies different $k^*$.
Confuses transition dynamics with steady-state determinants.
B
The steady state $k^* = (s/(n+\delta))^{1/(1-\alpha)}$ depends only on parameters, not $k_0$.
Correct: $k_0$ affects the path to $k^*$, not $k^*$ itself.
C
$k^*$ depends on $k_0$ only when $\alpha > 0.5$.
Fabricated condition; $\alpha$ does not gate path-dependence in Solow.
D
Without technological progress, neither country has a steady state.
Wrong — Solow without $g$ still has a steady state in $k$, just not in $y$ growth.

Solow's steady state is determined by parameters $(s,n,\delta,\alpha)$ alone. Different $k_0$ implies the two countries are at different points on the transition path toward the same $k^*$. This is the conditional-convergence prediction.

Q3Deep-short

An estimator satisfies $E[\hat\theta_N] = \theta + 2/N$. Which is true?

Try answering in your head first.
A
Biased and consistent.
Correct: bias $2/N \to 0$.
B
Biased and inconsistent.
Misses that bias vanishes as $N \to \infty$.
C
Unbiased and consistent.
Confuses asymptotic unbiasedness with finite-sample unbiasedness.
D
Unbiased and inconsistent.
Doubly wrong — neither property holds.

Finite-sample bias is $E[\hat\theta_N] - \theta = 2/N \ne 0$ for finite $N$, so biased. But $\lim_{N\to\infty} 2/N = 0$, so the estimator is asymptotically unbiased — and given the bias is the only deviation, also consistent.

Q4What it measures

In the Solow model with land, output is $Y = K^{\alpha}(AL)^{\beta} X^{\gamma}$ with $\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 1$ and fixed land $X$. The quantity $\gamma/\beta$ measures:

Try answering in your head first.
A
The depreciation rate of land.
Land doesn't depreciate in the model; tempting if conflating with $\delta$.
B
The drag on per-capita output growth from population growth, relative to the productivity contribution.
Correct: the steady-state growth rate of $y$ is $g\cdot\beta/(1-\alpha) - n\cdot\gamma/(1-\alpha)$; the ratio $\gamma/\beta$ scales the population drag relative to the technology contribution.
C
The capital share relative to labor share.
That would be $\alpha/\beta$, not $\gamma/\beta$.
D
The land-augmenting technical change rate.
No land-augmenting term exists in this specification.

Per-capita output grows at $g\beta/(1-\alpha) - n\gamma/(1-\alpha)$. The ratio $\gamma/\beta$ is therefore the rate at which population growth drags down $\dot y / y$ per unit of productivity contribution.

Q5Cross-topic

MRW (1992) augment Solow with human capital and find $\alpha + \phi \approx 0.66$, much higher than the capital share $\alpha \approx 0.3$ alone. Which Solow prediction does this revised exponent rescue, and through which mechanism?

Try answering in your head first.
A
It rescues balanced growth by raising the steady-state growth rate.
Confuses level effects with growth-rate effects; $\alpha+\phi$ doesn't change steady-state growth.
B
It rescues the slow observed convergence speed: $\lambda = (1-\alpha-\phi)(n+\delta+g)$ is small when $\alpha+\phi$ is large.
Correct: empirical convergence is ~2%/yr; basic Solow with $\alpha=0.3$ predicts ~5–6%; raising the broad-capital exponent slows predicted convergence to match data.
C
It rescues the assumption of constant returns to scale.
CRS still holds in MRW — $\alpha + \phi + (1-\alpha-\phi) = 1$ across $K, H, L$.
D
It rescues the cross-country dispersion of $k^*$ by raising the elasticity to $\delta$.
MRW's contribution is to the elasticity of $y^*$ with respect to $s$ and $n$, not $\delta$.

The bridge: convergence-speed and investment-rate-effect are the same parameter $1-\alpha-\phi$ wearing two hats. MRW estimate $\alpha+\phi \approx 0.66$ from cross-country regressions, which simultaneously (a) flattens the predicted convergence rate to ~2% and (b) amplifies the elasticity of $y^*$ with respect to $s$ — both empirical puzzles resolved by the same coefficient.

Connection: Links the rate-of-convergence puzzle (asymptotic) to the investment-rate-effect puzzle (cross-section) — same exponent in the production function fixes both.
Q6Multi-select

Which of the following are among Kaldor's stylized facts of growth that the basic Solow model is designed to match? Select all that apply.

Try answering in your head first.
A
Output per worker grows at a roughly constant rate over long horizons.
Yes — Kaldor fact 1; Solow with $g$ generates this.
B
The capital–output ratio $K/Y$ is roughly constant over long horizons.
Yes — Kaldor fact 2; matched in steady state by construction.
C
The labor share of income is roughly constant.
Yes — Kaldor fact 4; matched under Cobb-Douglas.
D
Cross-country income dispersion has narrowed monotonically since 1960.
No — actual data show divergence among non-converging country groups; not a Kaldor fact and Solow doesn't predict it.
Pick all that apply, then submit.

Kaldor's facts the basic Solow targets: constant growth rate of $y$, constant $K/Y$, constant labor share, constant real interest rate, plus growth differentials across countries. D is the opposite of what data show — the unconditional cross-country distribution has widened, which is itself part of the empirical critique of unconditional Solow.

Q7Numeric

With Cobb-Douglas $f(k) = k^{0.5}$, $s = 0.4$, $n = 0$, $\delta = 0.1$, the steady-state capital per worker $k^*$ equals:

Try answering in your head first.
A
4
Correct: $k^* = (0.4/0.1)^{1/(1-0.5)} = 4^2 = 16$. Wait — pick this if you forget to square.
B
8
Off by a factor of 2 — common arithmetic slip.
C
16
Correct: $(s/(n+\delta))^{1/(1-\alpha)} = (0.4/0.1)^2 = 16$.
D
25
Result of $(s/\delta)^{1/\alpha}$ — wrong exponent.

$k^* = (s/(n+\delta))^{1/(1-\alpha)} = (0.4/0.1)^{1/0.5} = 4^2 = 16$. The exponent is $1/(1-\alpha)$, not $1/\alpha$. With $\alpha = 0.5$ the inverse is 2, so we square; with $\alpha = 0.3$ the inverse is $\approx 1.43$.

Q8Graph

Four points are marked on the Solow diagram below. Which is the steady-state capital per worker $k^*$?

. Investment $s\cdot f(k)$ vs effective depreciation $(n+\delta)\cdot k$.

Try answering in your head first.
A
Point A (early on the investment curve, below the depreciation line)
At A, $sf(k) > (n+\delta)k$ — capital is still accumulating; not steady state.
B
Point B (where the two curves intersect)
Correct — intersection is where $\dot k = 0$.
C
Point C (on the depreciation line at high $k$)
C is on the depreciation line but not the investment curve — would be steady state only if it were also an intersection.
D
Point D (further along the investment curve)
D sits on the investment curve at $k$ past the intersection — $sf(k) < (n+\delta)k$ there, so capital is decumulating.

Steady state is where investment equals break-even depreciation, i.e. the two curves intersect. Points on a single curve (A, C, D) are not steady states regardless of which curve they sit on.

Q9Derivation · justify step

The Solow law of motion in per-worker form is derived as below. Which step justifies line (3)?

(1)K_{t+1} = (1-\delta) K_t + s Y_t
(2)k_{t+1} = K_{t+1}/L_{t+1}
(3)k_{t+1}(1+n) = (1-\delta) k_t + s f(k_t)
(4)\Delta k_t \approx s f(k_t) - (n+\delta) k_t
Try answering in your head first.
A
Substituting $L_{t+1} = (1+n)L_t$ and dividing by $L_t$.
Correct — $K_{t+1}/L_t = k_{t+1}(1+n)$ since $L_{t+1}/L_t = 1+n$.
B
Taking the limit as $\delta \to 0$.
$\delta$ is preserved in line (3); no limit taken.
C
Imposing the steady-state condition $\Delta k = 0$.
Steady state isn't used until later; line (3) still has $k_{t+1} \ne k_t$.
D
Linearizing $f(k)$ around $k^*$.
$f$ is left as $f(k_t)$ — no Taylor expansion.

Going from line (1) to line (3) requires dividing both sides by $L_t$ and recognizing $L_{t+1} = (1+n)L_t$, so $K_{t+1}/L_t = k_{t+1} L_{t+1}/L_t = k_{t+1}(1+n)$. Output per worker $Y_t/L_t = f(k_t)$ uses constant returns.

Q10Derivation · source

The steady-state capital per worker in basic Solow is $k^* = \left(\dfrac{s}{n+\delta}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)}$. Which derivation produces this expression?

Try answering in your head first.
A
Set $sf(k^*) = (n+\delta)k^*$ with $f(k)=k^{\alpha}$, rearrange to $(k^*)^{1-\alpha} = s/(n+\delta)$, raise both sides to $1/(1-\alpha)$.
Correct: literal derivation.
B
Maximize consumption $c = (1-s)f(k)$ with respect to $s$, set derivative to zero.
That yields the Golden Rule $\alpha = s$, not $k^*$.
C
Solve the optimal-savings problem $\max \sum \beta^t u(c_t)$ subject to the budget.
That's Ramsey/Cass-Koopmans, not Solow.
D
Take a log-linear approximation of $\dot k / k$ around 0.
Gives convergence speed $\lambda$, not the level $k^*$.

Set $\dot k = 0$, plug in Cobb-Douglas, isolate $k^*$. The exponent $1/(1-\alpha)$ comes from inverting $(k^*)^{1-\alpha}$. B is the Golden Rule problem; C is Ramsey; D is the convergence-rate calculation.

Q11Derivation · why

The open-economy national accounting identity gives $\text{CA} = S - I$. Why does this hold?

Try answering in your head first.
A
Because the central bank fixes the exchange rate.
Identity holds regardless of exchange-rate regime — it's an accounting identity, not a policy result.
B
Because output equals expenditure: $Y = C + I + G + NX$; and saving is $S = Y - C - G$. Substituting gives $S - I = NX = CA$ (abstracting from net factor income).
Correct: pure rearrangement of expenditure + saving definitions; mechanical, not behavioral.
C
Because nominal interest rates equalize across countries.
Confuses uncovered interest parity (a behavioral equilibrium condition) with an accounting identity.
D
Because the marginal propensity to save equals the import share.
Fabricated relationship; no such equality in any standard model.

$\text{CA} = S - I$ is an identity derived from the definition of saving and the expenditure decomposition of $Y$. It holds in every period of every economy regardless of policy, behavior, or equilibrium concept. The other options confuse identities with equilibrium conditions or invent relationships.

Q12Multi true-false

Consider a Cobb-Douglas Solow economy with $\alpha = 0.3$, $\delta = 0.05$, $n = 0.01$, and saving rate $s = 0.25$. The economy sits at its steady state. Evaluate each statement.

Think through the setup first.
ACapital per worker $k$ is constant over time.
By definition of steady state, $\dot k = 0$.
BOutput per worker $y$ grows at rate $g$.
There is no technology term in basic Solow; $y$ is constant in steady state. Picked by students who conflate basic Solow with the Solow-plus-technology variant.
CThe economy is at the Golden Rule.
Golden Rule requires $s = \alpha = 0.3$, but here $s = 0.25$. Tempting because 'steady state' and 'Golden Rule' are introduced near each other.
DThe steady-state rental rate equals $\alpha(n+\delta)/s$.
Plug $k^* = (s/(n+\delta))^{1/(1-\alpha)}$ into $r = \alpha k^{\alpha-1}$ and simplify. Holds at any steady state, GR or not.
Pick T or F for each statement.

The cluster targets the four most common Solow confusions: (i) what 'steady state' actually constrains ($k$, not $y$ growth in basic Solow); (ii) the role of technology in producing growth in $y$ — absent here; (iii) the gap between any steady state and the Golden Rule; (iv) the formula $r^* = \alpha(n+\delta)/s$ that holds at every steady state.

Scenario

Setup. Consider a closed economy in basic Solow with Cobb-Douglas $f(k) = k^{0.3}$, depreciation $\delta = 0.05$, population growth $n = 0.01$, and saving rate $s = 0.2$. The economy is initially at its steady state $k^*_0$. Then the saving rate permanently rises to $s' = 0.3$.

Q13Deep-short

Immediately after the saving rate rises (before $k$ has time to adjust), which is true at the old $k^*_0$?

Try answering in your head first.
A
Investment exceeds depreciation, so $\dot k > 0$.
Correct: $sf(k^*_0) > (n+\delta)k^*_0$ once $s$ rises.
B
Investment equals depreciation; nothing changes until the next period.
Confuses 'still at $k^*_0$' with 'still at steady state' — but the equilibrium condition depends on the new $s$.
C
Output per worker $y$ jumps upward immediately.
$y = f(k)$ depends only on $k$, not $s$; $y$ is unchanged at the instant of the shock.
D
Capital per worker jumps to the new steady state instantly.
Capital evolves continuously; no discrete jump in stocks.

At $k^*_0$, the new investment curve $s'f(k)$ lies above the depreciation line, so $\dot k > 0$ and capital starts accumulating toward the new (higher) steady state.

What is the new steady-state capital per worker $k^*_1$ (to 1 decimal)?

8.6

11.7

C
15.4
Correct: $(0.3/0.06)^{1/0.7} = 5^{1.4286} \approx 9.52$. Wait — pick this if the exponent is mis-applied as $\approx 1.6$.
D
9.5
Correct: $(0.3/0.06)^{1/(1-0.3)} = 5^{1.4286} \approx 9.52$.

$k^*_1 = (s'/(n+\delta))^{1/(1-\alpha)} = (0.3/0.06)^{1/0.7} = 5^{1.4286} \approx 9.5$. The chain rewards keeping the post-shock $s$ straight — easy to mistake for the pre-shock value.
