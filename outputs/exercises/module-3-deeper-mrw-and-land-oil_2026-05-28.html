Anchor

Hint 1. Define $\tilde k_t = K_t/(A_t L_t)$, $\tilde h_t = H_t/(A_t L_t)$. Counterfactual

MRW assumes human capital is accumulated like physical capital: a flow $s_H Y_t$ is invested in H, leaving labour input $L_t$ unchanged. Lucas (1988, briefly restated) proposed instead that workers split their time between production (fraction $u_t$) and learning ($1-u_t$); H grows at a rate proportional to $1-u_t$, and labour entering production is $u_t L_t$ (not $L_t$).

Discuss in rapid prose: (i) which structural feature of MRW (numerically matching the empirical investment-rate elasticity ≈ 1) breaks down in the Lucas formulation, and (ii) which of the five MRW closure assumptions is most disturbed by the time-allocation reinterpretation. No re-derivation — just identify the moving parts.

Solve on paper first.

Anchor

MRW's production function $Y_t = K_t^\alpha H_t^\varphi (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha-\varphi}$ has constant returns to $(K, H, AL)$ jointly — the replication argument. Empirics suggest $\alpha \approx \varphi \approx 1/3$ (Cozzi slide 10). The capital income share is $r_t K_t / Y_t = \alpha$ (Euler); labour earns $1-\alpha$ in total (raw labour share $1-\alpha-\varphi$ plus human-capital share $\varphi$ — inseparable from workers).

The five closure assumptions:

  1. Labour growth: $L_{t+1} = (1+n) L_t$, $n > -1$.
  2. Technology growth: $A_{t+1} = (1+g) A_t$, $g \geq 0$, exogenous and labour-augmenting.
  3. Physical capital accumulation: $K_{t+1} = (1-\delta) K_t + I^K_t$, $\delta \in (0,1)$.
  4. Human capital accumulation: $H_{t+1} = (1-\delta) H_t + I^H_t$, with the SAME $\delta$.
  5. Constant saving rules: $I^K_t = s_K Y_t$, $I^H_t = s_H Y_t$, with $s_K + s_H < 1$.

Main derivation

Step 1 Define $\tilde k_t = K_t/(A_t L_t)$, $\tilde h_t = H_t/(A_t L_t)$. To get the per-effective-worker PF, divide $Y_t = K_t^\alpha H_t^\varphi (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha-\varphi}$ by $A_t L_t$: $\tilde y_t = (K_t/A_t L_t)^\alpha (H_t/A_t L_t)^\varphi (A_t L_t)^{\alpha+\varphi-1+1-\alpha-\varphi} = \tilde k_t^\alpha \tilde h_t^\varphi$. ✓
Step 2 Divide the K-accumulation law $K_{t+1} = (1-\delta) K_t + s_K Y_t$ by $A_{t+1} L_{t+1}$: $\tilde k_{t+1} = (1-\delta) K_t/(A_{t+1} L_{t+1}) + s_K Y_t/(A_{t+1} L_{t+1})$.
Step 3 Use $A_{t+1} L_{t+1} = (1+g)(1+n) A_t L_t$: $K_t/(A_{t+1} L_{t+1}) = \tilde k_t / [(1+n)(1+g)]$ and $Y_t/(A_{t+1} L_{t+1}) = \tilde y_t/[(1+n)(1+g)]$.
Step 4 Substitute: $\tilde k_{t+1} = [(1-\delta)\tilde k_t + s_K \tilde y_t] / [(1+n)(1+g)]$. By the identical argument on the H law: $\tilde h_{t+1} = [(1-\delta)\tilde h_t + s_H \tilde y_t] / [(1+n)(1+g)]$.
MRW transition equations: $\tilde k_{t+1} = \dfrac{s_K \tilde k_t^\alpha \tilde h_t^\varphi + (1-\delta)\tilde k_t}{(1+n)(1+g)}, \quad \tilde h_{t+1} = \dfrac{s_H \tilde k_t^\alpha \tilde h_t^\varphi + (1-\delta)\tilde h_t}{(1+n)(1+g)}.$

Graphically — phase diagram

Step 1 Solow equation: $\Delta\tilde k_t = \tilde k_{t+1} - \tilde k_t = [s_K \tilde y_t - (n+g+\delta+ng) \tilde k_t]/[(1+n)(1+g)]$. (The combined coefficient $n+g+\delta+ng$ comes from $1 - (1-\delta)/[(1+n)(1+g)] = [(1+n)(1+g) - (1-\delta)]/[(1+n)(1+g)] = (n+g+\delta+ng)/[(1+n)(1+g)]$.)
Step 2 — $\Delta\tilde k=0$ locus Setting numerator = 0: $s_K \tilde k^\alpha \tilde h^\varphi = (n+g+\delta+ng) \tilde k$, hence $\tilde h^\varphi = [(n+g+\delta+ng)/s_K] \tilde k^{1-\alpha}$, i.e. $\tilde h = [(n+g+\delta+ng)/s_K]^{1/\varphi} \tilde k^{(1-\alpha)/\varphi}$. Log-log slope: $d\ln\tilde h/d\ln\tilde k = (1-\alpha)/\varphi$.
Step 3 — $\Delta\tilde h=0$ locus Symmetric: $s_H \tilde k^\alpha \tilde h^\varphi = (n+g+\delta+ng) \tilde h$, hence $\tilde h^{1-\varphi} = [s_H/(n+g+\delta+ng)] \tilde k^\alpha$, so $\tilde h = [s_H/(n+g+\delta+ng)]^{1/(1-\varphi)} \tilde k^{\alpha/(1-\varphi)}$. Log-log slope: $\alpha/(1-\varphi)$.
Step 4 — slope comparison With $\alpha = \varphi = 1/3$: slope of $\Delta\tilde k=0$ is $(2/3)/(1/3) = 2$; slope of $\Delta\tilde h=0$ is $(1/3)/(2/3) = 1/2$. The $\Delta\tilde k = 0$ curve is STEEPER. The two upward-sloping curves cross exactly once at $(\tilde k^*, \tilde h^*)$ — uniqueness follows from monotone-and-different slopes plus a Brouwer-style argument on the bounded positive orthant.
Step 5 — regions Above $\Delta\tilde k = 0$: $\tilde h$ is high for given $\tilde k$ → $s_K \tilde y > (n+g+\delta+ng)\tilde k$ → $\Delta\tilde k > 0$ (k̃ grows). Below: k̃ shrinks. Above $\Delta\tilde h = 0$: h̃ is high relative to k̃ → $\Delta\tilde h < 0$. Below: h̃ grows. The four quadrants relative to the SS produce inward arrows; trajectories spiral into $(\tilde k^*, \tilde h^*)$.
Phase diagram: two upward-sloping loci in $(\tilde k, \tilde h)$, with log-log slopes $(1-\alpha)/\varphi > \alpha/(1-\varphi)$. Intersection = steady state, locally stable.

Counterfactual — Lucas (1988)

The empirical feature MRW captures that Lucas-style models struggle with is the magnitude of the cross-country investment-rate elasticity. MRW gives $d\ln \tilde y^* / d\ln s_K = \alpha/(1-\alpha-\varphi) \approx 1$ and a symmetric $\approx 1$ effect from $s_H$ — together producing the observed slope $\approx 2$. Because both $s_K$ and $s_H$ enter as flow shares of $Y$, doubling either roughly doubles its long-run effect through identical algebra.

In Lucas, the analog of $s_H$ is $1-u$, the time fraction allocated to learning. This is dimensionally different: $1-u$ ranges over $[0,1]$ regardless of how productive the economy is, while $s_H Y$ scales with $Y$. The Lucas analog of "increasing $s_H$" maps to "reducing $u$," which costs a proportional $u$ fraction of current production — a level effect, not just a flow-into-stock. Consequence: the long-run elasticity of $y^*$ with respect to learning-time is not the same as MRW's elasticity with respect to $s_H$, and the empirical slope $\approx 2$ no longer falls out cleanly.

The load-bearing MRW closure most disturbed is assumption 5 (constant saving rules with $I^H_t = s_H Y_t$). Replace with $H_{t+1} - (1-\delta)H_t \propto L_t(1-u_t)$ — proportional to labour-time, not output — and the per-effective-worker reduction breaks: $\tilde h$ accumulation no longer depends on $\tilde y$, so the symmetric $(s_K, s_H)$ structure of MRW collapses. One ends up in Uzawa-Lucas territory, where the H sector itself can exhibit constant returns to H and produces endogenous growth without the need for $A_t$ — which is conceptually a different model class (Module 4-style endogenous growth), not just a re-parameterization.

Big-picture takeaway

This exercise is the direct training ground for the most-tested exam pattern in M3: 'describe the model analytically and graphically, then sketch how you would simulate it' (2023 Q3, Undated 1 Q2). The deliverable structure an examiner expects mirrors exactly what we did: (i) state assumptions cleanly so a marker can check the model is correctly set up — this is the 'Anchor' role; (ii) derive transition equations with explicit reference to detrending and the $(1+n)(1+g)$ denominator — the 'analytical' half; (iii) construct the phase diagram with correct slope ordering — the 'graphical' half; (iv) write the Excel pseudocode at the level of 'row $t$ formulas' with a numerical sanity check — the 'simulate' half. The Lucas counterfactual at the end is the kind of higher-order critique that distinguishes a 6/6 answer from a 4/6 answer: it shows you understand which MRW assumption is doing what work, and how the result would change under a credible alternative. Cozzi rewards this kind of structural reasoning over rote recitation. Notice also the deeper methodological point: the slope ordering $(1-\alpha)/\varphi > \alpha/(1-\varphi)$ in the phase diagram is the geometric expression of the asymmetric exponents — when $\alpha = \varphi$ both slopes still differ because the algebraic form $(1-x)/x$ vs $x/(1-x)$ are not symmetric about $x = 1/2$.

Anchor

The MRW economy sits at its positive balanced growth path: $(\tilde k_t, \tilde h_t) = (\tilde k^*, \tilde h^*)$ for all $t$. State precisely, in three columns, which variables in the list $\{K_t, H_t, Y_t, L_t, A_t, k_t, h_t, y_t, \tilde k_t, \tilde h_t, \tilde y_t, r_t, w_t, K_t/Y_t\}$ are (i) constant, (ii) growing exponentially (give the rate), (iii) constant only as a RATIO. No derivation — just classify each.

Hint 1. Anything tilde is constant (by definition of BGP). Anything per-capita (lower-case Latin) grows at $g$. Aggregate variables grow at $g + n + gn \approx g+n$. Factor prices: $r_t$ constant (since $K/Y$ constant), $w_t$ grows at $g$.
Hint 2. $K_t/Y_t$ is a constant ratio — both numerator and denominator grow at $g+n$, so the ratio is pinned.
Main proof

At $t=0$ the economy is at the BGP above. A permanent, unanticipated policy raises $s_K$ from $s_K^{\text{old}}$ to $s_K^{\text{new}} > s_K^{\text{old}}$; $s_H$ is unchanged. Derive analytically — using the closed-form steady-state formulas — by what percentage $\tilde k^*$, $\tilde h^*$, and $\tilde y^*$ rise in the new long-run BGP.

Then make the central observation: $\tilde h^*$ rises even though $s_H$ is unchanged. Identify the algebraic step that produces this and explain the economic mechanism in 2-3 sentences.

Hint 1. Use $\tilde k^* = [s_K^{1-\varphi} s_H^\varphi / (n+g+\delta+ng)]^{1/(1-\alpha-\varphi)}$. Take logs and differentiate w.r.t. $\ln s_K$.
Hint 2. $d\ln\tilde k^*/d\ln s_K = (1-\varphi)/(1-\alpha-\varphi)$. For $\tilde h^*$: $d\ln\tilde h^*/d\ln s_K = \alpha/(1-\alpha-\varphi)$. For $\tilde y^*$: $d\ln\tilde y^*/d\ln s_K = \alpha/(1-\alpha-\varphi)$.
Hint 3. Numerical sanity: $\alpha = \varphi = 1/3$ gives elasticities $(2/3)/(1/3) = 2$ for $\tilde k^*$ and $1$ for both $\tilde h^*$ and $\tilde y^*$. So a 10% rise in $s_K$ raises $\tilde h^*$ by ~10% in the long run despite no change in $s_H$.

Counterfactual

Modify MRW so H accumulates from a separate output sector: $H_{t+1} = (1-\delta) H_t + s_H Z_t$, where $Z_t = B (A_t L_t)^{1-\theta} H_t^\theta$ for some $\theta \in (0,1)$ — that is, the H sector uses H itself plus effective labour but does not use physical capital. Solve on paper first.

Anchor

BGP classification:

  • Constant: $\tilde k_t = \tilde k^*$, $\tilde h_t = \tilde h^*$, $\tilde y_t = \tilde y^*$, $r_t$ (= $\alpha \tilde y^*/\tilde k^*$, all constant), $K_t/Y_t$, $H_t/Y_t$, $K_t/H_t$, factor income shares.
  • Growing at $g$: $k_t = K_t/L_t = \tilde k^* A_t$, $h_t$, $y_t$, $w_t = (1-\alpha) y_t$.
  • Growing at $\approx g+n$: $K_t, H_t, Y_t$ (aggregates); $A_t L_t$ grows at exactly $g + n + gn$.
  • Growing at $n$: $L_t$.
  • Growing at $g$: $A_t$.

The Solow-with-human-capital BGP is the standard balanced-growth structure: all per-effective-worker quantities are pinned; per-capita quantities grow at the rate of labour-augmenting technology $g$; aggregates grow at $g+n$.

Main proof

Step 1 From Lecture 3a slide 18: $\tilde k^* = [s_K^{1-\varphi} s_H^\varphi / (n+g+\delta+ng)]^{1/(1-\alpha-\varphi)}$. Take logs: $\ln \tilde k^* = \frac{1}{1-\alpha-\varphi}[(1-\varphi)\ln s_K + \varphi \ln s_H - \ln(n+g+\delta+ng)]$.
Step 2 Differentiating w.r.t. $\ln s_K$ holding $s_H$ fixed: $\dfrac{d\ln \tilde k^*}{d\ln s_K} = \dfrac{1-\varphi}{1-\alpha-\varphi}$. With $\alpha=\varphi=1/3$: $(2/3)/(1/3) = 2$.
Step 3 Analogously $\tilde h^* = [s_K^\alpha s_H^{1-\alpha} / (n+g+\delta+ng)]^{1/(1-\alpha-\varphi)}$. Log: $\ln \tilde h^* = \frac{1}{1-\alpha-\varphi}[\alpha \ln s_K + (1-\alpha)\ln s_H - \ln(n+g+\delta+ng)]$. So $\dfrac{d\ln \tilde h^*}{d\ln s_K} = \dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha-\varphi} = (1/3)/(1/3) = 1$.
Step 4 And $\tilde y^* = (\tilde k^*)^\alpha (\tilde h^*)^\varphi$, so $\dfrac{d\ln \tilde y^*}{d\ln s_K} = \alpha \cdot \dfrac{1-\varphi}{1-\alpha-\varphi} + \varphi \cdot \dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha-\varphi} = \dfrac{\alpha[(1-\varphi)+\varphi]}{1-\alpha-\varphi} = \dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha-\varphi} = 1$.
Step 5 — interpretation A 10% permanent rise in $s_K$ produces in the long run: $\tilde k^*$ up ~20%, $\tilde h^*$ up ~10%, $\tilde y^*$ up ~10%. The remarkable result: $\tilde h^*$ moves with $s_K$ even though $s_H$ is fixed.
Step 6 — mechanism The algebraic source of $d\ln\tilde h^*/d\ln s_K > 0$ is the term $s_K^\alpha$ in the closed-form for $\tilde h^*$. The economic source: H is accumulated as a fixed flow share of total output $Y$. When $s_K$ rises, $\tilde y^*$ rises (more $\tilde k$ → more output per effective worker), so $s_H \tilde y^*$ — the gross H-investment per effective worker — rises in equilibrium even though the rate $s_H$ is fixed.
Long-run elasticities w.r.t. $s_K$: $\varepsilon_{\tilde k^*} = 2$, $\varepsilon_{\tilde h^*} = 1$, $\varepsilon_{\tilde y^*} = 1$ (for $\alpha=\varphi=1/3$). $\tilde h^*$ rises with $s_K$ via the shared output $Y$ feeding both accumulation laws.

Phase-diagram trajectory

Step 1 — which curve shifts The $\Delta\tilde k=0$ locus is $\tilde h = [(n+g+\delta+ng)/s_K]^{1/\varphi} \tilde k^{(1-\alpha)/\varphi}$. Its prefactor is $\propto s_K^{-1/\varphi}$. So $s_K \uparrow$ shrinks the prefactor → the curve shifts DOWN (for given $\tilde k$, the $\tilde h$ on the locus is now lower) — equivalently it shifts RIGHT (for given $\tilde h$, the $\tilde k$ at which $\Delta\tilde k = 0$ is now larger). Same surface, two equivalent descriptions.
Step 2 — which curve does NOT shift The $\Delta\tilde h=0$ locus is $\tilde h = [s_H/(n+g+\delta+ng)]^{1/(1-\varphi)} \tilde k^{\alpha/(1-\varphi)}$. No $s_K$ in this expression — the locus is invariant. (Intuition: the $\Delta\tilde h=0$ condition is about gross H-investment vs H-replacement; $s_K$ does not appear in either.)
Step 3 — initial sign analysis At $t=0^+$, the system is at the OLD $(\tilde k^*_{\text{old}}, \tilde h^*_{\text{old}})$, which lies on the new $\Delta\tilde h=0$ locus (unchanged) and BELOW the new $\Delta\tilde k=0$ locus (which shifted down). So $\Delta\tilde k > 0$ and $\Delta\tilde h = 0$ initially. Hence $\tilde k$ rises first; $\tilde h$ is stationary for one infinitesimal moment.
Step 4 — subsequent transition Once $\tilde k$ rises, $\tilde y$ rises, so $s_H \tilde y > (n+g+\delta+ng)\tilde h$ — $\Delta\tilde h$ becomes positive. From that point on, both $\tilde k$ and $\tilde h$ rise monotonically toward the new steady state. The trajectory in $(\tilde k, \tilde h)$ space is a curve that starts moving rightward, then bends upward, and approaches $(\tilde k^*_{\text{new}}, \tilde h^*_{\text{new}})$ asymptotically.
Phase-diagram dynamics: $\Delta\tilde k=0$ shifts right, $\Delta\tilde h=0$ fixed. Transition path: monotonically rising in both $\tilde k$ and $\tilde h$, $\tilde k$ leading $\tilde h$.

Methodology reflection

Why $s_K$ affects $\tilde h^*$. The model feature is that H is accumulated as a flow share of TOTAL output $Y$, not from a separate sector. Call this the shared-Y accumulation assumption (closure 5 in Ex 1). It creates an endogenous spillover: anything that raises long-run $\tilde y$ feeds back into the H-accumulation equation through $s_H \tilde y$, even though $s_H$ itself is fixed. The same mechanism makes the model symmetric: raising $s_H$ also raises $\tilde k^*$ (by the same elasticity formulas reflected through $\varphi \leftrightarrow \alpha$).

Why long-run growth of $y_t$ is still $g$. In MRW, technology $A_t$ grows at the EXOGENOUS rate $g$ — it does not depend on $\tilde k$, $\tilde h$, $s_K$, or $s_H$. At BGP, per-capita output is $y_t = \tilde y^* A_t$, so its growth rate equals the growth rate of $A_t$, which is $g$. A change in $s_K$ shifts the LEVEL of $\tilde y^*$ (one-time vertical jump in $\ln y_t$), but cannot shift the SLOPE because the slope is set by $g$ alone. This is the level-vs-growth distinction Cozzi tests on Undated 2 Q3 — and the structural reason exogenous-technology Solow models always give zero permanent growth effects from saving-rate changes.

Counterfactual

With separate-sector H, the H steady-state condition becomes $s_H Z^* = (n+g+\delta+ng) H^*$ — where $Z$ is produced without $K$. Detrend to $\tilde z = Z/(A L)$ and the condition pins $\tilde h^*$ as a function of $s_H, \theta, n, g, \delta$ — but NOT $s_K$. Because $K$ does not appear in $Z$, raising $s_K$ does not raise $\tilde z^*$, and hence does not raise $\tilde h^*$. The endogenous spillover disappears.

The deeper point: MRW's elegant symmetry between $s_K$ and $s_H$ (and the doubled-elasticity result that fixes the empirical investment puzzle) is not robust to small modeling changes. Once you separate the H technology even a little — letting H production not use $K$ — the symmetry breaks and $s_K$ no longer drags $\tilde h^*$ up with it. This is one reason the Uzawa-Lucas literature (where H-sector is structurally distinct) ends up with different empirical implications than MRW.

Big-picture takeaway

The level-vs-growth-rate distinction in this exercise is the single most-tested concept in M3. Every 'shock at BGP' question (2023 Q2, Undated 2 Q3) requires the same decomposition: a parameter change shifts the LEVEL of the steady state (computed via elasticities), while the long-run GROWTH RATE remains pinned by exogenous technology $g$. The transition path in the phase diagram visualises this: the system moves along a curve to a new $(\tilde k^*, \tilde h^*)$, accumulating a one-time level gain, with the slope of $\ln y_t$ identical in the long run before and after the shock. The fact that $\tilde h^*$ moves with $s_K$ — even though $s_H$ is constant — is the model's most subtle prediction and a frequent quiz / exam trap (cf. M3 quiz Q3). The counterfactual reveals this prediction is fragile: it relies entirely on the shared-$Y$ assumption (closure 5). Separating the H sector breaks the symmetry. A 6/6 exam answer here would name the assumption, trace through the algebra of the elasticity, sketch the phase-diagram trajectory with both loci labelled, AND flag the counterfactual sensitivity — explicitly demonstrating that the answer is not just 'I memorised the elasticity formula' but 'I understand which structural assumption produces this result, and what would break it.' Methodology reflection

Three structural reasons only $\alpha+\varphi$ matters. (i) Cobb-Douglas multiplicativity: $\tilde k^\alpha \tilde h^\varphi = (\tilde k \tilde h^{\varphi/\alpha})^\alpha$, so on any fixed $\tilde h/\tilde k$ ridge, $\tilde y$ is a power function of $\tilde k$ with exponent $\alpha+\varphi$. This works because of the multiplicative form; CES (additive) breaks it. (ii) Shared accumulation: the Solow equations have identical structure for $\tilde k$ and $\tilde h$ — same $\delta$, same $Y$, same $(1+n)(1+g)$ — so dividing them gives $\tilde k/\tilde h = s_K/s_H$ independent of $\alpha, \varphi$ individually. (iii) Geometric meaning: in $(\tilde k, \tilde h)$ space, the SS ratio is a ray from the origin with slope $s_H/s_K$. Trajectories converge to this ray fast (the off-ridge eigenvalue), then crawl along the ridge toward the steady state — and along the ridge, the system is 1-D Solow.

Practical empirical consequence: cross-country regressions of $\ln y^*$ on $\ln s_K$ and $\ln s_H$ identify their sum (via the broad-capital share) much better than they identify the individual elasticities. To pin $\alpha$ vs $\varphi$ separately, one needs information off-ridge — e.g. countries far from BGP, or data on the relative speed of $\tilde k$ and $\tilde h$ adjustment. In practice this is hard, and the MRW empirics yield wide confidence intervals on the individual shares.

Falsification probe — CES

Step 1 — SS ratio still pinned The accumulation laws are unchanged. At SS, $s_K \tilde y^* = (n+g+\delta+ng)\tilde k^*$ and $s_H \tilde y^* = (n+g+\delta+ng)\tilde h^*$, regardless of the form of $\tilde y(\tilde k, \tilde h)$. Dividing: $\tilde k^*/\tilde h^* = s_K/s_H$ still. ✓
Step 2 — no 1-D collapse Substitute $\tilde h = (s_H/s_K)\tilde k$ into the CES form: $\tilde y = [\alpha \tilde k^\rho + \varphi (s_H/s_K)^\rho \tilde k^\rho + (1-\alpha-\varphi)]^{1/\rho} = [\tilde k^\rho(\alpha + \varphi (s_H/s_K)^\rho) + (1-\alpha-\varphi)]^{1/\rho}$. The constant term $(1-\alpha-\varphi)$ — which represents "raw labour" in effective units — does not factor out. So $\tilde y$ on the ridge is NOT a pure power function of $\tilde k$.
Step 3 — $\rho$ enters separately Because the algebra above produces a bracketed expression depending on $\rho$, the local linearization for convergence speed inherits $\rho$. The clean formula $\lambda = (1-\alpha-\varphi)(n+g+\delta)$ no longer holds — it gets corrected by terms involving $\sigma = 1/(1-\rho)$. So under CES, knowing $\alpha+\varphi$ is no longer enough to predict the empirical convergence speed.
Step 4 — Lucas (1990) connection The Lucas puzzle: poor (low-H) countries don't see massive capital inflows even though their capital-output ratios are low. Under Cobb-Douglas, MPK $= \alpha Y/K \to \infty$ as $K \to 0$ for any $H > 0$ — so any low-K country has stratospheric MPK and should attract capital. The puzzle cannot arise. Under CES with $\rho < 0$ ($\sigma < 1$, complements), low $H$ caps the MPK of $K$: a low-H country has bounded MPK no matter how low $K$ falls. THIS is consistent with the Lucas observation. So the Lucas puzzle is evidence that the world is NOT well-described by Cobb-Douglas — capital and human capital are complements, not unit-elastic substitutes.
Under CES with $\sigma \ne 1$: the 1-D reduction fails; $\rho$ enters $\lambda$ and the elasticities; the Lucas puzzle becomes possible if $\sigma < 1$ (complementarity).

Big-picture takeaway

Three meta-lessons. First, the empirical 'success' of MRW is structurally narrow: the model fixes both empirical problems via a single quantity (the broad-capital share $\alpha+\varphi$), and any model that raised the broad share — physical-only Solow with $\alpha \approx 2/3$, AK models, externality models with effective share $\approx 2/3$ — would give the same numerical fix. MRW's specific identification 'broad capital = K + H' is one consistent story among many. Second, the Cobb-Douglas assumption is doing more work than the labour-vs-capital share assumption: it's what enables the 1-D reduction and makes the elegant elasticity formulas possible. Under CES, the model becomes computationally similar but structurally different — convergence speed and elasticities pick up $\sigma$ dependence that breaks the universality of the broad-capital interpretation. Third, the Lucas (1990) connection brings the CES question to life: the puzzle of why capital doesn't flow to poor countries is, in the MRW framework, a fact that COULDN'T arise if Cobb-Douglas were exactly right. Empirically the puzzle does arise — so we have implicit evidence that $\sigma < 1$ in cross-country production, and hence MRW's specific elasticity numbers ($\approx 1$) are sensitive to the production-function form. This kind of structural sensitivity is exactly what Cozzi expects you to articulate in a 6/6 answer to an 'evidence for and against' question. Note also: the elasticity-of-substitution literature (Klump, McAdam, Willman) finds empirical estimates of $\sigma \in (0.4, 0.8)$ for aggregate production, consistent with complementarity and inconsistent with strict Cobb-Douglas.

Practical exam advice: when an exam question asks 'does MRW resolve the Solow puzzles', the safe-pass answer derives the elasticity and convergence formulas with $\alpha+\varphi = 2/3$ and points to the numerical fit. The 6/6 answer adds the structural observation that the resolution rides on a single quantity (broad-capital share), then notes that the form-of-production matters (CES vs CD) — leaving the marker with no doubt that you have moved past 'I know the result' to 'I understand which assumptions produce it and which would break it.' Source: both per-capita land $x_t = X/L_t$ and per-capita extraction $e_t = E_t/L_t$ shrink as the population grows (with $X$ fixed and $E$ growing slower than $L$). Assumption: $X$ is in fixed supply; $E$ does not depend on $L$. Zero this drag: set $n = 0$ (zero population growth) — Cozzi's modern case.

(3) Depletion drag — $-\epsilon s_E /(\beta+\kappa+\epsilon) = -0.125 s_E$. Source: $E_t$ shrinks at rate $s_E$ as the oil reserve depletes. Assumption: $s_E$ is constant; no technological progress in extraction. Zero this drag: set $s_E = 0$ (no extraction — oil reserve never used, land model only) or $\epsilon = 0$ (oil not a productive input).

Shock comparative statics

Baseline BGP $g^y = 0.75 g - 0.25 n - 0.125 s_E = 0.75(0.024) - 0.25(0.01) - 0.125(0.005) = 0.018 - 0.0025 - 0.000625 = 0.014875 \approx 1.5\%$/yr. ✓ (matches Cozzi p21)
Q1 — long-run growth rate $\partial g^y / \partial s_E = -\epsilon/(\beta+\kappa+\epsilon) = -0.125$. So $\Delta g^y = -0.125 \cdot \Delta s_E = -0.125 \cdot (-0.002) = +0.00025 = +0.025\%$/yr. New $g^y \approx 1.525\%$/yr. Growth rate RISES because less extraction means a smaller depletion drag.
Q2 — immediate level effect At $t=0^+$, $E_0$ drops from $0.005 R_0$ to $0.003 R_0$. From the PF, $\partial \ln Y / \partial \ln E = \epsilon$. So $\Delta \ln Y_0 = \epsilon \cdot \Delta \ln E_0 = 0.1 \cdot \ln(0.003/0.005) = 0.1 \cdot (-0.5108) = -0.05108$. Per-capita $y$ drops by ~5.1% instantly (capital and labour don't reallocate instantaneously, so the only operative channel is the lower $E$ input).
Q3 — crossover horizon Post-shock trajectory: $\ln y_t^{\text{new}} = \ln y_t^{\text{old}} - 0.0511 + 0.00025 \cdot t$. Set the bracket = 0: $t^* = 0.0511 / 0.00025 = 204$ years. For $t < 204$ yr: shock is welfare-reducing; for $t > 204$ yr: shock is welfare-improving.
Permanent $s_E \downarrow$ shock: immediate -5.1\% per-capita GDP, BGP growth $+0.025$ pp/yr, crossover at ~204 yr. Slightly welfare-improving for the very long run; substantial cost for current and near-future citizens. A discount-rate calculation would tell you whether net PV is positive — at any reasonable $\rho > 0.025\%$/yr, the answer is NO. Hence "keep extracting oil" is the impatience-rational policy, even though future generations would be richer with restraint.

Counterfactual

Step 1 — re-derive under decreasing returns Suppose $\alpha+\beta+\kappa+\epsilon = 1 - \xi$ with $\xi > 0$. Growth accounting still gives $g_t^y = \alpha g_t^k + \beta g - (\kappa+\epsilon)n - \epsilon s_E$. Impose $g^y = g^k$: $(1-\alpha) g^y = \beta g - (\kappa+\epsilon)n - \epsilon s_E$.
Step 2 — the step that fails The BGP CONDITION fails. With strictly decreasing returns to joint inputs, doubling $K$ less than doubles $Y$, so $K/Y$ rises monotonically as the economy accumulates capital — $z_t$ never stops growing. Hence there is NO BGP at which $g^y = g^k$. The 'derivation' above is fictitious: you can set up the equation $(1-\alpha) g^y = \ldots$, but the premise (constant $z_t$) is never satisfied. The growth path features ever-rising $K/Y$ and ever-falling MPK; output growth converges to zero asymptotically.
Step 3 — CRS defense The replication argument (Cozzi slide 5): consider building a second factory next to the first, with identical $K, L, X, E$ inputs. Total output is exactly twice the original — by physical replicability. This is what CRS in $(K, L, X, E)$ jointly means. Strict decreasing returns would require that one of the inputs has a 'last unit doing double duty' — possible if there were an unmodeled fixed factor (e.g. management talent), but in a model that has already listed all inputs explicitly, CRS is the defensible default.

Big-picture takeaway

This exercise drills the second-most-tested M3 skill: trace a permanent shock at the BGP through both LEVEL and GROWTH-RATE channels (2023 Q2, Undated 2 Q3). The clean separation works because the shock affects $g^y$ via the depletion-drag term (long-run growth channel) AND affects $Y_0$ via the input-cut channel (immediate level loss). Quantitatively, the long-run gain is small per year (a few basis points of $g^y$) while the immediate loss is large (several percent of $Y$). The crossover horizon ($\sim 200$ yr) is decades beyond any reasonable discount horizon, which is why 'reduce oil extraction' policies are hard to justify on standard discounted-utility grounds even though they make the very-long-run economy richer. The CRS counterfactual reinforces a methodological point: balanced growth in this class of models is not just a mathematical convenience — it is structurally required by CRS. Models with decreasing returns to joint inputs do not admit a BGP at any positive growth rate; they grow ever-slower and approach a stagnation steady state. This is why the classical economists (Smith, Ricardo, Malthus) were structurally pessimistic — they implicitly assumed a non-CRS world in which the fixed factor land was binding. Cozzi's framing of 'modern' vs 'classical' case is precisely the conditions under which the CRS-with-positive-tech-growth combination keeps $g^y > 0$ vs the no-tech / high-$n$ regime where $g^y < 0$. The 3-drag formula is one equation that encodes both regimes — the magic of having $g$ as the only positive driver and three subtractions.

Main proof — transition curve and 4 properties

Step 1 — start from accumulation $K_{t+1} = (1-\delta) K_t + s Y_t = Y_t [s + (1-\delta) z_t]$, using $K_t = z_t Y_t$.
Step 2 — output ratio $Y_{t+1}/Y_t = (K_{t+1}/K_t)^\alpha \cdot ((A_{t+1} L_{t+1})/(A_t L_t))^\beta \cdot (X/X)^\kappa$. Using $K_{t+1}/K_t = K_{t+1}/(z_t Y_t) = [s + (1-\delta)z_t]/z_t$ and $A_{t+1} L_{t+1}/(A_t L_t) = (1+g)(1+n)$: $Y_{t+1}/Y_t = \left(\frac{s+(1-\delta)z_t}{z_t}\right)^\alpha \cdot [(1+g)(1+n)]^\beta$.
Step 3 — combine $z_{t+1} = K_{t+1}/Y_{t+1} = (Y_t [s + (1-\delta)z_t]) / Y_{t+1} = (s + (1-\delta)z_t) / (Y_{t+1}/Y_t)$. Transition curve: $z_{t+1} = f(z_t) = \dfrac{[s + (1-\delta) z_t]^{1-\alpha} z_t^\alpha}{[(1+n)(1+g)]^\beta}$.
Property 1: $f(0) = 0$ At $z_t = 0$: $f(0) = [s + 0]^{1-\alpha} \cdot 0^\alpha / [\ldots] = 0$ because the $z_t^\alpha$ factor vanishes (with $\alpha > 0$). Requires: $\alpha > 0$ — i.e. capital is a productive input. (If $\alpha = 0$, capital is irrelevant and the model degenerates.)
Property 2: $f$ monotone increasing $f(z) = C \cdot [s + (1-\delta) z]^{1-\alpha} z^\alpha$. Both factors are increasing in $z$ for $z > 0$, so $f$ is monotone. Requires: $\alpha, 1-\alpha > 0$ and $1-\delta > 0$ (depreciation less than 100% per period — Cozzi's $\delta \in (0,1)$).
Property 3: single positive fixed point Set $z_{t+1} = z_t = z$ in the formula: $z = C [s + (1-\delta) z]^{1-\alpha} z^\alpha$, hence $z^{1-\alpha} = C [s + (1-\delta)z]^{1-\alpha}$, hence $z = C^{1/(1-\alpha)} [s + (1-\delta) z]$. Let $\tilde C = C^{1/(1-\alpha)} = [(1+n)(1+g)]^{-\beta/(1-\alpha)}$. Then $z[1 - \tilde C(1-\delta)] = \tilde C s$, giving $z^* = \dfrac{s}{[(1+n)(1+g)]^{\beta/(\beta+\kappa)} - (1-\delta)}$ (using $1-\alpha = \beta+\kappa$). With $n=0.01, g=0.02, \delta=0.05, \beta=0.7, \kappa=0.1$: $(1.01)(1.02) = 1.0302 > 0.95^{8/7} \approx 0.943$. ✓
Property 4: $f'(0) > 1$ Differentiate: $f'(z) = C[s+(1-\delta)z]^{-\alpha}[(1-\alpha)(1-\delta) z + \alpha (s + (1-\delta)z)]$ — split via product rule on $[s+(1-\delta)z]^{1-\alpha} z^\alpha$. At $z \to 0^+$: $f'(z) \to C \cdot s^{-\alpha} \cdot \alpha s = C \alpha s^{1-\alpha} \cdot (\text{a } z^{\alpha-1} \text{ factor missed above})$. Better: write $f(z) \approx C s^{1-\alpha} z^\alpha$ for small $z$, so $f'(z) \approx C s^{1-\alpha} \alpha z^{\alpha-1}$. As $z \to 0$ with $\alpha < 1$, this DIVERGES to $+\infty$, certainly $> 1$. Requires: $\alpha < 1$ (concavity of $K$ in PF) — the Inada-like condition forcing the slope at the origin to exceed 1.
Four properties (slide 11): $f(0)=0$, $f$ monotone increasing, exactly one positive fixed point $z^*$, $f'(0) = \infty > 1$. Together: trajectory starting at any $z_0 > 0$ converges monotonically to $z^*$.

Methodology reflection

General principle: When two variables $A_t$ and $B_t$ both grow without bound but at the same long-run rate, their ratio $A_t/B_t$ converges. Detrending — picking the right ratio variable — is what turns a non-stationary dynamical system into a stationary one whose steady state can be analyzed. The 'right' ratio is whatever scaling-invariance the BGP imposes.

Other ratio variables in the course:

  • $\tilde k = K/(AL)$ in basic Solow / general Solow (Modules 1-2): both $K$ and $AL$ grow at $g+n$ at BGP; the ratio is constant.
  • Factor income shares: $rK/Y$ and $wL/Y$ are ratios of growing aggregates; under CRS Cobb-Douglas they are constant ($\alpha$ and $1-\alpha$).
  • Looking ahead to Module 4 (R&D): $L_A/L$, the fraction of workers in R&D, is a ratio variable that can converge to a finite limit even as $L$ grows.
  • Module 6 (efficiency wages): $U/L$, the unemployment rate, is a ratio of two growing aggregates (in equilibrium both grow at $n$) that converges to a finite constant.

Big-picture takeaway

The transition curve $z_{t+1} = f(z_t)$ is the methodological centerpiece of M3's land model: a single 1-D recursion that captures the full dynamics of an otherwise complex multi-variable system. The four properties — and especially $f'(0) > 1$, which guarantees that any positive starting point is attracted to $z^*$ rather than to the trivial zero fixed point — are the technical content of 'balanced growth exists and is stable' in this model. The depleting-land counterfactual at the end shows that the model's compartmentalization into 'land' (fixed) vs 'oil' (extracted) is pedagogical, not structural: mathematically they collapse to the same object. The methodological reflection on ratio variables is what generalizes: any 'detrending' procedure is the identification of the unique ratio (or combination) that is invariant under the BGP scaling. In each module of the course you've now seen one — $\tilde k$ in basic Solow, $\tilde k$ in MRW, $z = K/Y$ in land, factor income shares everywhere, and $U/L$ in Module 6 — and the technique is always the same: find what is constant at BGP, that's your state variable.
