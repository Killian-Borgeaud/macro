Scope: Module 3 Lecture 3a §3.1-§3.2 (factor prices and income shares)
Anchor

State in one sentence what it means for human capital to be inseparable from workers in MRW, and explain — in one further sentence — what this implies operationally for marginal-product calculations: when differentiating $Y$ with respect to $L$, which quantity must we hold fixed, $H_t$ or $h_t = H_t/L_t$? (No derivation required.)

Hint 1. Inseparability means there is no separate market for human-capital services; the wage compensates raw labour PLUS the human capital embedded in workers.
Hint 2. Operationally: hiring one more worker brings the average per-worker human capital along automatically, so $h_t$ stays fixed while $H_t = h_t L_t$ rises by exactly $h_t$.
Main proof

Starting from the aggregate MRW production function $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha} H_t^{\phi} (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha-\phi}$, derive the rental rate $r_t$ and the wage $w_t$ from the firm's profit-maximisation FOCs. Show explicitly where the inseparability anchor enters the wage derivation. Then compute the income shares $r_t K_t / Y_t$ and $w_t L_t / Y_t$.

Hint 1. Before differentiating with respect to $L_t$, rewrite $H_t = h_t L_t$ so the production function exposes $L_t$ to the exponent $\phi + (1-\alpha-\phi) = 1-\alpha$.
Hint 2. For $r_t$: differentiate with respect to $K_t$, holding everything else fixed. You don't need to worry about the inseparability for this one — $K_t$ and $L_t$ vary independently.
Hint 3. For $w_t$: differentiate with respect to $L_t$, holding $K_t, A_t, h_t$ fixed (NOT $H_t$). The exponent on $L_t$ in the rewritten form is $1-\alpha$, so $\partial Y/\partial L = (1-\alpha)\,Y/L$.
Hint 4. For income shares: $r_t K_t = \alpha\,Y_t$ in one line. The labour share is $1-\alpha$ — NOT $1-\alpha-\phi$. The 'extra' $\phi$ is the human-capital return, which flows to workers through the wage.

(b) What implied labour share does this give, in terms of $\alpha$ and $\phi$? (c) At $\alpha = \phi = 1/3$, how does the wrong labour share compare to the data, and what's the conceptual error?

Hint 1. If you hold $H_t$ fixed, the exponent on $L_t$ in $Y = K^\alpha H^\phi (AL)^{1-\alpha-\phi}$ is $1-\alpha-\phi$ — not $1-\alpha$.

Solve on paper first.

Anchor

Human capital in MRW is bundled with the workers who carry it: every worker is endowed with the same per-capita stock $h_t = H_t/L_t$, and there is NO separate market for human-capital services — its return is paid through the wage. Operationally, this means that when the firm computes the marginal product of labour ($\partial Y/\partial L$), it must hold $h_t$ fixed (not $H_t$), because hiring one additional worker brings $h_t$ extra units of human capital along automatically.

Main proof

Start by rewriting the production function in a form that exposes the inseparability. Substitute $H_t = h_t L_t$ into $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha} H_t^{\phi} (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha-\phi}$:

Step 1 $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha} (h_t L_t)^{\phi} (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha-\phi} = K_t^{\alpha} h_t^{\phi} L_t^{\phi} A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi} L_t^{1-\alpha-\phi}$.
Step 2 Collect the $L_t$ exponents: $\phi + (1-\alpha-\phi) = 1-\alpha$. So $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha} h_t^{\phi} A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi} L_t^{1-\alpha}$. (★)

Notice the exponent on $L_t$ in (★) is $1-\alpha$, NOT $1-\alpha-\phi$. That's because $H_t = h_t L_t$ already carries some of the $L_t$ exponents along.

Rental rate $r_t$

Step 1 Differentiate (★) with respect to $K_t$ (holding $L_t, A_t, h_t$ fixed): $\partial Y_t/\partial K_t = \alpha K_t^{\alpha-1} h_t^{\phi} A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi} L_t^{1-\alpha}.$
Step 2 Equivalent per-effective-worker form: $r_t = \alpha\,(K_t/(A_tL_t))^{\alpha-1}\,(H_t/(A_tL_t))^{\phi}.$ (Verify by expanding: $(K/(AL))^{\alpha-1}(H/(AL))^\phi \cdot A_t = K^{\alpha-1}A^{-(\alpha-1)}L^{-(\alpha-1)}\cdot h^\phi A^{-\phi}\cdot \ldots$ matches up to the $A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi}L^{1-\alpha}$ from (★).)

Wage $w_t$ (this is where inseparability bites)

Step 1 Differentiate (★) with respect to $L_t$, holding $K_t, A_t, h_t$ fixed (NOT $H_t$): $\partial Y_t/\partial L_t\big|_{K,A,h} = (1-\alpha)\,K_t^{\alpha} h_t^{\phi} A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi} L_t^{-\alpha}.$
Step 2 The exponent on $L_t$ in (★) is $1-\alpha$, so differentiation produces $(1-\alpha) L_t^{-\alpha}$. Note that $h_t$ does NOT contribute because it is held fixed.
Step 3 In per-effective-worker form: $w_t = (1-\alpha)\,(K_t/(A_tL_t))^{\alpha}\,(H_t/(A_tL_t))^{\phi}\,A_t$.

Income shares

Step 1 Multiply $r_t$ by $K_t$: $r_t K_t = \alpha\,K_t^{\alpha} h_t^{\phi} A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi} L_t^{1-\alpha}$. The right-hand side equals $\alpha\,Y_t$ by direct comparison to (★).
Step 2 Multiply $w_t$ by $L_t$: $w_t L_t = (1-\alpha)\,K_t^{\alpha} h_t^{\phi} A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi} L_t^{1-\alpha} = (1-\alpha)\,Y_t$.
Income shares: $\dfrac{r_t K_t}{Y_t} = \alpha,\qquad \dfrac{w_t L_t}{Y_t} = 1-\alpha.$

The capital share equals $\alpha$ (same as in basic Solow). The labour share equals $1-\alpha$ — and crucially, this lump sum includes BOTH the compensation for raw labour AND the return to embedded human capital. There is no separate income flow to human-capital owners; the $\phi$ that would have gone to human capital flows through the wage to workers, because the workers ARE the human capital.

Counterfactual: holding $H_t$ fixed by mistake

Suppose a student instead uses the unsubstituted form $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha} H_t^{\phi} (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha-\phi}$ and differentiates with respect to $L_t$ holding $H_t$ fixed.

Step 1 The exponent on $L_t$ in this form is $1-\alpha-\phi$, so $\partial Y_t/\partial L_t\big|_{K,A,H} = (1-\alpha-\phi)\,K_t^{\alpha} H_t^{\phi} A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi} L_t^{-\alpha-\phi}$.
Step 2 Multiplying by $L_t$: $w_t^{\text{wrong}} L_t = (1-\alpha-\phi)\,Y_t$. The implied labour share is $1-\alpha-\phi$.
Step 3 At $\alpha = \phi = 1/3$: wrong labour share $= 1/3$. But the empirical labour share is around $2/3$. So this mistake would say labour earns half of what it actually earns — a 100% miscalculation.

The conceptual error: holding $H_t$ fixed amounts to treating human capital as a SEPARATE factor that the firm could hire independently of workers — like another machine. In reality, every worker comes attached with $h_t$ units of $H$, so hiring one more worker raises $H_t$ automatically by $h_t$. Holding $H_t$ artificially fixed denies the firm this 'free' bundled increase, which is why the implied marginal product of labour comes out too small.

The lesson is structural: in basic Solow, $K$ and $L$ are genuinely separable factors, and the marginal-product computations are routine. In MRW, the labour market becomes the unique vehicle through which human-capital income reaches its owners — so the wage absorbs the $\phi$ exponent automatically, and the labour share in NIPA-style data tracks $1-\alpha$, not $1-\alpha-\phi$.

Why this matters for measurement

The inseparability has a practical consequence for empirical work. National-accounts statisticians cannot directly observe how much of measured wages compensates raw labour versus embedded human capital — the two flows are bundled together in a single wage payment. To estimate $\phi$ separately from $1-\alpha-\phi$ you need auxiliary information about the wage premium for education, the cost of forgone earnings during schooling, or the wage spread between skilled and unskilled workers (MRW use the ratio of the average wage to the minimum wage in US manufacturing for this — they argue it implies $\phi \approx 1/3$).

A second consequence is for the firm side. Profit-maximisation under perfect competition gives BOTH the right rental rate $r_t = \alpha\,Y_t/K_t$ and the right wage $w_t = (1-\alpha)\,Y_t/L_t$, but the firm does NOT 'know' that some of the wage is really compensation for human capital — it just hires labour bundled with whatever human capital workers happen to embody. Workers internalise the decomposition only when deciding whether to invest in their own education (the macroeconomy's $s_H$ choice), since that decision compares the future wage premium of an educated worker against the current consumption sacrifice. So the inseparability creates an interior question — "how much should you invest in education?" — that is conceptually distinct from the firm's choice over $K$, and motivates the separate Golden-Rule treatment of $s_H$ in Exercise 3.

Finally, the result that the LABOUR share equals $1-\alpha$ (not $1-\alpha-\phi$) is what makes the MRW model EMPIRICALLY CONSISTENT with the observed labour share. The basic Solow model already nailed labour share $\approx 2/3$ at $\alpha = 1/3$; MRW preserves this victory because the extra $\phi$ flows through the wage. If we had instead built a model where $H$ were separately marketed with its own price (a 'human-capital rental market'), we'd predict a labour share of only $1-\alpha-\phi \approx 1/3$ — strongly contradicted by data. The MRW formulation is therefore not just a mathematical convenience; it's the right structural model of how human capital actually enters the labour market.

Exam connection and common pitfalls

On a Cozzi-style exam, this derivation can come up in two forms. The first is the explicit ask: 'derive the factor prices and income shares in the Solow model with human capital.' If you see this, work through the steps above carefully — the load-bearing step is the rewriting $H_t = h_t L_t$ before differentiating with respect to $L_t$, which is the move that exposes the correct labour exponent $1-\alpha$. The second form is implicit: a question about 'why is human capital treated differently from physical capital in MRW?' or 'discuss why the empirical labour share is consistent with MRW'. Both questions are answered by the inseparability argument — that human capital cannot be hired separately from workers, so its return flows through the wage and is invisible in factor-share data taken at face value.

Two common pitfalls to avoid. Pitfall 1: writing the wage as $(1-\alpha-\phi) Y/L$ — this is what you get if you mistakenly hold $H$ fixed, and it's wrong. Always rewrite $H = hL$ first, or remember that $h$ is what's truly fixed when one extra worker is hired. Pitfall 2: conflating the wage's two components conceptually. The wage is a single market price, paid by firms to workers; it doesn't unbundle into 'raw labour wage' and 'human capital rental' anywhere in the model. The decomposition $(1-\alpha) = (1-\alpha-\phi) + \phi$ is something WE do to interpret the wage; it's not visible to the firm or the worker. This bundling is also what makes empirical identification of $\phi$ hard — you can't observe the two components separately, only their sum.

Exercise 2MRW steady state and its elasticities, two ways
Scope: Module 3 Lecture 3a §3.3-§3.4 (Solow equations), §4.1-§4.2 (steady state, elasticities)
Anchor

Write down the MRW Solow equations in per-effective-worker form (one each for $\tilde k$ and $\tilde h$) and state the definition of a steady state. Use $D \equiv n+g+\delta+ng$ as shorthand.

Hint 1. From the transition equations $\tilde k_{t+1} = (s_K \tilde k_t^\alpha \tilde h_t^\phi + (1-\delta)\tilde k_t)/((1+n)(1+g))$, subtract $\tilde k_t$ from both sides to get $(1+n)(1+g)\Delta\tilde k_t = s_K \tilde k_t^\alpha \tilde h_t^\phi - D\tilde k_t$.
Hint 2. A steady state is a positive pair $(\tilde k^*, \tilde h^*)$ at which both $\Delta\tilde k = 0$ and $\Delta\tilde h = 0$.
Main proof

Solve the steady-state equations explicitly for $\tilde k^*$, $\tilde h^*$, and $\tilde y^*$ (the latter via $\tilde y^* = \tilde k^{*\alpha}\tilde h^{*\phi}$). Then take logs of $\tilde y^*$ and read off the three elasticities of $\tilde y^*$ with respect to $s_K$, $s_H$, and $D$. Show every algebraic step.

Hint 1. From $\Delta\tilde k=0$: rearrange to $\tilde k^{*1-\alpha} = (s_K/D)\,\tilde h^{*\phi}$. From $\Delta\tilde h=0$: $\tilde h^{*1-\phi} = (s_H/D)\,\tilde k^{*\alpha}$.
Hint 2. From the second relation, solve for $\tilde h^*$ in terms of $\tilde k^*$, then substitute into the first. You'll get a single equation in $\tilde k^*$ alone with exponent $(1-\alpha-\phi)/(1-\phi)$.
Hint 3. Simplifying the exponent: $(1-\alpha) - \alpha\phi/(1-\phi) = [(1-\alpha)(1-\phi) - \alpha\phi]/(1-\phi) = (1-\alpha-\phi)/(1-\phi)$. The $\alpha\phi$ terms cancel.
Hint 4. Once you have $\tilde k^*$, the symmetric formula for $\tilde h^*$ follows by relabelling. For $\tilde y^*$: combine the $s_K$ and $s_H$ exponents using $\alpha(1-\phi) + \alpha\phi = \alpha$ and similarly for $s_H$.
Method variation

Now derive the SAME three elasticities without solving for $\tilde k^*, \tilde h^*$ explicitly. Take logs of the steady-state equations to get a linear system in $(\ln\tilde k^*, \ln\tilde h^*)$ with $(\ln s_K, \ln s_H, \ln D)$ as right-hand side. Implicit-differentiate, solve for $\partial\ln\tilde k^*/\partial \ln s_K$ and $\partial\ln\tilde h^*/\partial \ln s_K$, then combine via $\ln\tilde y^* = \alpha\ln\tilde k^* + \phi\ln\tilde h^*$. Show the linear system and its solution.

Hint 1. Taking logs of $\Delta\tilde k=0$ yields $(\alpha-1)\ln\tilde k^* + \phi\ln\tilde h^* = \ln D - \ln s_K$. Symmetric equation for $\tilde h$.
Hint 2. Coefficient matrix $M = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha-1 & \phi\\\alpha & \phi-1\end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $\det M = (\alpha-1)(\phi-1) - \alpha\phi = 1 - \alpha - \phi$ (verify by expanding).
Hint 3. Take partials of both equations with respect to $\ln s_K$, then solve the resulting 2×2 system. You'll find $\partial\ln\tilde k^*/\partial\ln s_K = (1-\phi)/(1-\alpha-\phi)$ and $\partial\ln\tilde h^*/\partial\ln s_K = \alpha/(1-\alpha-\phi)$.
Methodology reflection

(a) Identify the load-bearing condition that BOTH methods rely on (and that must not be violated for either to work). (b) Suppose we wanted to extend MRW to three accumulated factors ($K$, $H$, and some 'social' capital $Z$ with exponent $\zeta$). Which method scales more gracefully? Why? (Two short paragraphs.)

Solve on paper first.

Anchor

The MRW Solow equations (per effective worker), with $D \equiv n+g+\delta+ng$:

$(1+n)(1+g)\,\Delta\tilde k_t = s_K\,\tilde k_t^{\alpha}\,\tilde h_t^{\phi} - D\,\tilde k_t,$
$(1+n)(1+g)\,\Delta\tilde h_t = s_H\,\tilde k_t^{\alpha}\,\tilde h_t^{\phi} - D\,\tilde h_t.$

A steady state is a positive pair $(\tilde k^*, \tilde h^*)$ at which both $\Delta\tilde k = 0$ and $\Delta\tilde h = 0$. The factor $(1+n)(1+g)$ does not vanish, so the SS condition is just $s_K \tilde k^{*\alpha}\tilde h^{*\phi} = D\tilde k^*$ and $s_H \tilde k^{*\alpha}\tilde h^{*\phi} = D\tilde h^*$.

Main proof — explicit solution

Step 1 Divide the $\tilde k$ equation by $\tilde k^*$ on both sides: $s_K\,\tilde k^{*\alpha-1}\,\tilde h^{*\phi} = D$, which rearranges to $\tilde k^{*1-\alpha} = (s_K/D)\,\tilde h^{*\phi}.$ (A)
Step 2 Same for $\tilde h$: $\tilde h^{*1-\phi} = (s_H/D)\,\tilde k^{*\alpha}.$ (B)
Step 3 From (B), solve for $\tilde h^*$: $\tilde h^* = (s_H/D)^{1/(1-\phi)}\,\tilde k^{*\alpha/(1-\phi)}$.
Step 4 Substitute into (A): $\tilde k^{*1-\alpha} = (s_K/D)\,(s_H/D)^{\phi/(1-\phi)}\,\tilde k^{*\alpha\phi/(1-\phi)}.$
Step 5 Move all $\tilde k^*$ terms to the left: $\tilde k^{*1-\alpha - \alpha\phi/(1-\phi)} = (s_K/D)\,(s_H/D)^{\phi/(1-\phi)}$.
Step 6 Simplify the exponent on $\tilde k^*$:
$(1-\alpha) - \alpha\phi/(1-\phi) = [(1-\alpha)(1-\phi) - \alpha\phi]/(1-\phi)$
$= [1 - \alpha - \phi + \alpha\phi - \alpha\phi]/(1-\phi) = (1-\alpha-\phi)/(1-\phi).$
Step 7 Raise both sides to the power $(1-\phi)/(1-\alpha-\phi)$: $\tilde k^* = (s_K/D)^{(1-\phi)/(1-\alpha-\phi)}\,(s_H/D)^{\phi/(1-\alpha-\phi)}$.
Step 8 Combine into a single fraction: $\tilde k^* = \bigl(s_K^{1-\phi}\,s_H^{\phi}/D\bigr)^{1/(1-\alpha-\phi)}.$
Step 9 By the symmetry of the system ($K \leftrightarrow H$, $\alpha \leftrightarrow \phi$, $s_K\leftrightarrow s_H$): $\tilde h^* = \bigl(s_K^{\alpha}\,s_H^{1-\alpha}/D\bigr)^{1/(1-\alpha-\phi)}.$
Step 10 For $\tilde y^* = \tilde k^{*\alpha}\tilde h^{*\phi}$, raise (Step 8) to $\alpha$ and (Step 9) to $\phi$, then multiply. The $s_K$ exponents: $\alpha(1-\phi)/(1-\alpha-\phi) + \phi\alpha/(1-\alpha-\phi) = \alpha[(1-\phi)+\phi]/(1-\alpha-\phi) = \alpha/(1-\alpha-\phi)$. Similarly $s_H$ exponent: $\phi/(1-\alpha-\phi)$. And $D$ exponent: $-(\alpha+\phi)/(1-\alpha-\phi)$.
Steady-state output (per effective worker):
$\tilde y^* = (s_K/D)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha-\phi)}\,(s_H/D)^{\phi/(1-\alpha-\phi)}.$

Elasticities

Step 11 Take logs: $\ln\tilde y^* = \dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha-\phi}(\ln s_K - \ln D) + \dfrac{\phi}{1-\alpha-\phi}(\ln s_H - \ln D)$.
Step 12 Read off coefficients: $\partial\ln\tilde y^*/\partial\ln s_K = \alpha/(1-\alpha-\phi)$, $\partial\ln\tilde y^*/\partial\ln s_H = \phi/(1-\alpha-\phi)$, $\partial\ln\tilde y^*/\partial\ln D = -(\alpha+\phi)/(1-\alpha-\phi)$ (combining the two $-\ln D$ terms).

At the baseline $\alpha = \phi = 1/3$: each saving-rate elasticity is $1$, and the $D$-elasticity is $-2$. These are the empirically-matched magnitudes that distinguish MRW from basic Solow.

Method variation — implicit differentiation

Step 1 Take logs of the two steady-state conditions:
$\ln s_K + \alpha\ln\tilde k^* + \phi\ln\tilde h^* = \ln D + \ln\tilde k^*\quad\Rightarrow\quad (\alpha-1)\ln\tilde k^* + \phi\ln\tilde h^* = \ln D - \ln s_K. \quad (I)$
$\ln s_H + \alpha\ln\tilde k^* + \phi\ln\tilde h^* = \ln D + \ln\tilde h^*\quad\Rightarrow\quad \alpha\ln\tilde k^* + (\phi-1)\ln\tilde h^* = \ln D - \ln s_H. \quad (II)$
Step 2 This is a 2×2 linear system in $(\ln\tilde k^*, \ln\tilde h^*)$ with matrix $M = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha-1 & \phi\\\alpha & \phi-1\end{pmatrix}$. Determinant: $\det M = (\alpha-1)(\phi-1) - \phi\alpha = \alpha\phi - \alpha - \phi + 1 - \alpha\phi = 1-\alpha-\phi$.
Step 3 Differentiate (I) and (II) with respect to $\ln s_K$ (holding $\ln s_H, \ln D$ fixed):
$(\alpha-1)\,\partial\ln\tilde k^*/\partial\ln s_K + \phi\,\partial\ln\tilde h^*/\partial\ln s_K = -1\quad (I')$
$\alpha\,\partial\ln\tilde k^*/\partial\ln s_K + (\phi-1)\,\partial\ln\tilde h^*/\partial\ln s_K = 0.\quad (II')$
Step 4 Solve. From (II'): $\partial\ln\tilde h^*/\partial\ln s_K = -\alpha/(\phi-1)\cdot\partial\ln\tilde k^*/\partial\ln s_K = \alpha/(1-\phi)\cdot\partial\ln\tilde k^*/\partial\ln s_K$.
Step 5 Substitute into (I'): $\partial\ln\tilde k^*/\partial\ln s_K\cdot\bigl[(\alpha-1) + \phi\alpha/(1-\phi)\bigr] = -1$. Simplify the bracket: $[(\alpha-1)(1-\phi) + \phi\alpha]/(1-\phi) = [\alpha - \alpha\phi - 1 + \phi + \phi\alpha]/(1-\phi) = [\alpha + \phi - 1]/(1-\phi) = -(1-\alpha-\phi)/(1-\phi)$.
Step 6 Hence $\partial\ln\tilde k^*/\partial\ln s_K\cdot[-(1-\alpha-\phi)/(1-\phi)] = -1$, giving $\partial\ln\tilde k^*/\partial\ln s_K = (1-\phi)/(1-\alpha-\phi)$.
Step 7 And $\partial\ln\tilde h^*/\partial\ln s_K = \alpha/(1-\phi)\cdot(1-\phi)/(1-\alpha-\phi) = \alpha/(1-\alpha-\phi).$
Step 8 Combine via $\ln\tilde y^* = \alpha\ln\tilde k^* + \phi\ln\tilde h^*$: $\partial\ln\tilde y^*/\partial\ln s_K = \alpha\cdot(1-\phi)/(1-\alpha-\phi) + \phi\cdot\alpha/(1-\alpha-\phi) = \alpha[(1-\phi)+\phi]/(1-\alpha-\phi) = \alpha/(1-\alpha-\phi).$
Same elasticity, second route: $\partial\ln\tilde y^*/\partial\ln s_K = \alpha/(1-\alpha-\phi)$, matching Step 12 of the explicit method.

Methodology reflection

(a) Load-bearing condition. Both methods rely on $1-\alpha-\phi > 0$. In the explicit method this is the exponent in every formula — if it equalled zero, the formulas would have undefined exponents ($1/0$). In the implicit method, this is exactly the determinant $\det M = 1-\alpha-\phi$ — if it equalled zero, the linear system would be singular and the steady state would not be locally unique. Both methods crash for the same economic reason: with $\alpha+\phi = 1$, the accumulated factors jointly exhibit constant returns to scale and the model loses its pull toward a finite steady state (it becomes a candidate endogenous-growth model, Module 4 territory).

(b) Scaling to three accumulated factors. The explicit method now requires solving a system of three coupled nonlinear equations, eliminating two variables in turn — feasible but ugly, with exponents proliferating. The implicit method generalises immediately: the linear system becomes 3×3, the matrix gets one extra row and column, and the determinant becomes $1-\alpha-\phi-\zeta$. Implicit differentiation is therefore the cleaner approach for $n$-factor generalisations — you can recover comparative-statics elasticities without ever having a closed-form expression for the SS values themselves.

Concrete preview of the 3-factor case

To make this concrete, suppose we add a third accumulated factor $Z$ — call it 'social capital' — with exponent $\zeta$, accumulation rate $s_Z$, and the same depreciation $\delta$. The aggregate production is $Y = K^\alpha H^\phi Z^\zeta (AL)^{1-\alpha-\phi-\zeta}$, and three Solow equations replace the two we worked with above. Taking logs of the three steady-state conditions gives a $3\times 3$ linear system in $(\ln\tilde k^*, \ln\tilde h^*, \ln\tilde z^*)$ with coefficient matrix
$M = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha-1 & \phi & \zeta \\ \alpha & \phi-1 & \zeta \\ \alpha & \phi & \zeta-1\end{pmatrix}$.
By the same algebra as the $2\times 2$ case (subtract any two rows to spot structure), one can show $\det M = -(1-\alpha-\phi-\zeta)$, so the condition for a unique steady state is $\alpha+\phi+\zeta < 1$ — the same content as before, naturally generalised. The elasticity of $\tilde y^*$ with respect to $s_i$ then turns out to be exponent$_i/(1-\alpha-\phi-\zeta)$ for $i\in\{K, H, Z\}$, again the natural generalisation. The explicit-solution method would require chasing through three nested substitutions; implicit differentiation handles the case in essentially the same effort as the $2\times 2$ case. This scaling advantage is what makes implicit-differentiation the method-of-choice once you accept that you don't actually need explicit formulas for $\tilde k^*$ etc. — only their elasticities.

Exam connection and common pitfalls

On a Cozzi-style exam this material can appear in three guises: 'derive the steady-state values', 'derive the elasticities', or 'derive the convergence equation' (the last is Exercise 4's domain, but uses Exercise 2's algebra). For all three, the load-bearing step is the simplification $(1-\alpha) - \alpha\phi/(1-\phi) = (1-\alpha-\phi)/(1-\phi)$ from Step 6 — most arithmetic mistakes happen here. A useful sanity check: in the limit $\phi\to 0$, the formula $\tilde y^* = (s_K/D)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha-\phi)}(s_H/D)^{\phi/(1-\alpha-\phi)}$ should reduce to the basic-Solow expression $\tilde y^* = (s/D)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$. Verify: the $s_K$ exponent goes to $\alpha/(1-\alpha)$ ✓; the $s_H$ exponent goes to $0$ ✓. Whenever your derivation passes this limit check, you've got the right answer.

Common pitfalls. Pitfall 1: trying to write down the answer from memory rather than deriving from the SS equations. The exponents look daunting, but they fall out automatically if you follow the algebra. Skipping steps leads to swapped exponents or missing $(1-\phi)$ factors. Pitfall 2: confusing $\tilde y^*$ with $y^*$. Recall $y^* = A_t \tilde y^*$, so $y^*$ GROWS over time at rate $g$. The constant quantity is $\tilde y^*$, and the elasticities you derive refer to it. On an essay, be explicit: 'in steady state, $y_t^* = A_t\tilde y^*$ grows at rate $g$, while the per-effective-worker quantity $\tilde y^*$ is constant and given by [formula].' Pitfall 3: using implicit differentiation but forgetting that you also need to verify the system is non-singular (i.e. $\det M \neq 0$). The condition $1-\alpha-\phi > 0$ is BOTH the existence condition for the steady state and the invertibility condition for the comparative-statics system — they're the same condition for the same economic reason.

Exercise 3MRW Golden Rule: derive, then break the symmetry
Scope: Module 3 Lecture 3a §4.3 (Golden Rule)
Anchor

Write the steady-state consumption per worker $c_t^*$ as a function of the saving rates $s_K, s_H$ and the steady-state output $y_t^* = A_t\,\tilde y^*$. State the optimisation problem (what is being maximised, what are the choice variables, what is held fixed) in one sentence.

Hint 1. In steady state, consumption per worker is $c_t^* = (1-s_K-s_H)\,y_t^*$ where $y_t^* = A_t\,\tilde y^*$.
Hint 2. We choose $s_K, s_H$ to maximise $\ln c_t^*$ (or equivalently $c_t^*$). The structural parameters $\alpha, \phi, n, g, \delta$ are held fixed. The $\ln A_t$ piece is irrelevant — it doesn't depend on the choice variables.

Anchor

Main proof

Step 1 Restate the steady-state output from Exercise 2: $\tilde y^* = (s_K/D)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha-\phi)}\,(s_H/D)^{\phi/(1-\alpha-\phi)}$ where $D = n+g+\delta+ng$.
Step 2 Take logs of $c_t^*$: $\ln c_t^* = \ln(1-s_K-s_H) + \ln A_t + \dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha-\phi}(\ln s_K - \ln D) + \dfrac{\phi}{1-\alpha-\phi}(\ln s_H - \ln D)$.
Step 3 The terms involving $\ln A_t$ and $\ln D$ are constants with respect to $s_K, s_H$. So maximising $\ln c_t^*$ is equivalent to maximising
$F(s_K, s_H) = \ln(1-s_K-s_H) + \dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha-\phi}\,\ln s_K + \dfrac{\phi}{1-\alpha-\phi}\,\ln s_H$.
Step 4 $F$ is strictly concave (sum of concave functions), so the FOCs deliver the global max. FOC for $s_K$:
$\partial F/\partial s_K = -\dfrac{1}{1-s_K-s_H} + \dfrac{\alpha}{(1-\alpha-\phi)\,s_K} = 0.$
Step 5 Rearranging: $\dfrac{\alpha}{(1-\alpha-\phi)\,s_K} = \dfrac{1}{1-s_K-s_H}$, i.e. $\alpha\,(1-s_K-s_H) = (1-\alpha-\phi)\,s_K. \quad (I)$
Step 6 FOC for $s_H$ (by symmetry): $\phi\,(1-s_K-s_H) = (1-\alpha-\phi)\,s_H. \quad (II)$
Step 7 Divide (I) by (II): $\dfrac{\alpha}{\phi} = \dfrac{s_K}{s_H}$, so $s_H = \dfrac{\phi}{\alpha}\,s_K. \quad (★)$
Step 8 Substitute (★) into (I): $\alpha\,\bigl(1 - s_K - (\phi/\alpha)\,s_K\bigr) = (1-\alpha-\phi)\,s_K$, which expands to $\alpha - \alpha\,s_K - \phi\,s_K = (1-\alpha-\phi)\,s_K$.
Step 9 Move all $s_K$ terms to the right: $\alpha = (1-\alpha-\phi)\,s_K + \alpha\,s_K + \phi\,s_K = s_K\,[(1-\alpha-\phi) + (\alpha+\phi)] = s_K \cdot 1 = s_K$.
Step 10 So $s_K^{**} = \alpha$. And from (★): $s_H^{**} = (\phi/\alpha)\cdot\alpha = \phi$.

The result is striking in its simplicity: invest in each factor in proportion to that factor's importance in production. At the baseline $\alpha = \phi = 1/3$, the Golden Rule prescribes saving $1/3$ of GDP for physical capital, $1/3$ for human capital, and consuming the remaining $1/3$. The total Golden-Rule saving rate is $\alpha + \phi$ — which equals the income share of the accumulated factors (capital + human capital), exactly mirroring the basic-Solow result $s^{**} = \alpha$ where the single Golden-Rule saving rate equals the share of the single accumulated factor.

Verification: the maximum is interior, not a corner

Two checks. First, the FOCs above implicitly assume $1-s_K-s_H > 0$ (consumption is strictly positive); if this were zero, the $\ln(1-s_K-s_H)$ term would diverge and we'd be at a corner. The FOCs themselves rule that out, because the LHS of (I) and (II) involve $1-s_K-s_H$ in the denominator. Second, the Hessian of $F$ is negative-definite: each of $\ln(1-s_K-s_H), \ln s_K, \ln s_H$ is concave, so their non-negative linear combination is concave. The unique stationary point we found is therefore a global maximum, not a saddle. So $(s_K^{**}, s_H^{**}) = (\alpha, \phi)$ is genuinely the Golden Rule.

Intuition for why the result is so clean

The cleanness — saving rate equals factor exponent, exactly — has an economic explanation. In steady state, the marginal benefit of one more unit invested in $K$ (in terms of long-run consumption) just equals the marginal cost (one unit of consumption foregone now). The marginal benefit operates through the chain $s_K \to \tilde k^* \to \tilde y^* \to c^*$, which is exactly the elasticity $\alpha/(1-\alpha-\phi)$ we computed in Exercise 2. The marginal cost is just unity per unit. Setting marginal benefit equal to marginal cost gives a condition where the consumption-share $(1-s_K-s_H)$ and the investment-share $s_K$ enter on equal footing in a way that makes $s_K = \alpha$ pop out exactly. The result generalises to any number of accumulated factors: $s_i^{**} = $ (Cobb-Douglas exponent of factor $i$).

The asymmetry — more human-capital investment than physical — reflects that in this 'knowledge economy', human capital contributes more to output per unit, so it's worth allocating more of GDP to its accumulation.

(b) The fraction consumed is $1 - s_K^{**} - s_H^{**} = 0.30$, i.e. 30%.

(c) Empirically, Western economies have $s_K + s_H \approx 0.35$, while MRW with $\alpha = \phi = 1/3$ would prescribe $s_K + s_H = 2/3 \approx 0.67$. Real economies save FAR below the Golden Rule. The model thus provides a strong theoretical case for public subsidies to education that push $s_H$ closer to $\phi$. Of course, the simple consumption-maximising calculus ignores the costs of getting there: raising $s_H$ requires either taxing current consumption (which causes a transitional consumption drop before steady-state consumption rises) or finding new revenue sources. But the long-run welfare ranking is unambiguous: MORE human-capital investment, until $s_H \approx \phi$.

Exam connection and common pitfalls

The Cozzi mock exam Q3 asks for the Cozzi (2017) hybrid model and its policy implications — a Module 5 question. If you see it, structure your answer the way this exercise does: derive the FOCs cleanly (3-4 lines), simplify to $s_K^{**} = \alpha, s_H^{**} = \phi$, then spend the bulk of the writing on what the result means — particularly the implication that real economies are under-saving in HUMAN capital by a factor of 2-3x relative to the GR prescription, which is exactly the quantitative case for education-subsidy policy. Why don't real-world governments push closer to the Golden Rule? Three reasons. First, the transition cost: raising $s_K$ or $s_H$ requires reducing current consumption — and the present generation bears the cost while future generations reap the benefit. In a political economy with finite horizons, this asymmetry biases policy below the Golden Rule. Second, the model assumes log utility from per-period consumption; with more general utility (especially with diminishing marginal utility), the optimal saving rates fall below the GR levels because consumption smoothing matters. Third, the model treats the savings choice as a single representative household; in reality, distributional concerns — who bears the cost of higher saving, who reaps the future return — complicate the welfare calculation. The Golden Rule is best read as a long-run consumption upper bound, not as a normative prescription. Anchor

The basic-Solow transition equation in per-effective-worker form is $\tilde k_{t+1} = \Phi(\tilde k_t) \equiv (s\,\tilde k_t^{\alpha} + (1-\delta)\,\tilde k_t)/((1+n)(1+g))$. Main proof

Step 1 Differentiate $\Phi$: $\Phi'(\tilde k) = (\alpha\,s\,\tilde k^{\alpha-1} + (1-\delta))/((1+n)(1+g))$.
Step 2 Evaluate at $\tilde k^*$. The steady-state condition $s\,\tilde k^{*\alpha} = (n+g+\delta+ng)\,\tilde k^*$ (which is $\Phi(\tilde k^*) = \tilde k^*$ after clearing the denominator $(1+n)(1+g)$) gives $s\,\tilde k^{*\alpha-1} = n+g+\delta+ng$.
Step 3 Substitute into Step 1: $\Phi'(\tilde k^*) = (\alpha\,(n+g+\delta+ng) + (1-\delta))/((1+n)(1+g))$.
Step 4 Now relate $\Phi'(\tilde k^*)$ to $1-\lambda$. The log-linearisation around $\tilde k^*$ gives $\ln\tilde k_{t+1} - \ln\tilde k^* \approx \Phi'(\tilde k^*)\cdot(\tilde k^*/\tilde k^*)\cdot(\ln\tilde k_t - \ln\tilde k^*) = \Phi'(\tilde k^*)\cdot(\ln\tilde k_t - \ln\tilde k^*)$ (the slope of $\Phi$ in log-log coordinates at a fixed point equals the linear-coordinate slope when $\Phi(\tilde k^*) = \tilde k^*$). Step 5 Simplify the numerator: $\alpha\,(n+g+\delta+ng) + (1-\delta)$. Add and subtract $(n+g+ng)$: $= \alpha(n+g+\delta+ng) + 1 - \delta = \alpha(n+g+\delta+ng) + 1 + (n+g+ng) - (n+g+\delta+ng) = \alpha(n+g+\delta+ng) + (1+n)(1+g) - (n+g+\delta+ng)$.
Step 6 Group: $= (1+n)(1+g) + (\alpha - 1)(n+g+\delta+ng) = (1+n)(1+g) - (1-\alpha)(n+g+\delta+ng)$.
Step 7 Therefore $\Phi'(\tilde k^*) = 1 - (1-\alpha)(n+g+\delta+ng)/((1+n)(1+g))$, giving
$\lambda = (1-\alpha)(n+g+\delta+ng)/((1+n)(1+g))$.
Step 8 For small $n, g$: $(1+n)(1+g) \approx 1$ and $ng \approx 0$, so $\lambda \approx (1-\alpha)(n+g+\delta).$

Cross-model bridge: MRW analog

The MRW system is two-dimensional in $(\tilde k, \tilde h)$. Linearising around $(\tilde k^*, \tilde h^*)$ in log-deviations $\hat k = \ln\tilde k - \ln\tilde k^*$, $\hat h = \ln\tilde h - \ln\tilde h^*$ produces two coupled equations:
$\hat k_{t+1} = a_{11}\hat k_t + a_{12}\hat h_t,\qquad \hat h_{t+1} = a_{21}\hat k_t + a_{22}\hat h_t,$
where the $a_{ij}$ are derivatives of the right-hand sides evaluated at the steady state — analogous to $\Phi'(\tilde k^*)$ but now a $2\times 2$ Jacobian.

The clever move (due to Mankiw–Romer–Weil) is to multiply the first equation by $\alpha$ and the second by $\phi$, then add. The trick exploits the identity $\ln\tilde y - \ln\tilde y^* = \alpha\hat k + \phi\hat h$ (which follows from $\tilde y = \tilde k^\alpha\tilde h^\phi$): the left-hand side of the summed equation is exactly $(\ln\tilde y_{t+1} - \ln\tilde y^*)$. After plugging in the $a_{ij}$ and simplifying, the right-hand side turns out to be a multiple of $(\alpha\hat k_t + \phi\hat h_t) = (\ln\tilde y_t - \ln\tilde y^*)$ — the same combination. The 2D system collapses to a 1D equation in $\ln\tilde y - \ln\tilde y^*$ alone.

The coefficient that emerges is the same structure as basic Solow's $\Phi'(\tilde k^*)$ but with $\alpha+\phi$ replacing $\alpha$: $1 - \lambda_{\text{MRW}} = 1 - (1-\alpha-\phi)(n+g+\delta+ng)/((1+n)(1+g))$, hence $\lambda_{\text{MRW}} \approx (1-\alpha-\phi)(n+g+\delta)$. At $\alpha = \phi = 1/3$ this gives $\lambda_{\text{MRW}} \approx (1/3)(0.075) = 0.025$ — half the basic-Solow value, matching the empirical 1-2% range much better.

Counterfactual: half-lives compared

  • Basic Solow, $\alpha = 1/3$: $\lambda = (2/3)(0.075) = 0.050$, so $T_{1/2} \approx 14$ years.
  • Basic Solow, $\alpha = 2/3$: $\lambda = (1/3)(0.075) = 0.025$, so $T_{1/2} \approx 28$ years.

Cases 2 and 3 give the EXACT SAME convergence rate and half-life. The general lesson: what determines convergence speed is $1 - (\text{sum of exponents on accumulated factors})$. In basic Solow with one accumulated factor of exponent $\alpha$, that's $1-\alpha$; in MRW with two accumulated factors of total exponent $\alpha+\phi$, that's $1-\alpha-\phi$. The mathematical structure is the same, only the bookkeeping changes.

So why prefer MRW (case 3) over basic Solow with $\alpha = 2/3$ (case 2)? Because case 2 is empirically untenable. The capital share of national income is observed to be about $1/3$ in NIPA data — that's a hard empirical constraint. Case 2 would require capital owners to earn $2/3$ of GDP, contradicting the observation. MRW resolves this by recognising that the 'extra' $\phi$ exponent accrues to workers as the return on their human capital — so observed labour share equals $1-\alpha \approx 2/3$ even with $\alpha+\phi = 2/3$ in the production function. MRW gets the right convergence speed AND the right income shares simultaneously; basic Solow with $\alpha = 2/3$ gets the speed but breaks the shares. That's why MRW is the canonical fix.

The empirical scoreboard

Putting numbers on the comparison reinforces the structural point. Cross-country growth regressions yield $\hat\lambda \approx 0.011$ — about 1.1% per year — with a 95% confidence interval of roughly $[0.6\%, 1.8\%]$. Basic Solow with $\alpha = 1/3$ predicts $\lambda \approx 5\%$, lying well outside this CI. MRW with $\alpha = \phi = 1/3$ predicts $\lambda \approx 2.5\%$, which is just above the upper bound of the empirical CI — much closer, though not perfectly within. Pushing $\alpha + \phi$ slightly higher (towards 0.75, say) brings MRW exactly into the empirical range while keeping observed shares intact, since the labour share still equals $1-\alpha$. The match is the strongest piece of empirical support for MRW.

The half-life intuition

A useful interpretive check: half-life $T_{1/2}$ for log-deviations from $\tilde y^*$ is $\ln 2/\lambda$, but the half-life for output ITSELF is more like $-1/\lambda$ when you measure absolute gap closing. The point is that with $\lambda \approx 2.5\%$ (MRW), if a country starts 50% below its steady-state level, it takes about 28 years to close half the remaining gap — a generation. Real-world catch-up episodes (post-war Germany, Japan, South Korea) all unfolded over multiple decades, consistent with this rate. Basic Solow's $\lambda \approx 5\%$ would predict a 14-year half-life — which is faster than any real-world catch-up we observe outside extraordinary post-disaster reconstruction. MRW's slower convergence is empirically natural, not an unfortunate concession.

Solve on paper first.

Anchor

(i) Combined production: $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}\,(A_t L_t)^{\beta}\,X^{\kappa}\,E_t^{\varepsilon}$, where $X$ is the fixed stock of land and $E_t$ is the flow of oil used as energy input in period $t$.

(ii) Constant returns to scale across all four inputs (replication argument): $\alpha + \beta + \kappa + \varepsilon = 1$.

(iii) Extraction rule: $E_t = s_E\,R_t$, where $R_t$ is the remaining oil stock. Mass balance: $R_{t+1} = R_t - E_t = R_t - s_E R_t = (1-s_E)\,R_t$, so $R_t = (1-s_E)^t\,R_0$ — geometric depletion.

Main proof

Step 1 Per-worker production. Divide $Y_t$ by $L_t$:
$y_t = Y_t/L_t = K_t^{\alpha} A_t^{\beta} L_t^{\beta-1} X^{\kappa} E_t^{\varepsilon}$.
Now substitute $K_t = k_t L_t$ so $K_t^{\alpha} = k_t^{\alpha} L_t^{\alpha}$:
$y_t = k_t^{\alpha} L_t^{\alpha+\beta-1} A_t^{\beta} X^{\kappa} E_t^{\varepsilon}.$
Step 2 Use $\alpha + \beta + \kappa + \varepsilon = 1$ to get $\alpha + \beta - 1 = -(\kappa+\varepsilon)$, so $L_t^{\alpha+\beta-1} = L_t^{-\kappa-\varepsilon} = L_t^{-\kappa} L_t^{-\varepsilon}$. Distribute:
$y_t = k_t^{\alpha}\,A_t^{\beta}\,(X/L_t)^{\kappa}\,(E_t/L_t)^{\varepsilon}.$
Step 3 Take logs and time differences. With $g_z \equiv \ln z_t - \ln z_{t-1}$:
$g_y = \alpha\,g_k + \beta\,g_A + \kappa\,g_{X/L} + \varepsilon\,g_{E/L}.$
Substitute the known growth rates: $g_A = g$ (by definition); $g_X = 0$ (X fixed), so $g_{X/L} = -g_L = -n$; $E_t = s_E R_t$ with $R_t$ falling at rate $s_E$, so $g_E = g_R = -s_E$ (approximately, for small $s_E$). Thus $g_{E/L} = g_E - g_L = -s_E - n$.
Step 4 Substitute:
$g_y = \alpha\,g_k + \beta\,g - \kappa\,n - \varepsilon\,(s_E + n) = \alpha\,g_k + \beta\,g - (\kappa+\varepsilon)\,n - \varepsilon\,s_E.$
Step 5 Apply the balanced-growth condition $g_k = g_y$ (since $K/Y$ constant in steady state implies $g_K = g_Y$, hence $g_k = g_y$ after subtracting $g_L = n$ from both):
$g_y = \alpha\,g_y + \beta\,g - (\kappa+\varepsilon)\,n - \varepsilon\,s_E.$
Step 6 Solve: $(1-\alpha)\,g_y = \beta\,g - (\kappa+\varepsilon)\,n - \varepsilon\,s_E$. Use $1-\alpha = \beta+\kappa+\varepsilon$:
Combined balanced-growth rate:
$g_y = \dfrac{\beta\,g - (\kappa+\varepsilon)\,n - \varepsilon\,s_E}{\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon}.$

Three growth drags are visible. (a) Technology effect, $\beta/(\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon) < 1$ — labour-augmenting progress is less effective than in a no-resource world. (b) Population growth, $-(\kappa+\varepsilon)/(\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon)\,n$ — more workers press on both fixed land and depleting oil. (c) Extraction, $-\varepsilon/(\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon)\,s_E$ — the oil stock itself shrinks, distinct from the per-capita thinning in (b).

Numeric inversion

Step 1 Plug $g_y = 0.015$, $n = 0.01$, $s_E = 0.005$, $\alpha = 0.2$, $\beta = 0.6$, $\kappa = \varepsilon = 0.1$ into the formula. Denominator $\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon = 0.8$:
$0.015 = (0.6\,g - 0.2\cdot 0.01 - 0.1\cdot 0.005)/0.8.$
Step 2 Multiply both sides by 0.8: $0.015\cdot 0.8 = 0.012 = 0.6\,g - 0.002 - 0.0005 = 0.6\,g - 0.0025$.
Step 3 Solve: $0.6\,g = 0.0145$, so $g = 0.0145/0.6 \approx 0.0242$.
Implied $g$: approximately $2.4\%$ per year.

Is this plausible? Yes — empirical estimates of long-run labour-augmenting technological progress in Western economies (from growth-accounting decompositions) come out at around 2-3% per year. The model's implied $g$ falls squarely in this range. The result is reassuring: with Nordhaus's structural parameters, the observed $g_y \approx 1.5\%$ is consistent with an underlying $g \approx 2.4\%$ minus the $\approx 0.9$ percentage points of combined growth drag from population growth ($-0.25\%$) and oil extraction ($-0.0625\%$), suitably scaled — and the difference comes out about right.

Methodology reflection

(1) What goes wrong under Leontief. In Cobb-Douglas, as oil becomes scarce its marginal product blows up to infinity, prices adjust, and producers smoothly substitute capital and effective labour for the dwindling oil. Output continues to grow, just at a slightly diminished rate. Under Leontief production $Y = \min(K^\alpha\cdot\text{const}, AL\cdot\text{const}, E/\eta)$ for some technical coefficient $\eta$, output is bounded above by the SMALLEST factor — once $E$ falls below some threshold, $Y$ collapses, regardless of how much $K$ or $AL$ you accumulate. So $g_y$ would NOT be a smooth function of $s_E$ — it would be near-$g$ for a long time, then abruptly turn negative or zero when oil hit a constraint. The model's optimistic conclusion (sustainable growth despite resource scarcity) is therefore CONDITIONAL on substitutability staying high; under Leontief, classical pessimism is re-vindicated and the entire policy implication flips.

(2) Why cross-country data can't settle the question. The distinction between Cobb-Douglas and Leontief matters most at the LIMITS — when $E \to 0$. But no country in the dataset has actually exhausted a major resource; we observe substitution responses that are well-behaved across the range we've sampled. The interesting regime — what happens at near-exhaustion — is precisely the regime we have no data for. So econometric tests using cross-country variation in resource scarcity will tend to find behaviour consistent with smooth substitution, simply because they're sampling the easy region. To distinguish the two views, we'd need either (a) long-run historical episodes where a binding resource ran out (and the data is patchy), or (b) microeconomic engineering studies of substitution possibilities at specific industrial bottlenecks (water, lithium, etc.). Macro time series alone cannot resolve the fundamental Cobb-Douglas-vs-Leontief question — but the answer matters enormously for whether we should be growth-optimists or growth-pessimists about the deep future.

Historical episodes of substitution

History gives some encouragement to the Cobb-Douglas view. The 19th century saw coal substitute for wood and animal labour as the primary energy source; the 20th saw oil and natural gas substitute for coal in many uses; the 21st is in the early stages of substituting renewables (solar, wind) for hydrocarbons. Each transition was costly and disruptive, but production did not collapse — prices rose, alternatives were found, and growth continued. The classical economists' worry that production was inherently bounded by a fixed natural input has been refuted, at least so far, by repeated successful substitutions. Each generation discovered that what looked like a hard physical limit was, on closer inspection, a constraint that could be relaxed by sufficient capital investment in new technology.

But this historical record is not unambiguous evidence for unlimited substitutability. Each past substitution was between things that ARE substitutable — different forms of energy, different metals, different fibres. The question for the deep future is whether substitution at the ECOSYSTEM level (atmospheric CO₂ sinks, biodiversity, clean water aquifers) is also unlimited. Many natural scientists doubt this: a habitable atmosphere is not really substitutable by capital in any meaningful sense. So Cobb-Douglas may be a reasonable working assumption for the medium run (next century or two) but a dangerous extrapolation to the very long run.

What the model is and isn't claiming

It's worth being explicit about what the optimistic conclusion of Lecture 3b actually claims. The conclusion is: GIVEN that substitution is unlimited and GIVEN labour-augmenting technical progress at around 2-3% per year, modest population growth, and Nordhaus-calibrated factor shares, the model implies positive sustained growth of GDP per capita. None of these GIVENs is a sure bet. Cobb-Douglas overstates substitutability. The 2-3% technological growth rate is an empirical regularity, not a law of nature. Population growth in developing countries is much higher and undermines the result mathematically. The model can be used either way — to justify growth optimism if you accept the assumptions, or to make the assumptions vividly clear if you doubt them. The CORRECT use is the second one: the model tells you what would have to be true for sustainable growth, and lets you (and the data) judge whether reality matches.

Exam connection and common pitfalls

This material is the heart of Mock Exam Q2 ('Solow with land and natural resources, plus a sketch of numerical simulation'). On the technical half, the derivation above — production function → per-worker form → take logs and time differences → impose balanced growth → solve for $g_y$ — is exactly the chain Cozzi wants to see. Pitfalls: (1) forgetting that BOTH $g_X = 0$ AND $g_E = -s_E$ have to enter as growth rates of the per-capita ratios, with $-n$ adding to each — easy to lose a $-n$ somewhere. (2) Stating the formula without justifying $g_k = g_y$ in the balanced-growth step — this is the balanced-growth assumption that defines steady state, not a derived fact. (3) Using $1-\alpha = \beta + \kappa + \varepsilon$ silently without flagging — small thing, but exam graders like to see you noting which identity you're using when. On the interpretive half (substitution discussion), the right framing is: 'the model's growth-optimism conclusion is conditional on unlimited substitutability between accumulated and non-renewable factors; if substitution is bounded (e.g. Leontief), classical pessimism returns'. That two-sentence summary captures the key methodological caveat that distinguishes a careful answer from a naive one.
