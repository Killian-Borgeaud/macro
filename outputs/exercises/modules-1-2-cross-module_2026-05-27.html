Exercise 1Closed-economy $k^*$ vs open-economy $k^*$: the savings-rate disconnect
Scope: Module 1 Lecture 1c p10 (basic-Solow steady state); Module 2 Lecture 2a p15-16 (open-economy law of motion, immediate adjustment of $k$).
Anchor

From the basic Solow model (Lecture 1c p10), write down $k^*$ explicitly in terms of $s, B, \alpha, n, \delta$. Then, in one sentence, explain the economic mechanism by which $s$ raises $k^*$ — what does saving more actually do in the closed economy?

Hint 1. The defining condition is $k_{t+1} = k_t$, i.e., savings per worker $sBk^\alpha$ exactly cover the replacement investment $(n+\delta)k$.
Hint 2. Equate $sBk^\alpha = (n+\delta)k$ and solve for $k$.
Main proof

In the small open economy of Lecture 2a (with $\delta = 0$ for clarity), derive $$k^* = \left(\frac{\alpha B}{\bar{r}}\right)^{\!\!\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}$$ from the perfect-capital-mobility condition that the domestic marginal product of capital equals the world real interest rate $\bar{r}$. Show every algebraic step.

Then flag which parameters of the basic-Solow steady state do not appear in this expression, and explain why their absence is the central puzzle of opening the capital account.

Hint 1. Profit maximization of the representative firm gives $r_t = \alpha B k_t^{\alpha-1}$ (Lecture 2a p12). Under perfect capital mobility the no-arbitrage condition is $r_t = \bar{r}$ for every $t$, not just in the long run.
Hint 2. So solve $\bar{r} = \alpha B k^{\alpha-1}$ for $k$. This is the same equation that determines $r^*$ in the closed economy, but read in the opposite direction: in the closed economy $k^*$ is pinned down by savings dynamics and $r^*$ is the residual; in the open economy $\bar{r}$ is pinned down by the world market and $k^*$ is the residual.
Hint 3. After solving, ask: where are $s$ and $n$? Neither appears. The closed-economy steady state depended on $s/(n+\delta)$. The open-economy capital intensity depends on neither — it depends only on technology and the world rate.
Method variation

Derive the same expression for $k^*$ a second way, this time without invoking MPK-arbitrage directly. Use the wealth accumulation equation $V_{t+1} - V_t = S_t$ (Lecture 2a p11) together with $V_t = K_t + F_t$.

Show that perfect capital mobility decouples the dynamics of $K_t$ from the dynamics of $V_t$ — that is, $K_t$ adjusts in one period to whatever level the arbitrage condition demands, regardless of what $S_t$ has done. Explain which variable absorbs the gap, and at what cost.

Hint 1. The wealth equation $V_{t+1} - V_t = S_t$ does not control $K_t$ directly — it controls $V_t = K_t + F_t$. The decoupling happens through $F_t$.
Hint 2. Net foreign assets $F_t$ absorb the gap. If $V_t < K_t^*$ (national wealth hasn't yet accumulated to the level the world rate justifies), the country borrows from abroad and $F_t < 0$. If $V_t > K_t^*$, the surplus is invested abroad and $F_t > 0$. Either way the domestic stock $K_t$ jumps to its target in one period — but the country pays interest $\bar{r} F_t$ on the imbalance.
Methodology reflection

Each derivation hinges on one assumption that does not survive scrutiny in the real world. State that assumption precisely for each method, and identify which assumption is load-bearing for the savings-rate-independence result.

  1. The MPK-arbitrage derivation assumes the world interest rate $\bar{r}$ is exogenous and constant. What does this rule out empirically?
  2. The wealth-decoupling derivation assumes $F_t$ can take any value with no friction. What does that rule out empirically?
Counterfactual

Drop the small economy assumption: the domestic economy is now large enough that its capital demand affects the world rate. Write $\bar{r} = R(K_\text{world})$ where $K_\text{world}$ now includes domestic $K$.

Without re-doing the full derivation, argue which features of the open-economy result survive (instant $k$-adjustment? $s$-independence?) and which collapse. Two to three sentences is enough.

Solve on paper first.

Anchor

From $k_{t+1} = k_t$ the steady-state condition is

Step 1. $sBk^{*\alpha} = (n+\delta)k^*$ — gross savings per worker $sBk^\alpha$ exactly cover the replacement investment $(n+\delta)k$ required to keep $k$ constant despite a growing labour force ($n$) and depreciation ($\delta$).
Step 2. Divide both sides by $k^{*\alpha}$: $sB = (n+\delta)k^{*\,1-\alpha}$.
Step 3. Solve for $k^*$: $k^{*\,1-\alpha} = sB/(n+\delta)$, hence $k^* = \left[sB/(n+\delta)\right]^{1/(1-\alpha)} = B^{1/(1-\alpha)}\left(s/(n+\delta)\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)}$.
$k^*_\text{basic} = B^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}} \left(\dfrac{s}{n+\delta}\right)^{\!\!\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}.$

Mechanism: a higher $s$ means more output is plowed back into capital each period. With $n+\delta$ fixed, the only way for replacement investment $(n+\delta)k$ to absorb the extra savings flow $sBk^\alpha$ is for $k$ itself to be larger — the steady-state balance is restored at a higher capital intensity. The closed economy must build its capital out of its own savings; there is no other source. Conversely, the elasticity $\partial \ln k^*/\partial \ln s = 1/(1-\alpha) \approx 1.5$ for $\alpha = 1/3$ — a 10% increase in $s$ raises long-run $k^*$ by about 15%.

Main proof

The firm maximizes $\Pi_t = BK_t^\alpha L_t^{1-\alpha} - r_t K_t - w_t L_t$ taking $r_t, w_t$ as given. The first-order condition with respect to $K_t$ gives

Step 1. $\partial \Pi_t / \partial K_t = 0 \Rightarrow r_t = \alpha B K_t^{\alpha-1} L_t^{1-\alpha}$.
Step 2. Factor out $L_t$ to express in per-worker terms: $r_t = \alpha B (K_t/L_t)^{\alpha-1} L_t^{\alpha-1} L_t^{1-\alpha} = \alpha B k_t^{\alpha-1}$ (the $L$-powers cancel).

Under perfect capital mobility, capital flows in or out of the country until the domestic rental rate equals the world rate. This must hold every period, not just in steady state, because any wedge $r_t \neq \bar{r}$ would trigger an instantaneous arbitrage opportunity (borrow at the lower rate, lend at the higher rate, infinite size):

Step 3. No-arbitrage condition: $r_t = \bar{r}$ for all $t$.
Step 4. Substitute Step 2 into Step 3: $\bar{r} = \alpha B k_t^{\alpha-1}$.
Step 5. Solve for $k_t$. Divide both sides by $\alpha B$: $\bar{r}/(\alpha B) = k_t^{\alpha-1}$. Raise both sides to the power $1/(\alpha-1)$. Since $\alpha-1 < 0$, this flips the fraction inside: $k_t = \left[\bar{r}/(\alpha B)\right]^{1/(\alpha-1)} = \left[\alpha B/\bar{r}\right]^{1/(1-\alpha)}$.
Step 6. The right-hand side carries no $t$-subscript — it depends only on parameters $\alpha, B, \bar{r}$. Hence $k_t$ is the same constant in every period, so we can write $k_t = k^*$ for all $t$.
$k^*_\text{open} = \left(\dfrac{\alpha B}{\bar{r}}\right)^{\!\!\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}$ — independent of $s, n, \delta$.

Parameters that vanish: $s$, $n$, and (had we kept it) $\delta$. Their absence is the entire point of Chapter 4. In the closed economy, the savings rate was the primary lever a government could pull to raise long-run capital per worker — that's the whole argument behind the Golden Rule prescription of Lecture 1c p15 ("encourage savings"). In the open economy, the world rate $\bar{r}$ pins down $k^*$, and a domestic shift in $s$ has zero effect on the productive capital stock that operates inside the country. The reason this is a puzzle (and not just a trivial calculation): the policy advice for raising domestic productivity is now completely different. Trying to raise $k^*$ by raising $s$ is futile in the small open economy. What $s$ does control is national wealth $v^*$ — and through that, national income $y^{n*}$ — but not the capital that workers in the country actually use.

To preview: this disconnect between $k^*$ (capital used domestically) and $v^*$ (wealth owned by domestic residents) is the engine of every result in Lecture 2a. It produces the two-speed dynamics (Method variation below), the wage caveat for strong savers (Ex 4), and the breakdown of the Golden Rule (Ex 5).

Method variation

Start from the wealth accumulation equation under $\delta = 0$:

Step 1. $V_{t+1} - V_t = S_t = s Y_t^n$, where $Y_t^n = Y_t + \bar{r}F_t$ is GNP (Lecture 2a p11).

Divide both sides by $L_{t+1} = (1+n)L_t$ to put the equation in per-worker form:

Step 2. $V_{t+1}/L_{t+1} = (V_t + S_t)/L_{t+1} = (V_t + S_t)/[(1+n)L_t]$, hence $v_{t+1} = \frac{1}{1+n}(v_t + sy_t^n)$ (Lecture 2a p17).

Crucially, this equation governs $v_t$, not $k_t$. The accounting identity $v_t = k_t + f_t$ has one variable on the left and two on the right — it leaves one degree of freedom. Given $v_t$, we can split it any way we like between $k_t$ and $f_t$:

Step 3. Perfect capital mobility resolves the split. The arbitrage condition $\bar{r} = \alpha B k_t^{\alpha-1}$ pins $k_t = k^*$ in every period. Whatever wealth $v_t$ remains is held as net foreign assets: $f_t = v_t - k^*$.

So $K_t$ and $V_t$ are decoupled. The wealth dynamics determine $v_t$ gradually (a slow-moving state evolving via the savings flow), while $k_t$ jumps instantly to its target whenever a parameter shifts. This is the famous "two-speed dynamics" of the small-open-economy Solow model — Lecture 2a uses the labels "immediate adjustment" (for $k_t$, Lecture 2a p16) and "gradual adjustment" (for $v_t$, p17).

Worked example to fix intuition: suppose the country starts in autarky steady state with $v_0 = k_0^c$ where $k_0^c$ is the closed-economy $k^*$ given $s,n,B,\alpha$. Open the capital account in period 1. If $\bar{r} > r_c^*$ (world rate exceeds the country's autarky rate), then $k^*_\text{open} < k_0^c$: domestic capital is excessive and capital flows out, so $k$ drops instantly while $v$ remains at $v_0$. The gap is absorbed by $f_1 = v_0 - k^*_\text{open} > 0$ — the country becomes a net creditor on impact. Going forward, $v_t$ evolves slowly under the new transition equation, but $k_t$ stays parked at $k^*_\text{open}$ forever.

The cost of the immediate $k$-adjustment is exactly the interest flow $\bar{r}f_t$ (positive if creditor, negative if debtor). Output $y^* = B k^{*\alpha}$ stays at the world-arbitrage level, but national income $y^{n*} = y^* + \bar{r}f^*$ can be either higher or lower than $y^*$, depending on the sign of $f^*$.

$k^* = \left(\dfrac{\alpha B}{\bar{r}}\right)^{\!\!\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}$ — same answer, but now visibly the result of $f_t$ absorbing the gap between actual wealth and the world-arbitrage capital stock.

Methodology reflection

(1) The MPK-arbitrage derivation assumes $\bar{r}$ is exogenous. Empirically this is the "small economy" assumption: the country's capital demand is too small to move the global capital market. It rules out the U.S., China, the eurozone as a bloc, and any other case where the country's saving or investment behaviour materially shifts world interest rates. For Switzerland, Denmark, Singapore, it's a defensible approximation; for "the OECD as a whole" it is not. The conceptual cost of relaxing it: $\bar{r}$ becomes endogenous to the country's $s$ via global market clearing, and the savings-rate-independence result evaporates (see the Counterfactual).

(2) The wealth-decoupling derivation assumes $F_t$ can take any value with no friction. This rules out capital controls (in force across most of the developing world until the 1990s), illiquidity in international debt markets, sovereign-risk premia that increase nonlinearly with $F_t/Y_t$, and the various forms of "home bias" in portfolio allocation that empirical finance has been documenting since French and Poterba (1991). Feldstein and Horioka's classic 1980 finding (and Lecture 2a p6) — that the OLS slope $\alpha_1$ in $(I/Y) = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 (S/Y)$ is empirically $\approx 0.89$ for 1960-74 OECD — is the standard empirical pushback: even rich-country savings are largely retained at home, suggesting $F$-adjustment is far from frictionless.

Load-bearing assumption for savings-rate independence: the first one. If $\bar{r}$ adjusts endogenously to global savings, then a domestic rise in $s$ feeds into the world capital supply, lowers $\bar{r}$ globally, and raises $k^*$ in every country including the original one — and the closed-economy elasticity of $k^*$ with respect to $s$ partially reappears. Capital mobility per se (the second assumption) is enough to deliver two-speed dynamics: $k$ jumps, $v$ crawls, $f$ absorbs the gap. But it is not enough to deliver savings-rate independence; that requires the marginal-buyer rate to be set somewhere outside the country's own savings supply.

Counterfactual

Drop the "small" assumption: now $\bar{r} = R(K_\text{world})$ where $K_\text{world}$ includes domestic $K$, and $R$ is a decreasing function (more global capital, lower MPK at the world level). What survives, what breaks?

Survives: instant $k$-adjustment. The arbitrage logic still applies in every period: capital flows wherever MPK is highest until rates are equalized. As long as capital can physically move within a period, $k_t$ still hits whatever target the (now endogenous) $\bar{r}$ demands. The fast/slow distinction between $k$ and $v$ is intact.

Breaks: savings-rate independence. A domestic shift in $s$ raises domestic $V$, which raises domestic $f$ (since $k$ is pinned by the rate), which contributes to $K_\text{world}$, which lowers $\bar{r}$, which raises $k^*$ in every country including the original. The country is now effectively a partial closed economy with respect to its own savings rate, with the closed-economy elasticity rescaled by the country's share of world capital. In the limit where the country is the world, you recover the closed-economy result exactly: $\partial k^*/\partial s$ converges to the closed-economy elasticity $k^*/[(1-\alpha)s]$.

Exercise 2Effective-labour units and the BGP: deriving $\tilde{k}^*$ from the ground up
Scope: Module 2 Lecture 2b p7-13 (effective-labour reformulation, transition equation, steady state); Module 1 Lecture 1c p10 (basic-Solow steady state, anchor contrast).
Anchor

In the basic Solow model (no technological progress), $y$ converges to a constant $y^*$ and then sits there forever. In one sentence, state why this contradicts stylized fact #5 (Western per-worker GDP grows at $\approx 2\%$ per year sustainedly). In one more sentence, state what minimal change the general Solow model makes to fix it.

Main proof

Starting from $Y_t = K_t^\alpha (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}$ with $A_{t+1} = (1+g)A_t$ and $L_{t+1} = (1+n)L_t$, derive the transition equation for $\tilde{k}_t \equiv K_t/(A_t L_t)$. From it, solve in closed form for the steady state $\tilde{k}^*$.

Show every algebraic step: the division step that introduces $(1+g)(1+n)$ on the denominator, the appearance of the drag term $(n+g+\delta+ng)$, the steady-state condition, and the final inversion.

Hint 1. Start from the standard capital accumulation equation $K_{t+1} = sY_t + (1-\delta)K_t$. Divide both sides by $A_{t+1}L_{t+1} = (1+g)(1+n) A_t L_t$.
Hint 2. On the right, use $Y_t/(A_tL_t) = \tilde{k}_t^\alpha = \tilde{y}_t$ and $K_t/(A_tL_t) = \tilde{k}_t$. The right-hand side becomes $[s\tilde{k}_t^\alpha + (1-\delta)\tilde{k}_t]\,/\,[(1+g)(1+n)]$.
Hint 3. Subtract $\tilde{k}_t$ from both sides to get the Solow equation. The algebraic identity $1 - (1+g)(1+n) = -(n+g+ng)$ combines with $-\delta$ to produce the drag $(n+g+\delta+ng)\tilde{k}_t$. Then impose $\tilde{k}_{t+1} = \tilde{k}_t = \tilde{k}^*$ and solve.
Method variation

Derive $\tilde{k}^*$ a second way: assume from the outset that a balanced growth path exists. Use the Kaldor fact that $K/Y$ is constant along the BGP (the capital-output ratio doesn't trend) together with the production function in level form to derive a relationship for $\tilde{k}^*$. Then close the system using the steady-state savings-investment identity.

Confirm you get the same closed-form expression.

Hint 1. $K/Y$ constant means $K_t/Y_t = \tilde{k}_t/\tilde{y}_t = \tilde{k}_t^{1-\alpha}$ is constant. So $\tilde{k}_t$ is pinned to a constant — call it $\tilde{k}^*$ — on the BGP.
Hint 2. The savings-investment identity in steady state: gross savings $sY_t$ must equal the gross investment needed to keep $K$ growing at its BGP rate $n+g$ (plus replacement $\delta K$). Per unit of effective labour: $s\tilde{y}^* = (n+g+\delta+ng)\tilde{k}^*$. Solve.
Methodology reflection

The first derivation manipulates the law of motion and discovers the BGP as the fixed point. The second imposes the BGP from the start and reads off the level. Each treats $\tilde{k}^*$ as the answer to a different question.

State precisely what question each method answers, and identify which method gives you the additional information that the BGP is unique and that the system converges to it from arbitrary initial conditions. Two or three sentences per method.

Solve on paper first.

Anchor

Stylized fact #5 (Lecture 1a, Ch 2): per-worker GDP in the Western world grows at roughly $2\%$ per year over long horizons with no sign of slowdown. The basic Solow model predicts $\dot{y} = 0$ in steady state — incompatible.

The minimal fix: introduce an exogenously growing labour-augmenting productivity $A_t$ with $A_{t+1} = (1+g)A_t$, so the production function becomes $Y_t = K_t^\alpha(A_tL_t)^{1-\alpha}$. Long-run per-worker output then grows at the rate $g$, by construction. Everything else about the Solow model stays the same — same savings rate, same depreciation, same competitive markets. The general Solow model is "basic Solow + one exogenous trend in $A$," nothing more.

Main proof

Reformulating the model in effective-labour units $\tilde{k}_t = K_t/(A_tL_t)$ is the standard trick that makes the system stationary. Here is why it works and how the algebra unfolds:

Step 1. Capital accumulation: $K_{t+1} = sY_t + (1-\delta)K_t$ (standard, unchanged from basic Solow).
Step 2. Divide both sides by $A_{t+1}L_{t+1}$. The left side becomes $K_{t+1}/(A_{t+1}L_{t+1}) = \tilde{k}_{t+1}$ by definition.
Step 3. Use $A_{t+1}L_{t+1} = (1+g)A_t \cdot (1+n)L_t = (1+g)(1+n) A_tL_t$. The right side becomes $\dfrac{sY_t + (1-\delta)K_t}{(1+g)(1+n)A_tL_t}$.
Step 4. Distribute the denominator over the two terms in the numerator: $\dfrac{sY_t}{(1+g)(1+n)A_tL_t} + \dfrac{(1-\delta)K_t}{(1+g)(1+n)A_tL_t}$. Use $Y_t/(A_tL_t) = \tilde{y}_t$ and $K_t/(A_tL_t) = \tilde{k}_t$.
Step 5. Result: $\tilde{k}_{t+1} = \dfrac{s\tilde{y}_t + (1-\delta)\tilde{k}_t}{(1+g)(1+n)}$.
Step 6. From the production function $Y_t = K_t^\alpha (A_tL_t)^{1-\alpha}$, divide both sides by $A_tL_t$: $\tilde{y}_t = (K_t/A_tL_t)^\alpha = \tilde{k}_t^\alpha$.
Step 7. Substitute $\tilde{y}_t = \tilde{k}_t^\alpha$ into Step 5: $\tilde{k}_{t+1} = \dfrac{s\tilde{k}_t^\alpha + (1-\delta)\tilde{k}_t}{(1+g)(1+n)}$. This is the transition equation in effective-labour units (Lecture 2b p10).

To get the Solow equation, subtract $\tilde{k}_t$ from both sides:

Step 8. $\tilde{k}_{t+1} - \tilde{k}_t = \dfrac{s\tilde{k}_t^\alpha + (1-\delta)\tilde{k}_t}{(1+g)(1+n)} - \tilde{k}_t = \dfrac{s\tilde{k}_t^\alpha + (1-\delta)\tilde{k}_t - (1+g)(1+n)\tilde{k}_t}{(1+g)(1+n)}$.
Step 9. Combine the $\tilde{k}_t$-terms in the numerator: $(1-\delta) - (1+g)(1+n) = 1 - \delta - (1 + n + g + ng) = -(n+g+\delta+ng)$. So
Step 10. $\tilde{k}_{t+1} - \tilde{k}_t = \dfrac{1}{(1+g)(1+n)}\left[s\tilde{k}_t^\alpha - (n+g+\delta+ng)\tilde{k}_t\right]$ — the Solow equation in effective-labour units (Lecture 2b screenshot of p13).

Now impose the steady-state condition $\tilde{k}_{t+1} = \tilde{k}_t = \tilde{k}^*$, which sets the LHS to zero:

Step 11. $0 = s\tilde{k}^{*\alpha} - (n+g+\delta+ng)\tilde{k}^*$, hence $s\tilde{k}^{*\alpha} = (n+g+\delta+ng)\tilde{k}^*$.
Step 12. Divide both sides by $\tilde{k}^{*\alpha}$: $s = (n+g+\delta+ng)\,\tilde{k}^{*\,1-\alpha}$.
Step 13. Rearrange: $\tilde{k}^{*\,1-\alpha} = s/(n+g+\delta+ng)$. Raise both sides to $1/(1-\alpha)$.
$\tilde{k}^* = \left(\dfrac{s}{n+g+\delta+ng}\right)^{\!\!\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}, \qquad \tilde{y}^* = \tilde{k}^{*\alpha} = \left(\dfrac{s}{n+g+\delta+ng}\right)^{\!\!\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}.$

Note the structural parallel to the basic-Solow expression: replace $(n+\delta)$ with $(n+g+\delta+ng)$ and you map one to the other. The drag is bigger by the contribution of $g$ (and the cross term $ng$, which is empirically tiny: $0.01 \times 0.02 = 0.0002$ vs the other drags on the order of $0.01-0.05$). For typical calibration $n=0.01$, $g=0.02$, $\delta=0.05$: the basic-Solow drag is $0.06$, the general-Solow drag is $0.0802$ — about 33% bigger, lowering $\tilde{k}^*$ relative to the basic-Solow analogue.

Method variation

Assume a BGP exists. The Kaldor stylized fact (constant $K/Y$) becomes the entry point:

Step 1. $K_t/Y_t = \dfrac{K_t/(A_tL_t)}{Y_t/(A_tL_t)} = \dfrac{\tilde{k}_t}{\tilde{y}_t} = \dfrac{\tilde{k}_t}{\tilde{k}_t^\alpha} = \tilde{k}_t^{1-\alpha}$.
Step 2. For $K_t/Y_t$ to be constant, $\tilde{k}_t^{1-\alpha}$ must be constant. Since $1-\alpha > 0$, this forces $\tilde{k}_t$ itself to be constant. Call its value $\tilde{k}^*$.
Step 3. If $\tilde{k}_t$ is constant in time, then $K_t = \tilde{k}_t \cdot A_tL_t$ grows at the same rate as $A_tL_t$, i.e., $K_{t+1}/K_t = (1+g)(1+n)$. So gross investment to maintain the BGP is $K_{t+1} - K_t + \delta K_t = [(1+g)(1+n) - 1 + \delta]K_t$.
Step 4. Expand $(1+g)(1+n) - 1 = n+g+ng$. So gross investment per period equals $(n+g+\delta+ng)K_t$.
Step 5. Savings-investment identity in equilibrium: $sY_t = $ gross investment $= (n+g+\delta+ng)K_t$.
Step 6. Divide both sides by $A_tL_t$: $s\tilde{y}^* = (n+g+\delta+ng)\tilde{k}^*$.
Step 7. Substitute $\tilde{y}^* = \tilde{k}^{*\alpha}$ and solve: $s\tilde{k}^{*\alpha} = (n+g+\delta+ng)\tilde{k}^*$, hence $\tilde{k}^{*\,1-\alpha} = s/(n+g+\delta+ng)$, hence $\tilde{k}^* = (s/(n+g+\delta+ng))^{1/(1-\alpha)}$.
Identical $\tilde{k}^*$ — but derived by imposing the BGP and reading off the level, rather than searching for the fixed point of the law of motion.

Methodology reflection

The two derivations answer fundamentally different questions, even though they produce the same formula.

The first method ("law-of-motion-and-fixed-point") asks: "What is the fixed point of the recursive map $\tilde{k}_t \mapsto \tilde{k}_{t+1}$ in $\tilde{k}$-space, and does the system actually go there?" It begins with no assumption that a BGP exists; the BGP is found as the fixed point of the transition equation $\tilde{k}_{t+1} = \phi(\tilde{k}_t)$. Because $\phi$ is monotone increasing (the slope is $\phi'(\tilde{k}) = (1/[(1+g)(1+n)])(\alpha s \tilde{k}^{\alpha-1} + 1 - \delta)$, which is positive everywhere) with $\phi'(0) = \infty$ and $\phi'(\infty) < 1$ (under the plausible stability condition $n+g+\delta+ng > 0$, Lecture 2b p10), the fixed point is unique and globally stable. So this method delivers BGP existence, uniqueness, and convergence — three properties for the price of one.

The second method ("BGP-imposed-and-leveled") asks: "Conditional on a BGP existing, what level of $\tilde{k}$ is consistent with it?" It assumes BGP existence from the start and reads off the level via the accounting identity sY = gross investment. It does not tell you whether the BGP is the unique attractor of the dynamic system, nor whether an economy starting at arbitrary $\tilde{k}_0$ will converge to it. For policy applications where you care about transition paths — will the savings-rate hike actually deliver the predicted higher $\tilde{k}^*$, and how long will it take to get most of the way there? — only the first method is sufficient.

Why both still matter pedagogically: the second derivation makes it transparent why the BGP has the form it does — savings have to fund the investment that maintains the capital-output ratio. The first derivation reveals the same form by mechanical fixed-point search but is less informative about the underlying economic accounting. The combination delivers more than either alone: existence and stability from one, structural intuition from the other.

Worked illustration of the transition. Suppose we calibrate $s = 0.22$, $\alpha = 1/3$, $n = 0.01$, $g = 0.02$, $\delta = 0.05$. Steady-state drag: $n+g+\delta+ng = 0.0802$. So $\tilde{k}^* = (0.22/0.0802)^{1/(2/3)} = 2.743^{1.5} \approx 4.54$, and $\tilde{y}^* = 4.54^{1/3} \approx 1.66$. Starting from $\tilde{k}_0 = 1$, the transition equation simulates $\tilde{k}_1 = (0.22 \cdot 1^{1/3} + 0.95 \cdot 1)/[(1.01)(1.02)] = (0.22 + 0.95)/1.0302 \approx 1.135$. After ten periods you are about a quarter of the way to $\tilde{k}^*$; after fifty periods, about $90\%$ of the way (consistent with convergence speed $\lambda \approx 5\%$ — see Ex 3). On the level scale, $k_t = A_t \tilde{k}_t$ continues to grow at $g$ along the BGP even when $\tilde{k}$ has stopped moving, which is what stylized fact #5 demanded. This is the cleanest illustration of why the effective-labour-unit trick is the right one: in physical units, capital grows forever, and there is no fixed point to compute; in effective-labour units, capital settles to a constant, and the entire growth-in-levels story emerges mechanically from the $A_t$-trend that multiplies through. Without the change of variable, the basic Solow toolkit (fixed-point analysis, linearization around steady state, comparative statics on $k^*$) would be inapplicable to a model with persistent growth.

Opening lets that excess capital flow out to chase higher returns abroad. Lower domestic $k$ raises domestic MPK back toward $\bar{r}$ (good for capital owners) but, by Cobb-Douglas, also lowers $w = (1-\alpha)Bk^\alpha$. The wage falls in absolute terms.

Political economy. In strong-saver economies, opening creates a clean Stolper-Samuelson-style distributional conflict: capital owners gain (their portfolio earns the higher $\bar{r}$), workers lose (real wages fall). The bourgeois-internationalist alliance "capital is mobile, labour is not" follows directly from the algebra. In weak-saver countries, the conflict flips: opening pulls in foreign capital, raises domestic $k$, raises wages, but lowers the rate of return capital owners earn at home — capital owners would prefer autarky, workers would prefer opening.

The political coalition supporting capital-account liberalization is therefore predictable from current-account position. In surplus countries that are net capital exporters (Germany, Japan, China, the Gulf, Switzerland) — strong savers in the model's sense — the elite-financier alliance pushes opening. In deficit countries that have been net importers (US over most of its postwar history, much of Latin America historically, Southern Europe in the 2000s) — weak savers — labour interests align with opening; the political resistance often comes from rentier or domestic-industrial coalitions worried about return-erosion or import competition. This is a regularity that the Solow model picks up via a single inequality on $s/n$ vs $\bar{s}/\bar{n}$ — one of the rare cases in growth theory where the math directly anticipates an observed political pattern.

One numerical illustration: Switzerland's gross savings rate is around $30\%$ and population growth near $0.7\%$, so $s/n \approx 43$. The OECD average is roughly $s/n \approx 20$. The model predicts Swiss real wages fall on opening — and historically Switzerland's wage premium relative to its high productivity has indeed compressed as the Swiss franc became a global vehicle currency and Swiss capital flooded out. The wage caveat is, in this small-economy sense, observable.

In the open economy of Lecture 2a (with $\delta = 0$), the savings rate no longer determines $k^*$ — the world rate $\bar{r}$ does, as you proved in Exercise 1. So what does $s$ control in the open economy, and what would a "Golden Rule for the open economy" even mean?

Sketch the argument: identify the steady-state object that $s$ does control, write $c^{n*}$ (national consumption per capita) as a function of $s$ using the open-economy steady-state formulas of Lecture 2a p20-22, and either (a) derive an open-economy $s^{**}$, or (b) argue that the concept either breaks down or coincides with a corner solution. 2-3 paragraphs.

Solve on paper first.

Anchor

$s^{**} = \alpha$ maximizes steady-state consumption per capita $c^*$, not steady-state output $y^*$. The distinction matters: raising $s$ beyond $s^{**}$ does raise $y^*$ (the elasticity of $y^*$ with respect to $s$ is $\alpha/(1-\alpha) > 0$ throughout), but it raises required investment $(n+\delta)k^*$ even faster — so the remaining "consumable" share $(1-s)y^*$ falls. The Golden Rule trades off level-of-output against the share-consumed. At $s = \alpha$, the marginal output gain exactly equals the marginal investment cost. Above $\alpha$, the economy is "over-saving" — accumulating capital that costs more in foregone consumption than it generates in extra steady-state output.

Main proof

Start from $\tilde{c}^* = (1-s)\tilde{y}^*$ in effective-labour units, with $\tilde{y}^* = (s/(n+g+\delta+ng))^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$ (from Ex 2):

Step 1. $\tilde{c}^*(s) = (1-s)\left(\dfrac{s}{n+g+\delta+ng}\right)^{\!\!\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$. Take logs to make the differentiation transparent.
Step 2. $\ln \tilde{c}^*(s) = \ln(1-s) + \dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\ln s - \dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\ln(n+g+\delta+ng)$.
Step 3. Only the first two terms depend on $s$. The third term is an $s$-independent additive constant. So maximizing $\tilde{c}^*$ is equivalent to maximizing $F(s) \equiv \ln(1-s) + \dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\ln s$.
Step 4. Differentiate: $F'(s) = -\dfrac{1}{1-s} + \dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\cdot\dfrac{1}{s}$.
Step 5. First-order condition $F'(s^{**}) = 0$: $\dfrac{\alpha}{(1-\alpha)s^{**}} = \dfrac{1}{1-s^{**}}$.
Step 6. Cross-multiply: $\alpha(1-s^{**}) = (1-\alpha)s^{**}$.
Step 7. Expand: $\alpha - \alpha s^{**} = s^{**} - \alpha s^{**}$. The $\alpha s^{**}$ terms cancel.
Step 8. So $\alpha = s^{**}$. Second-order check: $F''(s) = -1/(1-s)^2 - \alpha/[(1-\alpha)s^2]$, which is negative for any admissible $s \in (0,1)$, so $F$ is concave everywhere and $s^{**}$ is the unique interior maximum.
$s^{**}_\text{general} = \alpha$ — identical to the basic-Solow Golden Rule, despite the presence of technological progress.

Why $g$ doesn't shift the result. $g$ enters $\tilde{c}^*$ only through the factor $(n+g+\delta+ng)^{-\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$, which is multiplicative in $s$. In logs, this becomes an additive $s$-independent constant. Constants do not move the maximizer of a smooth function. So $g$ lowers the level of $\tilde{c}^*$ that the Golden Rule achieves (technology growth raises required investment to maintain effective-labour-unit capital), but it does not change the optimal share of output to save. The Golden Rule formula is purely structural: it pins $s^{**}$ to $\alpha$ regardless of the discounting environment, the rate of technological progress, or the depreciation rate. The only thing it depends on is the elasticity of output with respect to capital — a feature of the production technology alone, not of any preference or growth parameter.

Calibration: with $\alpha \approx 1/3$, the Golden Rule prescribes saving roughly $33\%$ of output. Empirical OECD savings rates are around $20$-$25\%$, so most countries are under-saving relative to the Golden Rule — the model's policy prescription is to encourage savings. This is the conclusion Cozzi flags in Lecture 1c p15 and reiterates in Lecture 2b p15.

Method variation

The same answer falls out of the marginal-product condition without ever writing $c$ as a function of $s$. Start from the production function:

Step 1. $Y_t = K_t^\alpha (A_tL_t)^{1-\alpha}$. The marginal product of capital is $r_t = \partial Y_t/\partial K_t = \alpha K_t^{\alpha-1}(A_tL_t)^{1-\alpha}$.
Step 2. Factor: $r_t = \alpha [K_t/(A_tL_t)]^{\alpha-1} = \alpha \tilde{k}_t^{\alpha-1}$. In steady state $r^* = \alpha (\tilde{k}^*)^{\alpha-1}$.
Step 3. Steady-state condition (Ex 2): $s(\tilde{k}^*)^{\alpha-1} = n+g+\delta+ng$, which rearranges to $(\tilde{k}^*)^{\alpha-1} = (n+g+\delta+ng)/s$.
Step 4. Substitute Step 3 into Step 2: $r^* = \alpha(n+g+\delta+ng)/s$.
Step 5. The modified Golden Rule condition (the Ramsey-style intuition: MPK net of depreciation should equal the effective growth rate of effective labour in steady state, otherwise capital is either too abundant or too scarce to maximize $c$): $r^* - \delta = n + g + ng$, i.e., $r^* = n + g + \delta + ng$.
Step 6. Substitute Step 4 into Step 5: $\dfrac{\alpha(n+g+\delta+ng)}{s} = n+g+\delta+ng$.
Step 7. The factor $(n+g+\delta+ng)$ is strictly positive (it's the steady-state drag, which must be positive for stability) and so can be cancelled from both sides: $\alpha/s = 1$, hence $s = \alpha$.
$s^{**} = \alpha$ — identical result via the marginal-product route, confirming the two derivations agree.

Equivalence of the methods. The consumption-maximization approach explicitly writes $\tilde{c}^*$ as a function of $s$ and finds the interior maximum by differentiation. The marginal-product approach imposes the first-order condition for an interior maximum directly — at the optimum, the social return on the marginal unit of savings (which is invested in capital and earns net MPK = $r^* - \delta$) must equal the effective rate at which the next generation's needs are growing ($n+g+ng \approx n+g$). Both are consequences of the same FOC; they just parameterize it differently. The marginal-product framing has the pedagogical advantage of foreshadowing the Ramsey model's "modified Golden Rule" (where the rate $n+g$ gets replaced by $n+g+\rho$ for time discount $\rho$ — but the structure is the same).

Counterfactual

In the open economy of Lecture 2a, $\bar{r}$ pins $k^* = (\alpha B/\bar{r})^{1/(1-\alpha)}$ independently of $s$ (Ex 1). So $s$ no longer affects domestic capital at all. What $s$ does affect is national wealth: $v^* = \dfrac{s}{n - s\bar{r}}w^*$ (Lecture 2a p20). Through $v^*$, $s$ shifts national income $y^{n*} = w^* + \bar{r}v^* = w^*/(1-s\bar{r}/n)$ and hence national consumption $c^{n*} = (1-s)y^{n*}$.

So we ask: what $s$ maximizes $c^{n*}$? Write $c^{n*}(s) = (1-s)\dfrac{w^*}{1-s\bar{r}/n}$ where $w^*$ is $s$-independent (since $k^*$ is). Log-differentiate:

Step 1. $\ln c^{n*}(s) = \ln(1-s) - \ln(1-s\bar{r}/n) + \ln w^*$ (last term constant in $s$).
Step 2. $\dfrac{d\ln c^{n*}}{ds} = -\dfrac{1}{1-s} + \dfrac{\bar{r}/n}{1-s\bar{r}/n}$ (chain rule on $-\ln(1-s\bar{r}/n)$ gives $-(-\bar{r}/n)/(1-s\bar{r}/n) = (\bar{r}/n)/(1-s\bar{r}/n)$).
Step 3. Set to zero: $\dfrac{\bar{r}/n}{1-s\bar{r}/n} = \dfrac{1}{1-s}$, i.e., $(\bar{r}/n)(1-s) = 1 - s\bar{r}/n$.
Step 4. Expand: $\bar{r}/n - s\bar{r}/n = 1 - s\bar{r}/n$. The $s\bar{r}/n$ terms cancel from both sides.
Step 5. Result: $\bar{r}/n = 1$, i.e., $\bar{r} = n$. This is a condition on parameters, not on $s$ — so the FOC has no $s$ to solve for.
There is no interior Golden-Rule savings rate in the small open economy. The FOC reduces to a knife-edge condition on the exogenous world rate, $\bar{r} = n$, which is generically false. The optimum is at a corner.

Two corner cases. If $\bar{r} > n$ (the world rate exceeds domestic population growth — the empirically typical case), then $d\ln c^{n*}/ds > 0$ throughout the admissible range $s\bar{r}/n < 1$, so $c^{n*}$ is maximized at the boundary $s \to n/\bar{r}$ — i.e., save as much as the stability condition allows. Going beyond would generate explosive wealth accumulation, but right at the boundary, $v^* \to \infty$ and $c^{n*} \to \infty$ (a degenerate but mathematically clean limit). If $\bar{r} < n$, the inequality flips: $d\ln c^{n*}/ds < 0$ everywhere and the optimum is $s = 0$ — never save at all, because marginal saving cannot keep up with the dilution from population growth.

The deep reason. The open-economy savings decision is no longer about building productive capital — that role has been delegated to the world capital market via $\bar{r}$. Savings are now pure asset accumulation: they fund foreign holdings $f^*$, which earn interest $\bar{r}f^*$. The closed-economy Golden Rule trade-off was "save more raises $y^*$ but also raises required investment, and the two effects cancel exactly at $s = \alpha$." In the open economy that internal trade-off doesn't exist, because saving doesn't drive domestic investment. The remaining trade-off is between consuming now versus consuming the compounded interest later, and that comparison is monotone in $s$ (either always positive or always negative depending on whether $\bar{r}$ beats $n$). The Golden Rule, as a concept, requires that savings actually drive capital accumulation; in the small open economy that link is severed, and the concept collapses.

(Note: in the large-economy counterfactual of Ex 1, $\bar{r}$ becomes endogenous in $s$ and an interior Golden Rule re-emerges, smoothly interpolating between the closed-economy $s^{**} = \alpha$ and this open-economy corner solution.)
