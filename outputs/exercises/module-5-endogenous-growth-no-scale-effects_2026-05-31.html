Exercise 1Killing the scale effect: deriving g^e = rho s_R^lambda
Scope: Lecture 5a pp.2-8 (R&D dilution, scale-free macromodel of truly endogenous growth, eqs (1)-(4)); contrast point restated from Module 4 / Chapter 9
Anchor

Restate from Module 4, no derivation. Chapter 9's R&D sector produces new technology according to $A_{t+1}-A_t=\rho A_t^{\phi}L_{At}^{\lambda}$, where $L_{At}$ is the number of workers in research, $\rho>0$ is research productivity, and $0<\phi\le 1$, $0<\lambda\le 1$. (a) Say in words what the exponent $\phi$ controls and what $\lambda$ controls. (b) Define, in one sentence, what the scale effect is.

Hint 1. $\phi$ is the exponent on the existing knowledge stock $A_t$ ("standing on shoulders"); $\lambda$ is the exponent on research labour $L_{At}$ ("stepping on toes" if $\lambda<1$).
Hint 2. The scale effect is about whether the level of the research input — and hence the size of the population — raises the economy's long-run growth rate.
Main proof

Now build the scale-free model of Lecture 5a. Set $\phi=1$ and replace the research level $L_{At}$ with the research share $s_{Rt}\equiv L_{At}/L_t$, so the R&D law becomes $A_{t+1}-A_t=\rho A_t s_{Rt}^{\lambda}$. Assuming a constant share $s_{Rt}=s_R$, derive the technological growth rate $g^e\equiv (A_{t+1}-A_t)/A_t$. Show every step and confirm explicitly that the result depends on neither $A_t$ nor $t$.

Hint 1. Divide both sides of $A_{t+1}-A_t=\rho A_t s_R^{\lambda}$ by $A_t$.
Hint 2. Because $\phi=1$, the knowledge-stock factor is $A_t^{\phi}=A_t^{1}$; after you divide by $A_t$ it becomes $A_t^{0}=1$ and disappears.
Hint 3. Once $s_R$ is constant, nothing on the right-hand side carries a time index — so the growth rate is constant. Check there is no $A_t$ and no $L_t$ left anywhere.
Counterfactual / falsification

Stress-test the result against a population jump. Suppose the labour force is permanently scaled up from $L$ to $cL$ with $c>1$, holding the R&D share $s_R$ fixed. Compute the resulting long-run technology growth rate under (i) the scale-free law $A_{t+1}-A_t=\rho A_t s_R^{\lambda}$, and (ii) the $\phi=1$ level law $A_{t+1}-A_t=\rho A_t L_{At}^{\lambda}$ with $L_{At}=s_R L$. By what factor does each growth rate change? Then state which formulation the Jones (1995) evidence — the number of US researchers rose several-fold across the 20th century while TFP growth stayed roughly flat — supports, and why.

Hint 1. For (ii), first substitute $L_{At}=s_R L$ to express the level-law growth rate in terms of $L$, then apply the jump $L\to cL$.
Methodology reflection

The algebra hides a story. In 2-4 sentences, explain why the R&D that drives aggregate growth is the share $s_R$ and not the level $L_A$ — i.e. why a bigger population dilutes research. Identify the one empirical assumption (about the number of products/sectors) that the whole dilution argument rests on, and say what would happen to the scale effect if that assumption failed.

Solve on paper first.

Anchor

This is the Chapter 9 R&D engine, restated as our launch pad. The flow of new ideas each period, $A_{t+1}-A_t$, is produced by combining two inputs: the stock of existing knowledge $A_t$ and the research labour $L_{At}$.

(a) $\phi$ is the elasticity of idea-production with respect to the existing knowledge stock $A_t$ — the "standing on the shoulders of giants" channel. If $\phi>0$, past discoveries make today's researchers more productive; $\phi=1$ means knowledge is exactly as fertile no matter how advanced the economy already is. $\lambda$ is the elasticity with respect to research labour $L_{At}$. If $\lambda<1$ there is duplication or congestion in research ("stepping on toes"): doubling researchers less-than-doubles the idea flow.

(b) Scale effect: the prediction that a larger research input — and therefore, since researchers are a slice of the population, a larger population — raises the economy's long-run growth rate. Under it, big countries should grow forever faster than small ones.

This is precisely the prediction of the original Romer (1990) R&D model — the knife-edge case $\phi=1$ fed by the research level $L_A$ with $n=0$, seen in Chapter 9 — and it is the prediction Module 5 sets out to repair, because the data flatly reject it.

$A_{t+1}-A_t=\rho A_t^{\phi}L_{At}^{\lambda}$  —  $\phi$: returns to the knowledge stock; $\lambda$: returns to research labour. Scale effect = the level $L_{A}$ (hence population) sets long-run growth.

Main proof

We now make the two changes that define the scale-free model: impose $\phi=1$, and feed the R&D sector the research share $s_{Rt}=L_{At}/L_t$ rather than the research level $L_{At}$. The law of motion becomes $A_{t+1}-A_t=\rho A_t s_{Rt}^{\lambda}$.

Step 1 Start from the new R&D law with a constant share $s_{Rt}=s_R$: $$A_{t+1}-A_t=\rho A_t s_R^{\lambda}.$$
Step 2 Divide both sides by $A_t$ to form the growth rate: $$\frac{A_{t+1}-A_t}{A_t}=\frac{\rho A_t s_R^{\lambda}}{A_t}.$$
Step 3 On the right, the knowledge-stock factor is $A_t^{\phi}=A_t^{1}$ because we set $\phi=1$. Dividing it by $A_t$ leaves $A_t^{1-1}=A_t^{0}=1$: $$\frac{A_{t+1}-A_t}{A_t}=\rho\, s_R^{\lambda}.$$
Step 4 Define $g^e\equiv (A_{t+1}-A_t)/A_t$. The right-hand side contains only constants — $\rho$, $s_R$, $\lambda$ — with no $A_t$ and no $L_t$ and no time index. Hence the growth rate is constant for all $t$.

The cancellation in Step 3 is the entire trick. With $\phi=1$ the knowledge stock enters the idea flow linearly, so it scales out when we divide; and because we wrote the input as the share $s_R$ (a pure number) rather than the level $L_A$, no measure of economy size remains. Population $L_t$ has vanished from the growth rate.

Why insist on $\phi=1$? It is the knife-edge that makes growth truly endogenous. If $\phi<1$, the knowledge-stock factor would leave a residual $A_t^{\phi-1}$ that decays as $A_t$ grows, so a constant $s_R$ could not hold up a constant growth rate and policy would touch growth only during the transition — the semi-endogenous case. Exactly at $\phi=1$ that decay disappears, the growth rate locks onto $\rho s_R^{\lambda}$, and a permanent change in the research share buys a permanent change in growth.

$g^e=\dfrac{A_{t+1}-A_t}{A_t}=\rho\, s_R^{\lambda}$  (constant; contains no $A_t$, no $L_t$ — "truly endogenous, scale-free")

"Truly endogenous": because $\phi=1$, a permanent change in the policy-controllable $s_R$ changes $g^e$ permanently, not just during a transition. "Scale-free": because only the share enters, the level of population is irrelevant to growth.

Counterfactual / falsification

Scale the labour force from $L$ to $cL$ ($c>1$), keeping $s_R$ fixed.

(i) Scale-free law $g^e=\rho s_R^{\lambda}$. No $L$ appears anywhere. After $L\to cL$ the growth rate is still $\rho s_R^{\lambda}$. Change factor: $1$ (unchanged).
(ii) Level law, $\phi=1$ Divide $A_{t+1}-A_t=\rho A_t L_{At}^{\lambda}$ by $A_t$: $g=\rho L_{At}^{\lambda}$. Substitute $L_{At}=s_R L$: $$g=\rho (s_R L)^{\lambda}=\rho s_R^{\lambda} L^{\lambda}.$$ Now apply $L\to cL$: $$g'=\rho s_R^{\lambda}(cL)^{\lambda}=c^{\lambda}\,\rho s_R^{\lambda} L^{\lambda}=c^{\lambda} g.$$ Change factor: $c^{\lambda}>1$.

So the two formulations make sharply different predictions. The level law says a bigger economy grows permanently faster — multiply the population by $c$ and the growth rate jumps by $c^{\lambda}$. The share law says size is irrelevant: only the fraction of workers in research matters.

To feel the magnitude, take $\lambda=0.8$ and a tenfold labour force ($c=10$): the level law multiplies the growth rate by $c^{\lambda}=10^{0.8}\approx 6.3$, flinging a 2%-growth economy up to roughly 13%. Nothing remotely like that explosion shows up across countries of vastly different size — which is the quantitative bite of the falsification, beyond the bare time-series of Jones (1995).

Verdict from Jones (1995). Over the 20th century the number of scientists and engineers engaged in US R&D rose by something like an order of magnitude. The level law predicts TFP growth should have soared by the corresponding $c^{\lambda}$ factor. Instead, measured TFP growth was roughly flat (even slightly declining). The data therefore falsify the level/scale formulation and are consistent with the scale-free share formulation, in which the several-fold rise in researchers — unaccompanied by a rise in their share, because the workforce and the number of sectors grew too — leaves $g^e$ untouched.

Population doubling $L\to cL$: scale-free $g^e$ unchanged; level-law $g$ multiplied by $c^{\lambda}$. Jones (1995) — researchers up several-fold, TFP growth flat — kills the level/scale law.

Methodology reflection

A larger population does not merely add researchers; it adds new products, new firms, and new sectors. Research done in one sector is mostly useful for that sector, so what raises aggregate productivity growth is research per sector. If the number of sectors is proportional to population $L$, then research per sector is proportional to $L_A/L=s_R$ — the share. A bigger economy has more researchers ($L_A=s_R L$ rises), but it also has proportionally more sectors to spread them over, so research per sector, and hence growth, is unchanged: the extra researchers are diluted across the extra sectors.

The load-bearing assumption is precisely "number of sectors $\propto$ population." If instead the number of sectors were fixed while population grew, the level $L_A$ would once again pile into a fixed set of product lines, research per sector would rise with $L$, and the scale effect would return. Dilution is what converts the level into a share; the proportionality of sectors to people is what powers the dilution.

The payoff is the lecture's headline claim: small economies — Switzerland, Austria, Sweden — can grow in the long run at the same rate as large ones — the US, Japan, Germany — provided they put the same fraction $s_R$ of their labour force into research. Size confers no growth advantage once dilution is taken seriously; only research intensity does.

Growth is driven by R&D per sector; with sectors $\propto$ population, that equals the share $s_R$. The scale effect dies because extra people bring extra sectors that dilute the extra researchers.
Exercise 2The rest of the model: capital transition and steady state
Scope: Lecture 5a pp.9-11 (complete model, per-effective-worker reduction, eqs (4)-(6))
Anchor

Final output is $Y_t=K_t^{\alpha}\,(A_t L_{Yt})^{1-\alpha}$, where only the non-research workers $L_{Yt}=(1-s_R)L_t$ produce goods (the fraction $s_R$ is in the lab). Define the per-effective-worker variables $\tilde k_t\equiv K_t/(A_t L_t)$ and $\tilde y_t\equiv Y_t/(A_t L_t)$, and show that $\tilde y_t=\tilde k_t^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}$. State in one sentence where the $(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}$ factor comes from.

Hint 1. Substitute $L_{Yt}=(1-s_R)L_t$ into the production function first.
Hint 2. To pull out the normaliser, write $K_t^{\alpha}=(A_t L_t)^{\alpha}\tilde k_t^{\alpha}$ and take $(A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}$ out of the labour term, so that $A_t L_t$ factors out cleanly when you divide.
Main proof

Capital accumulates as $K_{t+1}=sY_t+(1-\delta)K_t$; population grows at rate $n$ and technology at the constant rate $g^e=\rho s_R^{\lambda}$ from Exercise 1. (a) Derive the transition equation for $\tilde k_t$. (b) Solve for the steady-state values $\tilde k^{*}$ and $\tilde y^{*}$. (c) Show that per-capita output $y_t=Y_t/L_t=\tilde y_t A_t$ grows at $g^e$ in the steady state. Show every algebraic step.

Hint 1. For (a), divide $K_{t+1}=sY_t+(1-\delta)K_t$ by $A_{t+1}L_{t+1}$ and use $A_{t+1}L_{t+1}=(1+g^e)(1+n)\,A_t L_t$.
Hint 2. For (b), set $\tilde k_{t+1}=\tilde k_t=\tilde k^{*}$ and use the identity $(1+n)(1+g^e)-(1-\delta)=n+g^e+\delta+ng^e$.
Hint 3. For (c), write the gross growth factor $y_{t+1}/y_t=(\tilde y_{t+1}/\tilde y_t)(1+g^e)$ and take the limit as $\tilde y_t\to\tilde y^{*}$.
Counterfactual

Notice that $s_R$ pulls in two directions: it raises the growth rate $g^e=\rho s_R^{\lambda}$, but it also lowers the level of output, both through the explicit $(1-s_R)$ factor in $\tilde y^{*}$ and through the larger $g^e$ in the steady-state denominator. Consider a permanent increase in $s_R$. Describe the resulting level-vs-growth tension on a plot of $\ln y_t$ against time: which effect dominates in the long run, and what does this imply for the difference between a growth-maximizing and a welfare-maximizing $s_R$? Brief prose; no full re-derivation.

Hint 1. On a log scale, the growth rate is the asymptotic slope of $\ln y_t$ and the level penalty is a downward shift of the intercept — which one wins as $t\to\infty$?
Solve on paper first.

Anchor

The per-effective-worker reduction strips out the two things that trend in the long run — technology $A_t$ and population $L_t$ — so we can study the stationary part of the economy. We normalise every aggregate by $A_t L_t$ ("effective labour").

Geometrically, dividing by $A_t L_t$ "de-trends" the economy: $K_t$ and $Y_t$ themselves grow without bound, but the ratios $\tilde k_t$ and $\tilde y_t$ strip out both trends — population and technology — leaving variables that settle to constants. Studying $\tilde k_t$ is how we turn a perpetually-growing economy into a stationary one with a solvable fixed point.

Step 1 Substitute the goods-producing labour $L_{Yt}=(1-s_R)L_t$ into the production function: $$Y_t=K_t^{\alpha}\big(A_t(1-s_R)L_t\big)^{1-\alpha}=K_t^{\alpha}(A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}.$$
Step 2 Divide through by $A_t L_t$ and rewrite $K_t^{\alpha}=(A_t L_t)^{\alpha}\tilde k_t^{\alpha}$: $$\tilde y_t=\frac{Y_t}{A_t L_t}=\frac{(A_t L_t)^{\alpha}\tilde k_t^{\alpha}(A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}}{A_t L_t}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}.$$
Step 3 The powers of $A_t L_t$ collect to $(A_t L_t)^{\alpha+(1-\alpha)-1}=(A_t L_t)^{0}=1$: $$\tilde y_t=\tilde k_t^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}.$$

The $(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}$ factor appears because only the fraction $1-s_R$ of the labour force makes goods; the share $s_R$ sits in the lab and contributes to growth rather than to current output. Diverting labour into research is therefore not free — it shows up here as a permanent down-scaling of the output level.

$\tilde y_t=\tilde k_t^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}$  — the $(1-s_R)$ factor is the output cost of staffing the R&D sector.

Main proof

(a) Transition equation.

Step 1 Begin with capital accumulation $K_{t+1}=sY_t+(1-\delta)K_t$ and divide both sides by $A_{t+1}L_{t+1}$. The left side is $\tilde k_{t+1}$.
Step 2 On the right, technology grows at $g^e$ and population at $n$, so $A_{t+1}L_{t+1}=(1+g^e)A_t\cdot(1+n)L_t=(1+n)(1+g^e)A_t L_t$. Hence $$\tilde k_{t+1}=\frac{sY_t+(1-\delta)K_t}{(1+n)(1+g^e)A_t L_t}=\frac{1}{(1+n)(1+g^e)}\Big[s\frac{Y_t}{A_t L_t}+(1-\delta)\frac{K_t}{A_t L_t}\Big].$$
Step 3 Recognise $Y_t/(A_t L_t)=\tilde y_t$ and $K_t/(A_t L_t)=\tilde k_t$, then substitute $\tilde y_t=\tilde k_t^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}$: $$\tilde k_{t+1}=\frac{1}{(1+n)(1+g^e)}\Big[s\,\tilde k_t^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}+(1-\delta)\tilde k_t\Big].$$
$\tilde k_{t+1}=\dfrac{1}{(1+n)(1+g^e)}\big[s\,\tilde k_t^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}+(1-\delta)\tilde k_t\big]$  (the $(1+n)(1+g^e)$ denominator dilutes capital across both more workers and more effective technology)

(b) Steady state.

Step 4 At a steady state $\tilde k_{t+1}=\tilde k_t=\tilde k^{*}$. Multiply the transition by $(1+n)(1+g^e)$: $$(1+n)(1+g^e)\tilde k^{*}=s(\tilde k^{*})^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}+(1-\delta)\tilde k^{*}.$$
Step 5 Move the $(1-\delta)\tilde k^{*}$ term to the left and factor $\tilde k^{*}$: $$\big[(1+n)(1+g^e)-(1-\delta)\big]\tilde k^{*}=s(\tilde k^{*})^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}.$$
Step 6 Expand the bracket: $(1+n)(1+g^e)=1+n+g^e+ng^e$, and subtracting $(1-\delta)$ gives $n+g^e+\delta+ng^e$. So $$(n+g^e+\delta+ng^e)\,\tilde k^{*}=s(\tilde k^{*})^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}.$$
Step 7 Divide by $(\tilde k^{*})^{\alpha}$ and by $(n+g^e+\delta+ng^e)$: $$(\tilde k^{*})^{1-\alpha}=\frac{s(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}}{n+g^e+\delta+ng^e}.$$ Raise both sides to $1/(1-\alpha)$: $$\tilde k^{*}=\Big(\frac{s}{n+g^e+\delta+ng^e}\Big)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}(1-s_R).$$
Step 8 Then $\tilde y^{*}=(\tilde k^{*})^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}=\Big(\frac{s}{n+g^e+\delta+ng^e}\Big)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}(1-s_R)^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}$, and $(1-s_R)^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}=(1-s_R)$, so $$\tilde y^{*}=\Big(\frac{s}{n+g^e+\delta+ng^e}\Big)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}(1-s_R).$$
$\tilde k^{*}=\Big(\dfrac{s}{n+g^e+\delta+ng^e}\Big)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}(1-s_R)$,   $\tilde y^{*}=\Big(\dfrac{s}{n+g^e+\delta+ng^e}\Big)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}(1-s_R)$

Read the steady-state denominator $n+g^e+\delta+ng^e$ as a "break-even" requirement: to hold $\tilde k$ constant, gross saving $s\tilde y$ must replace capital lost to depreciation ($\delta$), diluted across new workers ($n$), and diluted across more effective technology ($g^e$, plus the small interaction $ng^e$). Saving above break-even raises $\tilde k$; the steady state is where saving exactly meets it. Note in passing that faster technology growth $g^e$ raises the denominator and so lowers $\tilde k^{*}$ — a subtle point the counterfactual leans on.

(c) Per-capita growth.

Step 9 Per-capita output is $y_t=Y_t/L_t=(Y_t/(A_t L_t))\,A_t=\tilde y_t A_t$. Form the gross growth factor: $$\frac{y_{t+1}}{y_t}=\frac{\tilde y_{t+1}A_{t+1}}{\tilde y_t A_t}=\frac{\tilde y_{t+1}}{\tilde y_t}\,(1+g^e).$$
Step 10 In the steady state $\tilde y_t\to\tilde y^{*}$, a constant, so $\tilde y_{t+1}/\tilde y_t\to 1$ and $y_{t+1}/y_t\to 1+g^e$. Therefore $$\frac{y_{t+1}-y_t}{y_t}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}g^e=\rho s_R^{\lambda}.$$

The level variables $\tilde k,\tilde y$ settle to constants, so all long-run per-capita growth is inherited from technology $A_t$ — and that rate, $g^e=\rho s_R^{\lambda}$, is set entirely by the policy-controllable research share. That is the punchline of "truly endogenous, scale-free" growth: the steady-state growth rate is a policy choice, not a demographic given.

On this balanced growth path the level variables $K_t,Y_t,C_t$ and the total wage bill all grow at $n+g^e$ (population plus technology), while their per-capita counterparts $y_t,c_t$ and the wage per worker grow at the pure technology rate $g^e$. The interest rate $r_t$, a function of $\tilde k^{*}$ alone, is constant — the defining signature of balanced growth.

$\dfrac{y_{t+1}-y_t}{y_t}\to g^e=\rho s_R^{\lambda}$  — per-capita output grows at the technology rate, which $s_R$ controls.

Counterfactual

A permanent rise in $s_R$ moves two margins in opposite directions. The growth margin: $g^e=\rho s_R^{\lambda}$ rises, so the asymptotic slope of $\ln y_t$ gets steeper. The level margin: $\tilde y^{*}$ carries the explicit $(1-s_R)$ factor (fewer goods-producers), and the larger $g^e$ also inflates the steady-state denominator $n+g^e+\delta+ng^e$, lowering the first factor too — so the intercept of $\ln y_t$ drops.

On a log-time plot the economy starts on a lower line but climbs at a steeper angle. Two lines of different slope always cross, and the steeper one is eventually higher for good — so for the purpose of output, any finite level penalty is asymptotically dominated by the growth gain. The growth-maximizing policy therefore wants $s_R$ as large as possible (push $s_R\to 1$).

But welfare is built on consumption, not output, and shovelling everyone into the lab ($s_R\to 1$) sends current consumption toward zero — there is almost no one left making goods. The welfare-maximizing $s_R$ is therefore strictly interior and below the growth-maximizing one: it trades the permanent growth dividend against the consumption sacrificed during the (long) transition. This is the classic level-vs-growth tension — permanent growth effects dominate level effects in the limit, but the transition cost is exactly what disciplines the optimal research share.

Contrast this with the basic Solow model of Module 1, where raising the saving rate $s$ lifts the economy onto a higher level path but leaves the long-run growth rate untouched (growth is exogenous there). Here the lever $s_R$ does what Solow's $s$ never could — it moves the growth rate itself. That is the qualitative break endogenous growth makes from the Solow tradition, and it is why a level-vs-growth trade-off, absent in Solow, appears the instant growth becomes a choice.

Higher $s_R$: steeper $\ln y_t$ slope (growth $\uparrow$) but lower intercept (level $\downarrow$). Growth always wins asymptotically $\Rightarrow$ growth-max wants $s_R\to 1$; the consumption cost $\Rightarrow$ welfare-max $s_R$ is interior and smaller.
Exercise 3The Cozzi hybrid: the growing-population growth rate, two ways
Scope: Lecture 5b pp.4-15 (hybrid A-equation, constant/shrinking/growing population cases, balanced-growth solution); numerical instance in the falsification uses the Session 5 p33 calibration
Anchor

Cozzi (2017) posits that technological progress is a linear combination of the two mechanisms: $$A_{t+1}-A_t=\alpha_{sem}\,\rho A_t^{\phi}L_{At}^{\lambda}+(1-\alpha_{sem})\,\rho A_t s_{Rt}^{\lambda},$$ with $0<\alpha_{sem}<1$ the weight on the semi-endogenous mechanism and constant $s_{Rt}=s_R$. (i) Divide by $A_t$ to obtain the growth rate $g_t$. (ii) For the two easy demographic cases $n=0$ and $n<0$, argue in one line each that $g_t$ converges to $(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e$, where $g^e=\rho s_R^{\lambda}$. (iii) State the one-sentence warning about the symbol $\alpha_{sem}$.

Hint 1. With constant $s_R$, $L_{At}=s_R L_t$. The first term keeps an $A_t^{\phi-1}$ after you divide; the second loses its $A_t$ entirely.
Hint 2. Note $g_t\ge(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}>0$ always, so $A_t\to\infty$; with $\phi<1$ that sends $A_t^{\phi-1}\to 0$.
Main proof (Method 1: ratio/division)

Take a growing population, $n>0$. Derive the steady-state hybrid growth rate $g^h$ by the ratio method: isolate the semi-endogenous term, write the same expression one period ahead, divide the two equations, and impose the balanced-growth condition $g_{t+1}=g_t=g^h$. Show every step, and state the condition under which the rate you obtain is the valid steady state.

Hint 1. Move the constant endogenous term across: $g_t-(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}=\alpha_{sem}\rho A_t^{\phi-1}L_{At}^{\lambda}$.
Hint 2. Write that same equation for $t+1$ and divide it by the $t$ version; the $\alpha_{sem}\rho$ cancels and $L_{At+1}/L_{At}=L_{t+1}/L_t=1+n$.
Hint 3. You reach $\dfrac{g_{t+1}-(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}}{g_t-(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}}=(1+g^h)^{\phi-1}(1+n)^{\lambda}$; set the left side to $1$ on the BGP and solve.
Method variation (Method 2: limiting behaviour)

Re-derive the same $n>0$ outcome a second way, by examining the asymptotics of the term $T_t\equiv A_t^{\phi-1}L_{At}^{\lambda}$ directly. For $T_t$ to settle to a positive constant on a balanced growth path (rather than vanish), how fast must $A_t$ grow relative to $L_{At}$? Use that to pin the surviving technology growth rate, and explain how comparing it with $(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e$ decides which of the two candidate rates is the steady state. Show the algebra of the "constant-$T_t$" condition.

Hint 1. $T_t=L_{At}^{\lambda}/A_t^{\,1-\phi}$ is constant in $t$ iff $A_t^{\,1-\phi}\propto L_{At}^{\lambda}$, i.e. $A_t\propto L_{At}^{\lambda/(1-\phi)}$ — take growth rates of both sides.
Hint 2. If $A_t$ grows faster than $L_{At}^{\lambda/(1-\phi)}$ then $T_t\to 0$ and only the endogenous term survives; the race is decided by which candidate rate, $g^{se}$ or $(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e$, is larger.
Falsification probe

A natural guess is that, since technology is a weight-$\alpha_{sem}$ blend of two mechanisms, the growth rate should be the same blend: $g^h\overset{?}{=}\alpha_{sem}\,g^{se}+(1-\alpha_{sem})\,g^e$. Show this guess is wrong: state what the model actually delivers, give a numerical instance where the guess and the truth differ, and explain in economic terms why combining the two mechanisms linearly does not combine the two growth rates linearly. Pin down the mathematical feature that makes the outcome "winner-take-all."

Anchor

The hybrid simply adds the two idea-engines: a semi-endogenous term (knowledge stock $A_t^{\phi}$ with $\phi<1$, fed by the research level $L_{At}$) and a truly-endogenous scale-free term (linear in $A_t$, fed by the research share $s_R$), weighted $\alpha_{sem}$ and $1-\alpha_{sem}$.

(iii) Warning: $\alpha_{sem}$ is the weight on the semi-endogenous mechanism. It has nothing to do with the Cobb-Douglas capital share $\alpha$ in $Y=K^{\alpha}(AL)^{1-\alpha}$; the two just share a Greek letter.

Main proof (Method 1: ratio/division)

For $n>0$ the level input $L_{At}=s_R L_t$ now grows, so the semi-endogenous term need not vanish — we must work for the answer.

Step 1 Isolate the semi-endogenous term by subtracting the constant endogenous part: $$g_t-(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}=\alpha_{sem}\rho A_t^{\phi-1}L_{At}^{\lambda}.\qquad(\ast)$$
Step 2 Write the identical relation one period ahead: $$g_{t+1}-(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}=\alpha_{sem}\rho A_{t+1}^{\phi-1}L_{At+1}^{\lambda}.\qquad(\ast\ast)$$
Step 3 Divide $(\ast\ast)$ by $(\ast)$. The constant $\alpha_{sem}\rho$ cancels: $$\frac{g_{t+1}-(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}}{g_t-(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}}=\Big(\frac{A_{t+1}}{A_t}\Big)^{\phi-1}\Big(\frac{L_{At+1}}{L_{At}}\Big)^{\lambda}.$$
Step 4 Since $L_{At}=s_R L_t$, the research-labour ratio equals the population ratio, $L_{At+1}/L_{At}=L_{t+1}/L_t=1+n$. And $A_{t+1}/A_t=1+g_t$, which on a BGP is $1+g^h$. So the right side is $(1+g^h)^{\phi-1}(1+n)^{\lambda}$.
Step 5 On the BGP $g_{t+1}=g_t=g^h$, so the left side equals $1$: $$1=(1+g^h)^{\phi-1}(1+n)^{\lambda}.$$
Step 6 Solve. Using $\phi-1=-(1-\phi)$, rewrite as $(1+g^h)^{1-\phi}=(1+n)^{\lambda}$, then raise to $1/(1-\phi)$: $$1+g^h=(1+n)^{\frac{\lambda}{1-\phi}}\;\Rightarrow\; g^h=(1+n)^{\frac{\lambda}{1-\phi}}-1\equiv g^{se}.$$

The hybrid steady state, in this case, is exactly the semi-endogenous rate. The derivation divided through by $g_t-(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}$, which is legitimate only if that quantity is positive — i.e. only if $g^{se}>(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e$. If that inequality fails, $(\ast)$ is non-positive and the manipulation is void; the surviving rate is then $(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e$, as Method 2 shows directly.

$n>0$, ratio method: $1=(1+g^h)^{\phi-1}(1+n)^{\lambda}\Rightarrow g^h=(1+n)^{\lambda/(1-\phi)}-1=g^{se}$, valid iff $g^{se}>(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e$.

Method variation (Method 2: limiting behaviour)

Instead of assuming a BGP, watch the term $T_t\equiv A_t^{\phi-1}L_{At}^{\lambda}=L_{At}^{\lambda}/A_t^{\,1-\phi}$ as $t\to\infty$.

Step 1 $T_t$ converges to a positive constant iff numerator and denominator grow at the same rate, i.e. $A_t^{\,1-\phi}\propto L_{At}^{\lambda}$, which means $A_t\propto L_{At}^{\lambda/(1-\phi)}$.
Step 2 Take gross growth factors of $A_t\propto L_{At}^{\lambda/(1-\phi)}$. Since $L_{At}$ grows at rate $n$: $$1+g^A=(1+n)^{\frac{\lambda}{1-\phi}}\;\Rightarrow\; g^A=(1+n)^{\frac{\lambda}{1-\phi}}-1=g^{se}.$$ So $g^{se}$ is precisely the technology rate that keeps the semi-endogenous term alive.
Step 3 The endogenous term alone guarantees $g_t\ge(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e>0$ — a positive floor on technology growth. Compare this floor with the survival rate $g^{se}$:
Case A: $(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e>g^{se}$ Technology is dragged up by the endogenous floor faster than the rate $g^{se}$ that $T_t$ needs to survive. So $A_t$ outgrows $L_{At}^{\lambda/(1-\phi)}$, the denominator wins, $T_t\to 0$, the semi-endogenous term dies, and $g^h=(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e$.
Case B: $g^{se}>(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e$ The semi-endogenous term grows fast enough to dominate and pins $g^h=g^{se}$; the endogenous floor is then the smaller number and is irrelevant to the long-run rate.

Both methods agree. In the $n>0$ case the steady-state rate is the larger of the two candidates, $g^h=\max\{g^{se},(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e\}$. Method 1 produced the candidate $g^{se}$ and an after-the-fact validity condition; Method 2 explains why the loser disappears — a term that needs technology to grow at exactly $g^{se}$ is annihilated whenever technology grows faster.

Constant-$T_t$ condition $\Rightarrow A_t\propto L_{At}^{\lambda/(1-\phi)}\Rightarrow g^A=g^{se}$. Hence $g^h=\max\{g^{se},\,(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e\}$ for $n>0$.

Falsification probe

What the model delivers. Collecting all three demographic cases, $g^h=\max\{g^{se},(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e\}$ (for $n\le 0$, $g^{se}\le 0<(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e$, so the endogenous rate wins automatically). This is a selection between the two rates, not a blend.

Numerical instance Take the Session-5 calibration $g^{se}=0.054$, $g^e=\rho s_R^{\lambda}=0.0761$, $\alpha_{sem}=0.4$, with $n>0$ large enough that $g^{se}>(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e$. Winner-take-all: $(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e=0.6\times 0.0761=0.0457$, so $g^h=\max\{0.054,0.0457\}=0.054$. Naive blend: $\alpha_{sem}g^{se}+(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e=0.4(0.054)+0.6(0.0761)=0.0216+0.0457=0.0673$. The guess (6.7%) overstates the truth (5.4%).

The convex combination always lands strictly between the two rates, so unless $g^{se}=(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e$ exactly, it can never equal $g^h$.

Why winner-take-all. The two mechanisms add in the level equation ($\Delta A$ is a sum of two terms), but a long-run growth rate is governed by whichever term grows fastest asymptotically. The slower term's share of $\Delta A$ shrinks to zero — it becomes a rounding error relative to the faster one. The decisive mathematical feature is $A_t^{\phi-1}\to 0$ when $\phi<1$ and $A_t\to\infty$: this steadily erodes the per-unit potency of the semi-endogenous term unless population growth $n$ pumps $L_{At}$ up fast enough to offset the erosion. It is a race between exponentials, and the loser's contribution to the growth rate vanishes. The weight $\alpha_{sem}$ only scales the level of each term, so it cannot change who wins the race — it can only move the threshold $\bar n$ at which the winner flips (Exercise 4).

$g^h=\max\{g^{se},(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e\}\ne\alpha_{sem}g^{se}+(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e$. Mechanisms add in levels; the faster-growing one alone sets the asymptotic rate. Winner-take-all, not a blend.

Methodology reflection

Method 1 (ratio) assumes a balanced growth path with $g_{t+1}=g_t=g^h$ and then solves for its value. It is fast and lands cleanly on the candidate rate $g^{se}$, but it presupposes convergence and hides why the other rate sometimes wins — the validity condition only surfaces as an afterthought when you check the sign of what you divided by. Method 2 (limiting behaviour) assumes less: it tracks the actual asymptotics of each term and reveals the mechanism — the semi-endogenous term survives only if $A_t$ grows no faster than $L_{At}^{\lambda/(1-\phi)}$, which is exactly the race that selects the winner.

So if a colleague doubted that a BGP exists at all, reach for Method 2: it never assumes one, instead showing each term either converges or vanishes. If you simply want the threshold $\bar n$ as fast as possible, reach for Method 1: it deposits you directly on $1=(1+g^h)^{\phi-1}(1+n)^{\lambda}$, whose validity boundary $g^{se}=(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e$ is the equation that defines $\bar n$.

Main proof

Step 1 Set the two candidate rates equal at $n=\bar n$: $$(1+\bar n)^{\frac{\lambda}{1-\phi}}-1=(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}.$$
Step 2 Add $1$ to both sides: $$(1+\bar n)^{\frac{\lambda}{1-\phi}}=(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}+1.$$

Intuitively, $\bar n$ answers the question "how fast must population grow for the level-fed semi-endogenous mechanism to win the asymptotic race?" That dependence on $s_R$ is exactly what the next part exploits.

Initialization (period 0, on the BGP): set $L_0=1$; set $A_0=\big(\rho(s_R L_0)^{\lambda}/g^{se}\big)^{1/(1-\phi)}\approx 1.79$ (from the Main proof) so technology starts on the BGP; set $K_0=\tilde k^{*}A_0 L_0$ using the steady-state $\tilde k^{*}=\big(\frac{s}{n+g^{se}+\delta+ng^{se}}\big)^{1/(1-\alpha)}(1-s_R)$ evaluated at the initial $n=0.04$ (this is the Exercise-2 reduction with $g^{se}$ in place of $g^e$). Fix parameters $\alpha=0.33,\ \phi=0.4,\ \lambda=0.8,\ \delta=0.1,\ s=0.25,\ s_R=0.04,\ \rho=1$.

Recursion — each period $t$, evaluate in this order:

1. Population $L_{t+1}=(1+n_t)L_t$ with $n_t=0.04$ for $t<10$ and $n_t=0.02$ for $t\ge 10$ (this single switch is the experiment). Research labour $L_{At}=s_R L_t$.
2. Technology $A_{t+1}=A_t+\rho A_t^{\phi}L_{At}^{\lambda}$, equivalently $A_{t+1}=A_t(1+g_t)$ with $g_t=\rho A_t^{\phi-1}L_{At}^{\lambda}$.
3. Output $Y_t=K_t^{\alpha}\big(A_t(1-s_R)L_t\big)^{1-\alpha}$.
4. Capital $K_{t+1}=sY_t+(1-\delta)K_t$.
5. Per-worker output and growth $y_t=Y_t/L_t$, then $g^y_t=(y_t-y_{t-1})/y_{t-1}$ (or $\ln y_t-\ln y_{t-1}$).

Run for, say, $t=0,\dots,100$ so the new BGP is reached. Plot $g^y_t$ against $t$ as the headline panel (the transition response to the $n$-drop), with supporting panels of $\ln y_t$ (to see the kink in slope) and $g_t$ (technology growth converging to its new $g^{se}$). Mark $t=10$, where the shock lands.

Before running anything, compute the two balanced-path benchmarks analytically so you know what the simulation must converge to. With the Exercise-5.2 calibration, Economy 1 ($n=0.04$) lands in the semi-endogenous regime with $g^h=g^{se}=0.054$, while Economy 2 ($n=0.02$) lands in the endogenous regime with $g^h=(1-\alpha_{sem})g^e=0.046$ (here $\bar n\approx 3.4\%$, so $n=4\%>\bar n$ but $n=2\%<\bar n$). The simulated $g^y_t$ must settle onto exactly these numbers — a built-in correctness check on the code.

States $K_t,A_t,L_t$; per period update $L\to A\to Y\to K\to y$; the only change at $t=10\to 11$ is $n:0.04\to 0.02$. Plot $g^y_t$ (and $\ln y_t$, $g_t$) over $\sim$100 periods.

Counterfactual

Semi-endogenous ($\phi=0.4$). The long-run technology rate $g^{se}=(1+n)^{\lambda/(1-\phi)}-1$ falls when $n$ falls — from $g^{se}=0.0537$ at $n=0.04$ to $g^{se}=0.0268$ at $n=0.02$. So the BGP growth rate ends lower. But the transition overshoots upward first: a slower-growing workforce shares the inherited capital among fewer new hands, so $\tilde k_t$ and $\tilde y_t$ rise toward new, higher steady-state values, and that transitory rise in $\tilde y_t$ pushes $g^y_t$ above its old level for a while. Bolboaca's plot shows $g^y_t$ staying above its old steady state for about 6 periods, then falling and converging to the new, lower $g^{se}$. Net: short-run boost, lower long-run growth.

Truly endogenous without scale ($\phi=1$). Now $g^e=\rho s_R^{\lambda}$ contains no $n$ at all. A permanent drop in $n$ leaves the long-run growth rate completely unchanged. The same capital-sharing transition still occurs — $\tilde y_t$ rises, so $g^y_t$ overshoots transiently and then returns — but it returns to the same $g^e$ as before. Only the level path of $y_t$ is permanently shifted up; the asymptotic slope of $\ln y_t$ is identical to the pre-shock slope.

This contrast is the whole point of Module 5. Under semi-endogenous growth, demography is destiny: slower population growth means permanently slower technology growth. Under truly-endogenous-without-scale growth, demography is irrelevant to long-run growth — only the research share $s_R$ matters — so even a shrinking-population country keeps growing at $g^e$. The two theories look nearly identical during the 6-period transition but diverge in the asymptote, which is exactly why the simulation must be run long enough to see the limiting slope, not just the overshoot.

The hybrid model of Exercises 3-4 unifies these two pictures. Whether the $n$-drop changes long-run growth depends on which side of the threshold $\bar n$ the new $n$ lands: in the Exercise-5.2 calibration ($\bar n\approx 3.4\%$) the drop from $4\%$ to $2\%$ carries the economy across $\bar n$, flipping it from the semi-endogenous regime (growth tied to $n$) into the endogenous one (growth set by $s_R$). On a plot of $\ln y_t$ the two economies' paths fan permanently apart after the shock — the visual signature that a regime change, not merely a level shift, has occurred.

The policy reading is sharp. A country fearing secular stagnation from a shrinking population is, on the semi-endogenous view, in real trouble — growth is chained to $n$. On the truly-endogenous-without-scale view it is not, provided it sustains its research share $s_R$. The hybrid model says which worry is correct depends on $\bar n$, and that policy itself, by lifting $s_R$ and hence $\bar n$, can move the economy into the regime where demographic decline no longer threatens long-run growth.

Semi-endogenous ($\phi<1$): $g^y_t$ overshoots $\sim$6 periods, then settles to a lower $g^{se}$ (demography is destiny). Truly endogenous ($\phi=1$): same transient overshoot, but long-run $g^e=\rho s_R^{\lambda}$ is unchanged — only the level shifts.
