Exercise 1The private–social wedge: where does the increasing-returns output go?
Scope: Lecture 4a §4–6 (firm vs aggregate production function, competitive factor prices), §7 (learning-by-doing motivation); Slides_Session4 p4–8. Quiz-targeted: Q4 (meaning of φ) and Q15 (factor shares under aggregate IRS).
Anchor

The representative firm, treating aggregate technology $A_t$ as given, produces $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}(A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}$, and the productive externality is $A_t = K_t^{\varphi}$ (the firm's productivity rises with the aggregate capital stock). Impose the equilibrium condition $K_t^d = K_t$ and write the aggregate production function in terms of $K_t$ and $L_t$ alone. Then say, in one phrase, why $\varphi$ is a fundamentally different object from $\alpha$ even though both sit on capital.

Hint 1. Substitute $A_t = K_t^{\varphi}$ into $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}(A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}$ and collect the powers of $K_t$.
Hint 2. You should get $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)} L_t^{1-\alpha}$; the exponents sum to $1 + \varphi(1-\alpha) > 1$. $\alpha$ is what one firm's own capital does; $\varphi$ governs the spillover running through everyone's capital.
Main argument

Here is the puzzle this model forces on you. The aggregate production function $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)}L_t^{1-\alpha}$ has increasing returns to scale — double $K$ and $L$ and output more than doubles. Yet under competitive markets the model still delivers $r_t K_t = \alpha Y_t$ and $w_t L_t = (1-\alpha)Y_t$, so the two factors together are paid $r_tK_t + w_tL_t = Y_t$ — exactly 100% of output, with nothing left over.

  1. Derive $r_t$ and $w_t$ from the firm's first-order conditions, remembering that the firm takes $A_t$ as given, and show the income shares are $\alpha$ and $1-\alpha$.
  2. Resolve the apparent contradiction: how can payments exhaust output if there are increasing returns? Identify precisely what is not being paid for, and to whom the extra returns to scale accrue.
  3. State, in one sentence each, what $\alpha$ measures and what $\varphi$ measures, and why they are not the same number despite both attaching to capital.
Hint 1. FOC for capital: $r_t = \partial Y_t/\partial K_t$ with $A_t$ held fixed. Differentiate $K_t^{\alpha}(A_tL_t)^{1-\alpha}$ w.r.t. $K_t$ — the exponent that comes down is $\alpha$, so $r_t = \alpha Y_t/K_t$.
Hint 2. At the firm level the production function is CRS in $(K,L)$ (the replication argument). By Euler's theorem a CRS function is exactly exhausted by its two marginal products — that is the real reason the shares sum to 1, and it has nothing to do with the aggregate returns to scale.
Hint 3. The increasing returns live only at the aggregate level, through the channel $A_t = K_t^{\varphi}$, which no atomistic firm internalizes. So the social return to capital, $\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)$, exceeds the private return $\alpha$.
Hint 4. The missing piece is the externality $\varphi(1-\alpha)$: it is a return to capital that accrues to society (all firms, via higher $A$) but is paid to nobody, because each firm sees only its private marginal product.

Hint 1. The slope of each curve is the corresponding marginal product of capital: the private slope is the MPK the firm is actually paid ($r_t$), the social slope is the MPK society receives. The vertical gap is a level (total-output) difference; the steepness gap is the marginal-incentive difference — that is the one prices get wrong.
Counterfactual

Now suppose a benevolent planner who recognizes $A_t = K_t^{\varphi}$ chooses the economy's capital accumulation.

  1. Write the planner's perceived marginal product of capital (differentiate the aggregate $Y$ treating the $A_t = K_t^\varphi$ link as endogenous) and compare it to the competitive $r_t = \alpha Y_t/K_t$. By what factor do they differ?
  2. Hence argue whether the decentralized economy accumulates too little or too much capital, and what a corrective investment subsidy would have to do. (Rapid prose; one line of algebra for the social MPK.)
Hint 1. Social MPK $= \dfrac{d}{dK}\,K^{\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)}L^{1-\alpha} = \big(\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)\big)\dfrac{Y}{K} = \dfrac{\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)}{\alpha}\,r_t$, a strict multiple of the private return.

Solve on paper first.

Anchor

Step 1 Substitute the externality $A_t = K_t^{\varphi}$ into the firm's function $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}(A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}$: $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}\big(K_t^{\varphi}L_t\big)^{1-\alpha} = K_t^{\alpha}K_t^{\varphi(1-\alpha)}L_t^{1-\alpha}$.
Step 2 Collect the powers of $K_t$: $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)}L_t^{1-\alpha}$. The capital exponent is $\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)$ and the exponents sum to $\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)+(1-\alpha)=1+\varphi(1-\alpha)>1$ whenever $\varphi>0$: increasing returns to scale.
Aggregate PF: $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)}L_t^{1-\alpha}$. $\alpha$ is the firm's private capital exponent; $\varphi$ is the elasticity of the spillover $A_t=K_t^{\varphi}$ — the strength with which everyone's capital lifts everyone's productivity.

Main argument

Step 1 — factor prices The firm chooses $K_t,L_t$ taking $A_t$ as a constant. The first-order conditions equate real rental rates to firm-level marginal products: $r_t=\dfrac{\partial Y_t}{\partial K_t}=\alpha K_t^{\alpha-1}(A_tL_t)^{1-\alpha}=\alpha\dfrac{Y_t}{K_t}$, and $w_t=\dfrac{\partial Y_t}{\partial L_t}=(1-\alpha)K_t^{\alpha}A_t^{1-\alpha}L_t^{-\alpha}=(1-\alpha)\dfrac{Y_t}{L_t}$.
Step 2 — shares Multiply through: $r_tK_t=\alpha Y_t$ and $w_tL_t=(1-\alpha)Y_t$. The capital income share is $r_tK_t/Y_t=\alpha$ and the labour share is $1-\alpha$, exactly, independent of $K,L,A$. Adding them, $r_tK_t+w_tL_t=\alpha Y_t+(1-\alpha)Y_t=Y_t$.
Step 3 — resolve the paradox The decisive fact is that at the firm level, with $A_t$ fixed, the function $K_t^{\alpha}(A_tL_t)^{1-\alpha}$ is homogeneous of degree one in the two paid inputs $(K_t,L_t)$ — $\alpha+(1-\alpha)=1$. By Euler's theorem a degree-one function is exactly exhausted by its two marginal products. So payments sum to firm output by an accounting identity that knows nothing about aggregate returns to scale.
Step 4 — the missing return The increasing returns are entirely a property of the aggregate map $K_t\mapsto A_t=K_t^{\varphi}$, which each firm treats as exogenous. The social marginal product of capital is $\big(\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)\big)Y/K$; the private one paid out is $\alpha Y/K$. The difference $\varphi(1-\alpha)\,Y/K$ is a genuine return to capital that flows to all firms through higher $A$ but is invoiced to no one. There is no third factor and no missing owner: the extra returns to scale are simply un-priced.
Shares: $r_tK_t/Y_t=\alpha$, $w_tL_t/Y_t=1-\alpha$, summing to 1 because the firm-level function is CRS. The aggregate IRS is the unpaid externality $\varphi(1-\alpha)$.

What each letter means. $\alpha$ is the elasticity of a single firm's output to its own capital, holding technology fixed — and, under competition, also its capital income share. $\varphi$ is the elasticity of economy-wide productivity to economy-wide capital — the spillover. A common quiz trap is to read the aggregate exponent $\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)$ as “capital's share”; it is the social output elasticity, not anyone's income.

Visualize

For $K>1$ the social curve (exponent $\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)\approx 0.73$) is steeper than the private curve (exponent $\alpha=0.33$); the steeper the curve, the larger the marginal product it represents. The firm is paid the slope of the private curve, $r_t=\alpha Y/K$. The slope of the social curve is the return society actually earns on the marginal unit of capital. It is the slope gap, not the level gap, that matters for incentives: investment decisions respond to the marginal return, and the private marginal return understates the social one by the factor below. The shaded vertical band is the cumulative output the spillover adds; the wedge in slopes is what a corrective subsidy must target.

Counterfactual — the planner

Step 1 A planner who internalizes $A_t=K_t^{\varphi}$ differentiates the aggregate function directly: social MPK $=\dfrac{d}{dK}K^{\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)}L^{1-\alpha}=\big(\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)\big)\dfrac{Y}{K}$.
Step 2 Compare to the competitive return $r=\alpha Y/K$: their ratio is $\dfrac{\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)}{\alpha}>1$. The planner values the marginal unit of capital by exactly this factor more than the market does.

Because the private return falls short of the social return, decentralized agents stop accumulating too early: the economy holds too little capital relative to the planner's optimum. A corrective investment (or capital) subsidy of rate $\tau$ that raises the private return to $\alpha(1+\tau)Y/K$ up to the social $\big(\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)\big)Y/K$ closes the wedge; that requires $1+\tau=\big(\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)\big)/\alpha$, i.e. $\tau=\varphi(1-\alpha)/\alpha$. The subsidy is larger the stronger the spillover $\varphi$.

Methodology reflection

The load-bearing choice is that the firm is atomistic and takes $A_t$ as given: the spillover is external to the firm's optimization. That single assumption is what keeps the firm's paid function CRS and the shares summing to one. If instead each firm had to pay for the technology it uses — if $A$ were a private, excludable input — then the aggregate IRS would show up inside the firm's problem, the paid function would be homogeneous of degree $1+\varphi(1-\alpha)>1$, and competitive marginal-product payments would exceed output. Something must give: either the firm cannot price-take (it must hold market power to earn the extra return), or it operates at a loss. This is exactly the fork Chapter 9 walks into — deliberately produced, paid-for technology (ideas) cannot be sold at marginal cost under perfect competition, so the R&D model is forced toward monopoly and patents. The externality model dodges the problem only because its spillover is unintentional and free.

Numbers to anchor it

Social vs private return With $\alpha=0.33$ and $\varphi=0.6$ the aggregate capital exponent is $\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)=0.33+0.6(0.67)=0.732$. The social marginal product of capital exceeds the privately-paid return by the factor $\dfrac{0.732}{0.33}\approx 2.2$: society earns more than double the market return on the marginal machine.
Corrective subsidy The investment subsidy that closes the wedge solves $\alpha(1+\tau)=\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)$, giving $\tau=\dfrac{\varphi(1-\alpha)}{\alpha}=\dfrac{0.6(0.67)}{0.33}\approx 1.22$ — a 122% subsidy. Even a "moderate" spillover demands a very large intervention, a measure of how far private incentives fall short of the social optimum.

The exact trap in quiz Q4 and Q15

Q4 (what $\varphi$ is). $\varphi$ is the elasticity of the spillover $A_t=K_t^{\varphi}$ with respect to aggregate capital — the strength of the externality. It is not a second slice of capital's private exponent; reading it as "extra $\alpha$" puts it on the wrong side of the private/social divide.

Q15 (the income share). Under aggregate IRS it is tempting to call capital's income share the social exponent $\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)\approx 0.73$. It is not. Competitive firms pay the private marginal product, so the capital share is $\alpha=0.33$; the remaining $\approx 0.40$ of social output elasticity is the unpriced externality, not anybody's income. The gap between $0.33$ (paid) and $0.73$ (social) is the chapter.

The micro-story and the one-line version

The motivating mechanism is learning-by-doing: workers grow more skilled as they operate new capital, and they carry those skills with them to other firms and other jobs. The benefit leaks out beyond the firm that bought the machine, which is why it appears at the aggregate level (in $A_t=K_t^{\varphi}$) but is invisible to, and unpaid by, the individual employer. One-line version: firm-level constant returns make the books balance ($\alpha+(1-\alpha)=1$); aggregate increasing returns make society richer than the books show — the difference is a return to capital everyone enjoys and no one charges for, the signature of an externality and the reason the decentralized economy under-invests.

Exercise 2Diminishing returns and the geometry of perpetual growth
Scope: Lecture 4a §9 (the role of φ; three regimes), §20–23 (AK model; no convergence; scale effect); Slides_Session4 p16 (diminishing returns ↔ convergence speed) and p31–33 (φ=1). Quiz-targeted: Q5 (AK structural properties). Uses a two-method (analytical + graphical) derivation.
Main proof (analytical)

Work in per-worker terms with population held constant, for transparency (the logic is unchanged with $n>0$ — just replace $\delta$ by $n+\delta$). Capital per worker accumulates as $k_{t+1}=s\,y_t+(1-\delta)k_t$ with $y_t=B\,k_t^{a}$, where $a\equiv\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)$ is the aggregate elasticity of output to capital. Define the capital growth rate $g_t^{k}\equiv k_{t+1}/k_t-1$.

  1. Show $g_t^{k}=sB\,k_t^{a-1}-\delta$.
  2. A steady state in the level of $k$ needs $g_t^{k}=0$ at some finite $k^*>0$. Solve $sB\,k^{a-1}=\delta$ and show a positive finite $k^*$ exists iff $a<1$.
  3. Take $a\to 1$ (i.e. $\varphi\to 1$): show $k^*\to\infty$, and that at $a=1$ exactly the equation loses its $k$ entirely, becoming $sB=\delta$. Interpret: for $a=1$ the growth rate is the constant $g^{k}=sB-\delta$, independent of $k$ — capital never stops growing. Connect $sB$ to the lecture's AK result $g_e=sA-\delta$.
Hint 1. Divide the accumulation equation by $k_t$: $k_{t+1}/k_t = sB\,k_t^{a-1}+(1-\delta)$, so $g_t^k = sB\,k_t^{a-1}-\delta$. The term $sB\,k_t^{a-1}$ is the saving rate times the average product of capital $y/k=Bk^{a-1}$.
Hint 2. Solve $sB\,k^{a-1}=\delta$: $k^{a-1}=\delta/(sB)$, so $k^*=\big(\delta/(sB)\big)^{1/(a-1)}$. For $a<1$ the exponent $a-1<0$, so $k^{a-1}$ runs monotonically from $+\infty$ (at $k\to 0$) down to $0$ (at $k\to\infty$) and hits the positive constant $\delta/(sB)$ exactly once.
Hint 3. At $a=1$: $k^{a-1}=k^0=1$ for all $k$, so $sB\,k^{a-1}=sB$ is constant. The equation $sB=\delta$ contains no $k$ — there is no level that stops growth. Instead $g^k=sB-\delta$ for all $k$; with the lecture's $Y=AK$ (so $B\equiv A=L^{1-\alpha}$) this is exactly $g_e=sA-\delta$.

Methodology reflection

$a=1$ is a single point on a continuum of $\varphi$ values. State the “knife-edge” objection to truly-endogenous (AK) growth in your own words, then give Cozzi's two-part rebuttal: one part about $\varphi=1$ as an approximation of $\varphi$ just below 1, and one part about what the data say about the $\varphi>1$ (accelerating-growth) case. Solve on paper first.

Main proof (analytical)

Step 1 Start from $k_{t+1}=sB\,k_t^{a}+(1-\delta)k_t$. Divide by $k_t$: $\dfrac{k_{t+1}}{k_t}=sB\,k_t^{a-1}+(1-\delta)$. Subtract 1: $g_t^k=\dfrac{k_{t+1}}{k_t}-1=sB\,k_t^{a-1}-\delta$.
Capital growth rate: $g_t^k=sB\,k_t^{a-1}-\delta$. The driving term is saving times the average product of capital, $sB\,k^{a-1}=s\,(y/k)$.
Step 2 A level steady state needs $g_t^k=0$: $sB\,k^{a-1}=\delta$, i.e. $k^{a-1}=\dfrac{\delta}{sB}$, hence $k^*=\Big(\dfrac{\delta}{sB}\Big)^{\frac{1}{a-1}}$.
Step 3 Existence. For $a<1$ the exponent $a-1<0$, so the map $k\mapsto k^{a-1}$ is continuous and strictly decreasing from $+\infty$ (as $k\to 0^+$) to $0$ (as $k\to\infty$). It therefore hits the positive constant $\delta/(sB)$ at exactly one $k^*>0$. For $a>1$ the map is increasing from $0$ to $\infty$ and also crosses once, but there the steady state is unstable and growth accelerates — the case the lecture brackets out. For $a=1$, see Step 4.
Step 4 The limit. As $a\to 1^-$, the exponent $\tfrac{1}{a-1}\to-\infty$. Since $\delta/(sB)<1$ under the usual condition $sB>\delta$, a number below 1 raised to a large negative power diverges: $k^*\to+\infty$. At $a=1$ exactly, $k^{a-1}=k^0=1$, so the steady-state equation collapses to $sB\cdot 1=\delta$, i.e. $sB=\delta$ — an equation with no $k$ in it. There is no capital level that halts growth.
Step 5 Instead, at $a=1$, $g_t^k=sB\,k^{0}-\delta=sB-\delta$ for every $k$: a constant. With the lecture's normalization $Y=AK$ — where $\varphi=1$ makes $A_t=K_t$ and the aggregate function becomes $Y=K\,(L^{1-\alpha})\equiv AK$ with $A=L^{1-\alpha}$ — the constant $B$ is precisely $A$, and we recover $g_e=sA-\delta$.
Knife-edge: a finite $k^*$ exists iff $a<1$. At $a=1$ diminishing returns to capital vanish, $k^*$ is gone, and growth is the constant $g_e=sA-\delta>0$ (assuming $sA>\delta$).

Graphical method

The crossings. The downward curves $sB\,k^{a-1}$ cut the flat break-even line $n+\delta$ once each. The Solow curve ($a=\alpha\approx0.33$) is steep and crosses early, at a small $k^*$. The semi-endogenous curve ($a\approx0.73$) is much flatter — its exponent $a-1\approx-0.27$ is close to zero, so the average product of capital falls only slowly — and it meets the break-even line far to the right, at a large $k^*$. The vertical gap between curve and line is the growth rate $g_t^k$; a flatter curve means the gap shrinks gradually as $k$ rises, so the economy creeps toward its (distant) steady state. That is slow convergence: stronger spillovers ($\varphi\uparrow$) flatten the curve, push $k^*$ out, and stretch the transition.

The AK line. At $a=1$ the savings term $sB\,k^{0}=sA$ no longer depends on $k$: a horizontal line. The figure places it above the break-even line, which requires $sA>n+\delta$ (with $n=0$, $sA>\delta$). Two horizontal lines never meet, so there is no $k^*$: the growth gap $sA-(n+\delta)$ is the same at every $k$, capital grows at that constant rate forever, and there is nothing for the economy to converge to. Geometry and algebra agree.

Counterfactual — $\varphi=0.99$

A finite $k^*$ still exists ($a=\alpha+0.99(1-\alpha)<1$), but with $a-1\approx-0.0067$ the steady state $k^*=(\delta/sB)^{1/(a-1)}$ is astronomically large. On the diagram the savings curve is almost flat — nearly indistinguishable from the AK horizontal over any realistic range of $k$. So the economy behaves like AK for decades or centuries: the growth rate decays toward zero so slowly that it is empirically a constant. The difference from true AK is only visible in the infinite limit, where the $\varphi=0.99$ economy eventually stalls at $k^*$ while the $\varphi=1$ economy never does. This is exactly why Cozzi treats $\varphi=1$ as a tractable stand-in for “$\varphi$ close to but below 1”.

Methodology reflection

The knife-edge objection: truly-endogenous growth needs $a=1$ exactly, and $a=1$ is a measure-zero point — any tremble away from it ($\varphi$ slightly below 1) destroys perpetual growth in the limit, so the result looks fragile and non-generic. Cozzi's rebuttal has two prongs. First, $\varphi=1$ is best read as an approximation: a $\varphi$ just below 1 behaves like AK for all practically relevant horizons (the counterfactual above), so the knife-edge is not a real-world fragility, only a mathematical one. Second, the alternative direction, $\varphi>1$, predicts accelerating growth — returns to capital and growth rates rising as a country gets richer — which the data flatly reject; so we are entitled to bracket attention to $\varphi\le 1$. I find the approximation argument the stronger of the two: it converts a sharp philosophical worry (“measure zero!”) into a quantitative question (“how slow is convergence?”), which is the question the empirics in the next exercise actually engage.

Putting numbers on "far to the right"

Two steady states Use the figure's calibration: $sB=0.2$, break-even $n+\delta=0.11$, so $k^*=\big((n+\delta)/sB\big)^{1/(a-1)}=0.55^{\,1/(a-1)}$. For Solow ($a=\alpha=0.33$, $a-1=-0.67$): $k^*=0.55^{-1.49}\approx 2.4$. For semi-endogenous ($a=0.732$, $a-1=-0.268$): $k^*=0.55^{-3.73}\approx 9.3$.

The semi-endogenous steady state sits nearly four times further out than the Solow one, from the same saving and depreciation rates — purely because the stronger spillover ($\varphi=0.6$) flattens the savings curve. A flatter curve means the growth gap above break-even closes only sluggishly, so the transition to $k^*$ takes far longer. "Slow convergence" is not a separate assumption; it is what a flat $sB\,k^{a-1}$ curve looks like.

The four AK properties (quiz Q5), read straight off the flat line

(1) No transitional dynamics. $g^k=sB\,k^{0}-\delta=sA-\delta$ does not depend on $k$, so there is nothing to "adjust toward": the economy is on its balanced path from $t=0$. (2) Constant growth. The vertical gap between the flat savings line and the break-even line is the same at every $k$, so $g_e=sA-\delta$ is constant. (3) No convergence. With no $k^*$, two economies starting at different $k_0$ both grow at $g_e$ forever; their ratio never closes — poor and rich do not converge. (4) Scale effect. Since $A=L^{1-\alpha}$, the height of the savings line $sA=sL^{1-\alpha}$ rises with population, so $g_e=sL^{1-\alpha}-\delta$ increases with $L$.

Each property the quiz tested is a feature of the same picture — a flat line sitting above the break-even line. The miss on Q5 (which features hold) dissolves once the geometry, not a memorized list, is the source of truth.

What changes with $n>0$?

Nothing qualitative. Reinstating population growth simply replaces $\delta$ by $n+\delta$: the break-even line sits higher, every $k^*$ moves accordingly, and the AK condition becomes $sA>n+\delta$. The existence-iff-$a<1$ result, the divergence at $a=1$, and the slow-convergence reading are all untouched.

Is the steady state stable?

For $a<1$, yes — and the same geometry shows it. To the left of $k^*$ the savings curve $sB\,k^{a-1}$ lies above the break-even line, so $g_t^k>0$ and $k$ rises toward $k^*$; to the right it lies below, so $g_t^k<0$ and $k$ falls back. The crossing is therefore globally attracting, and the convergence speed is governed by how steeply the savings curve cuts the line: a flat cut (large $\varphi$, $a$ near 1) means a weak restoring force and slow return, a steep cut (small $a$) means fast return. This is precisely why "stronger spillover" and "slower convergence" are the same statement read twice. At $a=1$ the restoring force vanishes entirely — the savings curve never cuts the line — so there is no point to be stable around, and the economy simply rides the constant gap upward forever. The knife-edge is the moment a stable steady state stops existing, not merely a point where convergence happens to be slow.

Connecting to the convergence-speed formula (Modules 1 and 3)

The same log-linearization you used for basic Solow gives a local convergence speed $\lambda_{\text{conv}}\approx(1-a)(n+\delta)$, with the aggregate exponent $a=\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)$ now playing the role that $\alpha$ (or $\alpha+\phi$ in MRW) played there. The reading is immediate: $\lambda_{\text{conv}}$ is proportional to $1-a$, the very thing that shrinks as the spillover strengthens. As $\varphi\to 1$, $a\to 1$, and $\lambda_{\text{conv}}\to 0$: convergence speed literally goes to zero at the knife-edge. So the "flat savings curve" of the diagram and the "small $\lambda_{\text{conv}}$" of the algebra are the same fact in two languages — and it is continuous with the convergence speed you already estimated for the Solow and MRW models, just with broad capital's exponent pushed toward one.

Anchor

$\varphi$ is the elasticity of new-idea production to the existing stock of knowledge. If $\varphi>0$, past discoveries make present research more productive — Newton's “standing on the shoulders of giants”; Romer's baseline takes $\varphi=1$ (knowledge is as productive in making ideas as a constant-returns input). If $\varphi<0$, the best ideas are found first and later ones are harder — “fishing out.” $\lambda$ is the elasticity to research labour. $\lambda=1$ means a researcher's contribution is independent of how many others are working; $\lambda<1$ means “stepping on toes” — duplication and congestion, so doubling the research force less than doubles output.

Main argument

Step 1 — non-rivalry kills competitive R&D An idea, once created, can be used by any number of firms simultaneously at no extra cost: its marginal cost of additional use is zero. Under perfect competition price equals marginal cost, so the idea's price is driven to zero and the inventor earns zero revenue from licensing it. But inventing it cost something positive (researchers' wages). A rational price-taking firm therefore never invents: it would sink a positive cost to earn zero. Private, competitive production of ideas collapses.
Step 2 — patents restore incentives at a price A patent makes the idea excludable and grants its holder temporary monopoly. The inventor can now charge a price above marginal cost and earn a stream of monopoly profits that can cover the fixed cost of invention. So private R&D becomes viable — but only by introducing monopoly. And monopoly pricing (price $>$ MC $=0$) means some users who value the idea at more than its zero marginal cost are priced out: the idea is used less than is socially optimal. The deadweight loss is the unavoidable cost of using the profit motive as the engine of progress.
Step 3 — why Chapter 8 escapes this In Chapter 8 the productivity spillover is an unintentional by-product. Firms accumulate capital for their own private return $r=\alpha Y/K$ (Exercise 1); the economy-wide rise in $A_t=K_t^{\varphi}$ happens for free, as a side-effect, whether or not anyone is compensated for it. Because nobody has to be paid to generate the externality, perfect competition is fine — the spillover does not need a revenue stream to exist. Chapter 9 ideas are different in kind: they are produced on purpose, at real resource cost, so they must generate revenue to be produced at all — and non-rivalry blocks that under competition. The deliberate-vs-incidental distinction is the whole difference.
Core asymmetry: unintentional spillovers (Ch 8) coexist with perfect competition; intentional, costly, non-rival ideas (Ch 9) require monopoly + patents, and hence a socially suboptimal level of idea use.

Counterfactual — $\lambda<1$ duplication

Step 1 Replace $L_A$ by $2L_A$: $\rho A^{\varphi}(2L_A)^{\lambda}=2^{\lambda}\,\rho A^{\varphi}L_A^{\lambda}$. Since $\lambda<1$, $2^{\lambda}<2$: doubling the research force multiplies new ideas by a factor below two. Equivalently, output per researcher $\Delta A/L_A=\rho A^{\varphi}L_A^{\lambda-1}$ is decreasing in $L_A$ (exponent $\lambda-1<0$). Researchers crowd each other — some rediscover what others are simultaneously finding.

Step 2 — direction of the distortion. Each research firm hires labour up to the point where its private marginal product of a researcher equals the wage, taking the aggregate research labour (and hence the congestion level) as given. It does not see that adding its own researcher lowers everyone else's productivity. That ignored effect is a negative externality, so the private marginal product overstates the social one: at the margin the decentralized economy puts too many workers into research relative to a congestion-internalizing optimum. (This pushes opposite to the positive standing-on-shoulders externality in $\varphi$, which on its own would justify more research; the net direction depends on which externality dominates — the reason the social planner's research subsidy in these models is theoretically ambiguous in sign.)

Two properties, not one: rivalry vs excludability

Ideas sit at the crossing of two independent attributes. Rivalry asks whether one agent's use uses the good up; excludability asks whether the owner can keep others out. A loaf of bread is rival and excludable (a private good); national defence is non-rival and non-excludable (a pure public good). An idea is non-rival but only partially excludable. Cozzi's two-clause summary is the key: non-rivalry prevents private production under perfect competition (price collapses to the zero marginal cost of an extra user), while complete non-excludability would prevent private production under any market structure (you cannot charge at all). A patent attacks the second problem — it manufactures excludability — which is the only reason the first problem (zero competitive price) can be escaped.

No free lunch: the public-production alternative

If monopoly deadweight loss is the cost of private R&D, why not have the state produce ideas with open access (price 0, socially efficient use)? Because the development cost must then be financed by distortionary taxes, and the profit motive — a powerful engine of discovery — is switched off. So society faces a genuine trilemma with no clean corner: monopoly deadweight loss (private + patents), tax distortion (public), or weak incentives (open non-profit). Chapter 9 deliberately keeps the profit motive and lives with the monopoly distortion.

Concrete cases

Calculus is the textbook non-rival good: every student and engineer uses it simultaneously, and your using it subtracts nothing from mine. A pharmaceutical molecule shows the funding problem starkly — a billion-dollar fixed R&D cost, then near-zero marginal cost to manufacture each pill; under marginal-cost pricing the developer never recoups the billion, so the patent lets price sit far above marginal cost (used "too little" while patented) until it expires and generics drive price toward marginal cost. The patent is the temporary monopoly that funds the invention, deadweight loss and all.

Why the growth rate is suboptimal in both directions

Excludability is imperfect, not absolute — patents expire, knowledge leaks, competitors reverse-engineer — so the inventor captures only a fraction of the social value an idea creates. That cuts two ways at once. From the creation side, the standing-on-shoulders externality ($\varphi>0$: today's ideas raise tomorrow's research productivity for everyone) is not internalized, so the decentralized economy invents too little. From the diffusion side, monopoly pricing on existing ideas (price $>$ MC $=0$) means they are used too little once invented. Two distortions pull in opposite directions: insufficient idea creation versus insufficient idea use. This is exactly why the socially optimal R&D subsidy is ambiguous in sign in general — layered on top of the $\lambda<1$ congestion externality of the counterfactual, the policy problem is genuinely a balance of three forces, not a one-line "subsidize research." Naming all three is what a deep answer does.

Reflection — the scale effect's two failures

The US time series is the cleanest refutation because it controls for everything that varies across countries. Hold one country fixed and let its population multiply several-fold over a century: the raw scale-effect model ($g_e\propto L^{1-\alpha}$ or $\propto L^{\lambda}$) predicts the growth rate should have risen by a large, computable factor. Instead measured US per-capita growth was close to a constant ~2% across the century. A prediction of “growth accelerates as you get bigger” meeting a century of “growth stayed flat” is as direct a falsification as macro offers.

It is essential not to conflate this with Panel A. Panel A is about $n$, the growth rate of population, and rejects the semi-endogenous prediction $g_{se}\uparrow n$. The US time series is about $L$, the level of population, and rejects the strictly-endogenous scale effect $g_e\uparrow L$. Two different models, two different right-hand-side variables, two different pieces of evidence — both pointing the wrong way for the raw theory. A common exam error is to cite the $g$–$n$ scatter as if it disproved the scale effect; it does not, it disproves the $n$-prediction.

Cross-module bridge → Module 5

Module 5's dilution assumption says that as population grows the economy spreads across proportionally more product lines, so research effort per line depends on the share $s_R$ of labour in R&D, not on the absolute headcount $L$. Replace $L$ by $s_R$ as the growth driver and the troublesome $g_e\propto L^{\lambda}$ becomes $g_e\propto\rho\,s_R^{\lambda}$: a positive endogenous growth rate that policy can move (via $s_R$) but that no longer depends on country size. The scale effect vanishes while endogenous growth survives. It also dissolves the “country or world?” worry: because absolute size dropped out, the model no longer predicts that big countries outgrow small ones, so it can sensibly be read country-by-country rather than only as a single-world aggregate.

Putting numbers on the US scale-effect failure

The predicted acceleration US population grew from about 76 million in 1900 to about 281 million in 2000 — a factor of $\approx 3.7$. The strictly-endogenous form $g_e\propto L^{1-\alpha}$ with $1-\alpha\approx 0.67$ predicts the growth rate should have risen by $3.7^{0.67}\approx 2.4$. So the raw scale-effect model says 20th-century US growth should have more than doubled; instead it hovered near 2% a year. A predicted $2.4\times$ acceleration meeting a flat outturn is a clean, quantitative falsification.

Why we should not over-reject (Cozzi's caveats)

Cozzi is careful not to throw the model out on the cross-country scatter alone. Three caveats blunt Panel A: (i) direction of causality — rich, fast-growing countries choose low fertility, so causation may run growth $\to n$, not $n\to$ growth; (ii) country vs world — the model may be meant for the world as one closed economy, not as a country-by-country prediction; (iii) slow convergence — if $\varphi$ is near 1 the observed pattern can be transitory dynamics, so we might "reject that the countries are in steady state, not the model itself." There is even a global-long-run defence: over 200 years the world has had both non-trivial population growth and non-trivial per-capita growth, loosely consistent with semi-endogenous growth if convergence is very slow.

Why Panel B is the decisive one

Panel B isolates the growth rate of technology $g_A$ (via growth accounting), not just capital deepening, and shows it co-moving with the investment rate. An exogenous-growth model has $g_A$ as a constant of nature: it structurally cannot produce a non-zero slope here, no matter how the transition is told. That is why a positive slope — the Bernanke–Gürkaynak-style finding — is the single cleanest piece of evidence that the technology growth rate is itself endogenous to how much an economy invests. The two panels together say: reject the raw $n$- and $L$-predictions, but keep the core endogenous idea that policy can move the growth rate.

Hence $\partial g_{se}/\partial s=0$: raising $s$ from 0.15 to 0.25 leaves the long-run growth rate exactly where it was.
Step 2 — but the level rises The per-effective-worker steady state solves $s\,\tilde k^{*a}=(\text{effective break-even})\,\tilde k^*$, giving $\tilde k^*\propto s^{1/(1-a)}$ with $a=\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)<1$, so $1/(1-a)>0$ and $\tilde k^*$ is increasing in $s$. Then $\tilde y^*=\tilde k^{*a}$ rises too. Since output per worker is $y_t=\tilde y_t A_t$ and $A_t$ keeps growing at the unchanged rate $g_{se}$, the whole $\log y$ path lifts to a higher parallel track. A permanent level effect, zero growth effect.
$\varphi<1$: policy shifts the intercept of $\log y(t)$ up; the slope $g_{se}$ is fixed by $n$ and $\varphi$.
Step 3 — AK: the rate itself moves With $\varphi=1$, $n=0$, the growth rate is $g_e=sA-\delta$. Differentiate: $\partial g_e/\partial s=A>0$. Raising $s$ from 0.15 to 0.25 raises $g_e$ permanently by $A\cdot 0.10$. A permanent growth effect: the slope of $\log y(t)$ increases.
$\varphi=1$: policy raises the slope of $\log y(t)$ forever — the paths diverge.

General principle. When capital (broadly, the accumulable factor) faces diminishing returns ($a<1$, i.e. $\varphi<1$), policy can only relocate the economy to a higher level path; the long-run slope is pinned by population growth and the spillover. When diminishing returns just vanish ($a=1$, $\varphi=1$), the same policy bends the slope permanently. The single parameter $\varphi$ — through whether $a$ is below or equal to 1 — decides which. The knife-edge of Exercise 2 is also the knife-edge of policy.

Visualize

The grey dashed line is the baseline. The teal curve is semi-endogenous: it tracks the baseline until $t=10$, rises above it through a transition, then settles onto a higher line parallel to the baseline — the level effect. The rust curve is truly endogenous: from $t=10$ it takes a permanently steeper slope and pulls away from the baseline without bound — the growth effect. Same policy, two qualitatively different geometries.

The transitory burst. Right after the saving rate jumps, the semi-endogenous economy finds itself below its new, higher steady state $\tilde k^*$. In that region investment exceeds break-even, so capital per effective worker climbs and output per worker grows faster than its long-run rate $g_{se}$. As $\tilde k_t$ approaches the new $\tilde k^*$ the gap closes and growth eases back to $g_{se}$. The burst is exactly the ordinary Solow transition — finite, front-loaded, and self-extinguishing — which is why it raises the level permanently but not the slope. (The figure renders this as the temporary steepening of the teal curve between $t=10$ and roughly $t=30$.)

Numeric inversion

Country 1 Long-run growth 2.0% before and after, but a permanent ~18% level gap. Growth rate untouched, level permanently shifted ⇒ a level effect ⇒ semi-endogenous ($\varphi<1$). Policy moved the intercept, not the slope.
Country 2 Long-run growth rose 2.0%→2.6% and the path keeps diverging ⇒ a growth effect ⇒ truly endogenous ($\varphi=1$). Policy moved the slope.

Why the growth number alone is not enough for Country 1. “2.0% after” is consistent with two different worlds: one where the policy did nothing, and one where it produced a pure level effect (same eventual slope, higher track). What distinguishes them is not the post-policy growth rate but the permanent level gap relative to the extrapolated old path. You must look at the intercept, not the slope, to detect a level effect — which is precisely why the visualization, not a single growth statistic, is the diagnostic.

Methodology reflection

The level-vs-growth distinction is the entire policy stake because only truly-endogenous growth promises a policymaker a permanent rise in the growth rate — a lever on the slope of $\log y$, which compounds without limit. Semi-endogenous growth (and exogenous Solow) offer only level effects: worth having, but one-off — the economy ends up richer, not permanently faster. That is a categorical difference in what economic policy can achieve. The catch is that the permanent-growth promise lives entirely on the $\varphi=1$ knife-edge of Exercise 2: a spillover even slightly weaker than $\varphi=1$ collapses the growth effect back into a (very slow) level effect. So the most exciting policy claim in growth theory rests on the least robust assumption in it — which is exactly the tension Cozzi wants a 6/6 answer to name, not hide.

Putting numbers on the growth effect

AK calibration Take Bolboaca's $\varphi=1$ economy: $\alpha=0.33$, $n=0$, $\delta=0.1$, $L_0=1$, so $A=L^{1-\alpha}=1$ and $g_e=sA-\delta=s-0.1$. At $s=0.15$, $g_e=0.05$; at $s=0.25$, $g_e=0.15$. The saving-rate rise triples the long-run growth rate, from 5% to 15% a year — permanently.

Run the identical $s:0.15\to 0.25$ change in the $\varphi=0.6$ economy and $g_{se}=(1+n)^{\varphi/(1-\varphi)}-1$ does not move at all; only $\tilde k^*$ and the level path rise. One experiment, two universes — and the only thing that changed between them is $\varphi$.

Why this is the exam's favourite pairing

"Derive $X$ and discuss the policy implications" is the recurring Module-4/5 template, and it rewards exactly this two-step: show mathematically that $g_{se}$ has no $s$ (or that $\partial g_e/\partial s=A>0$), then translate it for a finance minister — level effects are worth having but one-off, growth effects compound forever. The 4/6 answer writes the algebra and stops; the 6/6 answer states the level-vs-growth dichotomy out loud and flags that the permanent-growth promise rides on the fragile $\varphi=1$ knife-edge. Naming the fragility is the differentiator, because honest economics is what the intuition mark rewards.
