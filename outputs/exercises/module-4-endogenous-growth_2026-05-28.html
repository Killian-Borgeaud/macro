Scope: Lecture 4a §4 (firm-level PF and equilibrium), §5 (aggregate PF and IRS), §6 (factor prices from firm-level Euler), §7 (motivation: learning-by-doing)
Anchor

The Chapter 8 externality model has one representative firm. At the firm level, the production function is $$Y_t^d = (K_t^d)^\alpha (A_t L_t^d)^{1-\alpha},$$ where the firm takes $A_t$ as given. Productive externalities tie $A_t$ to the aggregate capital stock: $$A_t = K_t^\varphi, \quad \varphi > 0.$$ In two sentences, state (i) what 'CRS to $(K_t^d, L_t^d)$' means at the firm level, and (ii) why this CRS holds despite the externality.

Hint 1. CRS to $(K^d, L^d)$ at the firm level means doubling both capital and labour while holding $A_t$ fixed doubles output.
Hint 2. The firm is a price-taker in $A_t$: from its planning desk, $A_t$ is exogenous because the firm is too small to move aggregate $K_t$. So firm-level CRS is CRS in the rival inputs only — the externality is an aggregate phenomenon, not a firm-level property.
Main proof: derive the aggregate PF and IRS

Derive the aggregate production function by (a) imposing the equilibrium conditions $K_t^d = K_t$ and $L_t^d = L_t$, (b) substituting the externality $A_t = K_t^\varphi$, and (c) collecting exponents. Show every algebraic step. Then sum the exponents on $K_t$ and $L_t$ and conclude that the aggregate PF exhibits increasing returns to scale (IRS) when $\varphi > 0$.

Hint 1. Start with the firm-level PF $Y_t^d = (K_t^d)^\alpha (A_t L_t^d)^{1-\alpha}$ and substitute the equilibrium conditions to get $Y_t = K_t^\alpha (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}$.
Hint 2. Now substitute $A_t = K_t^\varphi$. You will get $Y_t = K_t^\alpha (K_t^\varphi L_t)^{1-\alpha}$. Use $(xy)^n = x^n y^n$ and $(x^a)^b = x^{ab}$ to expand.
Hint 3. Collect the $K_t$ exponents using $x^a x^b = x^{a+b}$. You should arrive at $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)} L_t^{1-\alpha}$.
Hint 4. Sum exponents: $\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha) + (1-\alpha) = 1 + \varphi(1-\alpha)$. With $\varphi > 0$ and $\alpha < 1$, the sum is strictly greater than one — IRS.

Anchor

(i) What CRS to $(K_t^d, L_t^d)$ means. The firm-level production function is $Y_t^d = (K_t^d)^\alpha (A_t L_t^d)^{1-\alpha}$. CRS in the rival inputs $(K_t^d, L_t^d)$ means: doubling both inputs while holding $A_t$ fixed exactly doubles output. Algebraically, replacing $K_t^d \to 2 K_t^d$ and $L_t^d \to 2 L_t^d$:

$$Y'^d = (2 K_t^d)^\alpha (A_t \cdot 2 L_t^d)^{1-\alpha} = 2^\alpha \cdot 2^{1-\alpha} \cdot Y_t^d = 2^{\alpha + (1-\alpha)} Y_t^d = 2 Y_t^d.$$

(ii) Why CRS holds despite the externality. The firm is a price-taker in aggregate $A_t$: from its planning perspective, $A_t$ is exogenous data because a single small firm cannot move the economy-wide capital stock that drives $A_t$. The replication argument — duplicate the plant, the workforce, and you duplicate the output — works perfectly with $A_t$ frozen. The productive externality $A_t = K_t^\varphi$ is a property of the aggregate equilibrium, not of the firm's technology.

Main proof: aggregate PF and IRS

Step 1 Start with the firm-level PF and impose equilibrium $K_t^d = K_t$, $L_t^d = L_t$. There is a single representative firm, so its choices coincide with aggregates:
$$Y_t = K_t^\alpha (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}.$$
Step 2 Substitute the externality $A_t = K_t^\varphi$:
$$Y_t = K_t^\alpha (K_t^\varphi L_t)^{1-\alpha}.$$
Step 3 Expand $(K_t^\varphi L_t)^{1-\alpha}$ using $(xy)^n = x^n y^n$ and $(x^a)^b = x^{ab}$:
$$(K_t^\varphi L_t)^{1-\alpha} = (K_t^\varphi)^{1-\alpha} L_t^{1-\alpha} = K_t^{\varphi(1-\alpha)} L_t^{1-\alpha}.$$
Step 4 Substitute back into $Y_t$ and collect the $K_t$ exponents via $x^a x^b = x^{a+b}$:
$$Y_t = K_t^\alpha \cdot K_t^{\varphi(1-\alpha)} \cdot L_t^{1-\alpha} = K_t^{\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)} L_t^{1-\alpha}.$$
$$Y_t = K_t^{\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)} L_t^{1-\alpha}.$$
Step 5 Sum the two exponents. The $K_t$ exponent is $\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)$; the $L_t$ exponent is $1-\alpha$. Adding:
$$\big[\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)\big] + (1-\alpha) = \alpha + 1 - \alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha) = 1 + \varphi(1-\alpha).$$

Conclusion: IRS at the aggregate. With $\varphi > 0$ and $0 < \alpha < 1$, the sum $1 + \varphi(1-\alpha) > 1$. Doubling $K_t$ and $L_t$ together multiplies aggregate output by $2^{1+\varphi(1-\alpha)}$, which exceeds $2$.

Where the IRS came from. At the firm level, doubling $K_t^d$ and $L_t^d$ with $A_t$ fixed gave exactly $2 Y_t^d$ (CRS, Anchor step). At the aggregate, however, doubling $K_t$ also doubles $A_t^{1/\varphi}$, which means $A_t$ rises by the factor $2^\varphi$. This second productivity boost is invisible to a single firm but appears in the aggregate via the substitution $A_t = K_t^\varphi$. Aggregate IRS equals firm-level CRS plus the productivity bonus the firm does not see.

Methodology reflection: capital share is $\alpha$, not $\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)$

Step 1 The firm's first-order condition for capital rental is derived from the firm-level PF — the only one the firm sees — treating $A_t$ as a fixed constant:
$$r_t = \frac{\partial Y_t^d}{\partial K_t^d}\bigg|_{A_t \text{ fixed}} = \alpha (K_t^d)^{\alpha - 1} (A_t L_t^d)^{1-\alpha}.$$
Step 2 Multiply and divide by $K_t^d$ to express in terms of output:
$$r_t = \alpha \cdot \frac{(K_t^d)^\alpha (A_t L_t^d)^{1-\alpha}}{K_t^d} = \alpha \frac{Y_t^d}{K_t^d}.$$
Step 3 Impose equilibrium $K_t^d = K_t$, $Y_t^d = Y_t$:
$$r_t K_t = \alpha Y_t \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \text{capital income share} = \alpha.$$
$r_t K_t = \alpha Y_t$ and $w_t L_t = (1-\alpha) Y_t$. Shares sum to one — the externality wedge $\varphi(1-\alpha) Y_t$ is unpaid social surplus.

By the same logic applied to labour, $w_t L_t = (1-\alpha) Y_t$. The two factor income shares — $\alpha$ for capital, $1-\alpha$ for labour — add to one. Yet the aggregate PF carries the larger sum-of-exponents $1 + \varphi(1-\alpha)$, which would imply an excess marginal product of $\varphi(1-\alpha) Y_t/K_t$ beyond what is paid to capital.

The economic meaning of the gap. The wedge $\varphi(1-\alpha) Y_t/K_t$ is the difference between the social marginal product of capital (the one that accounts for $A_t = K_t^\varphi$ moving with aggregate $K_t$) and the private marginal product (the one the firm sees, with $A_t$ held fixed). A social planner who internalised the externality would value an extra unit of capital at the social MPK and would therefore choose more capital than the decentralised market does. The wedge is the source of underinvestment relative to the social optimum. It does not appear in factor incomes because the firms paying for capital are not paid for the externality they create — it is the unpaid social surplus from learning-by-doing.

Counterfactual: labour-driven externality $A_t = L_t^\varphi$

Step 1 Substitute $A_t = L_t^\varphi$ into the firm-level PF and impose equilibrium $K_t^d = K_t$, $L_t^d = L_t$:
$$Y_t = K_t^\alpha (L_t^\varphi L_t)^{1-\alpha}.$$
Step 2 Combine the $L_t$ factors using $L_t^\varphi \cdot L_t = L_t^{1+\varphi}$:
$$Y_t = K_t^\alpha (L_t^{1+\varphi})^{1-\alpha} = K_t^\alpha L_t^{(1+\varphi)(1-\alpha)}.$$
Step 3 Sum the exponents:
$$\alpha + (1+\varphi)(1-\alpha) = \alpha + (1-\alpha) + \varphi(1-\alpha) = 1 + \varphi(1-\alpha).$$
Labour-driven: $Y_t = K_t^\alpha L_t^{(1+\varphi)(1-\alpha)}$. Sum of exponents $= 1 + \varphi(1-\alpha)$ — identical IRS magnitude to the capital-driven case.

What is the same. The strength of IRS, measured by sum of exponents minus 1, is $\varphi(1-\alpha)$ in both variants. The textbook's mathematical machinery — semi-endogenous growth with growth rate proportional to $n$, AK limit at $\varphi = 1$ — would carry over with the same exponents.

What is different — economically. In the capital-driven version (textbook), the engine of growth in the semi-endogenous regime is population growth $n > 0$ pulling aggregate $K_t$ upward, which then pulls $A_t = K_t^\varphi$ upward. The story is: bigger workforce uses more machines, and more machines means more learning-by-doing spillover. In the labour-driven version, the engine would run directly: $A_t = L_t^\varphi$ rises whenever $L_t$ rises, no capital channel needed. Cozzi (Lec 4a p7) motivates the textbook choice with the learning-by-doing story — workers get more skilled by using new capital. A labour-driven externality would tell a different motivation: workers get more skilled by simply having more workers around (peer-effect spillovers, for example). The two stories explain the same algebra but identify different policy levers — capital subsidies under the capital-driven version, immigration/fertility policy under the labour-driven version.

Synthesis: what this exercise teaches

The externality model architecture sits at an interesting conceptual point: it generates increasing returns at the aggregate level without violating the perfect-competition assumption at the firm. The trick — putting the IRS in the equilibrium aggregation rather than in the firm's technology — is mathematically clean and economically defensible (learning-by-doing is a real, empirically documented phenomenon). But it also reveals a tension that runs through Module 4: the productive externality is, definitionally, a market failure, yet decentralised firms still make standard profit-maximising decisions. The decentralisation works at the price level but produces a socially suboptimal aggregate outcome — the textbook signature of an externality.

Lesson 1: the capital share remains $\alpha$. Factor prices are set at the firm level, where $A_t$ is taken as given. The firm's marginal product of capital is $\alpha (K_t^d)^{\alpha-1}(A_t L_t^d)^{1-\alpha}$, which evaluated at equilibrium gives $r_t = \alpha Y_t/K_t$. The aggregate-IRS exponent $\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)$ would be the social marginal product — what a planner who internalised the externality would value — but it is not what the market pays. This consistency with the empirical observation that capital's share of national income is roughly $1/3$ in most economies (Cobb-Douglas calibration with $\alpha \approx 1/3$) is what makes the externality model defensible: it does not require us to reconcile observed factor shares with an inflated capital exponent. The externality is paid to nobody.

Lesson 2: the IRS magnitude is $\varphi(1-\alpha)$, regardless of channel. The counterfactual showed that a labour-driven externality ($A_t = L_t^\varphi$) produces the same aggregate IRS magnitude — $1 + \varphi(1-\alpha)$ — as the capital-driven version. The choice of channel matters for the economic interpretation (learning-by-doing through new capital vs peer-effect spillovers through labour density) and for the policy lever (capital subsidies vs urbanisation or immigration policy), but the mathematical machinery is identical. This robustness of architecture is what makes the externality framework portable: the same algebra describes a wide variety of empirical phenomena.

Lesson 3: the aggregate PF is the input for what comes next. The expression $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)} L_t^{1-\alpha}$ derived in the Main proof is the starting point for Exercise 2 (semi-endogenous regime, $\varphi < 1$) and Exercise 3 (AK regime, $\varphi = 1$). The three regimes by $\varphi$ are not three different models; they are three different parameter regions of the same aggregate PF. The qualitative behaviour switches at $\varphi = 1$: below, diminishing returns to capital alone (semi-endogenous); at, constant returns (truly endogenous, AK); above, accelerating returns (explosive, ruled out empirically). Holding this structural intuition in mind makes the rest of Module 4 — and the parallel R&D treatment in Lecture 4b — much easier to navigate.

Exercise 2Semi-endogenous externality model: derivation of $g_{se} = (1+n)^{\varphi/(1-\varphi)} - 1$
Scope: Lecture 4a §9 (three regimes), §10-12 (per-effective-worker reduction and transition equation), §13-15 (steady-state growth rate and policy implications)
Anchor: $\tilde y_t = \tilde k_t^\alpha$

Per-effective-worker variables are $\tilde k_t \equiv K_t/(A_t L_t)$ and $\tilde y_t \equiv Y_t/(A_t L_t)$. Starting from the aggregate PF $Y_t = K_t^\alpha (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}$ (the form before substituting the externality), show in three lines that $\tilde y_t = \tilde k_t^\alpha$. State which constants disappear and why.

Hint 1. Divide both sides of the PF by $A_t L_t$.
Hint 2. Write $Y_t/(A_t L_t) = K_t^\alpha (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}/(A_t L_t) = K_t^\alpha (A_t L_t)^{-\alpha} = (K_t/(A_t L_t))^\alpha$.
Main proof: derive $g_{se}$ from the steady-state BGP requirement

Assume $0 < \varphi < 1$ and $n > 0$. In steady state, $\tilde k_t = K_t/(A_t L_t)$ converges to a constant $\tilde k^*$. The aim is to find the steady-state growth rate of technology, $g_{se} \equiv (A_{t+1} - A_t)/A_t$ at the SS.

(a) In steady state, $\tilde k$ constant means that $K_t$, $A_t$, and $L_t$ grow such that their ratio is fixed. Express the steady-state capital growth rate, $1 + g_K^* \equiv K_{t+1}/K_t$, in terms of $1 + g_{se}$ and $1 + n$.

(b) From the externality $A_t = K_t^\varphi$, derive a second relation between $1 + g_t$ and $1 + g_K$ (valid at any date). Specialise to steady state.

(c) Combine (a) and (b) and solve for $1 + g_{se}$. Show every algebraic step.

Hint 1. (a) $\tilde k_{t+1}/\tilde k_t = (K_{t+1}/K_t) \cdot (A_t/A_{t+1}) \cdot (L_t/L_{t+1})$. Setting this to 1 at steady state: $1 + g_K^* = (1 + g_{se})(1+n)$.
Hint 2. (b) From $A_t = K_t^\varphi$: $A_{t+1}/A_t = (K_{t+1}/K_t)^\varphi$, i.e., $1 + g_t = (1 + g_K)^\varphi$. At SS: $1 + g_{se} = (1 + g_K^*)^\varphi$.
Hint 3. (c) Substitute (a) into (b): $1 + g_{se} = \big[(1 + g_{se})(1+n)\big]^\varphi$. Now isolate $1 + g_{se}$: $(1+g_{se})^{1-\varphi} = (1+n)^\varphi$. Take the $1/(1-\varphi)$ power: $1 + g_{se} = (1+n)^{\varphi/(1-\varphi)}$.
Method variation: derive $g_{se}$ directly from the aggregate PF

Now derive $g_{se}$ a second way, working directly from the aggregate PF (equilibrium form):

$$Y_t = K_t^{\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)} L_t^{1-\alpha}.$$

On a balanced growth path (BGP), $Y_t$ and $K_t$ grow at the same constant rate $g_K^*$ (because $Y/K$ is constant in steady state). Use this BGP requirement to find $g_K^*$ in terms of $n$, then convert to $g_y \equiv g_K^* - n$ (the per-worker growth rate). Verify your answer agrees with the previous derivation when you also use the externality $1 + g_{se} = (1+g_K^*)^\varphi$.

Hint 1. Take logs of the PF: $\log Y_t = [\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)] \log K_t + (1-\alpha) \log L_t$. In growth-rate form (for small rates): $g_Y = [\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)] g_K + (1-\alpha) n$.
Hint 2. On a BGP $g_Y = g_K = g_K^*$. Solve: $g_K^* \{1 - [\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)]\} = (1-\alpha) n$. Simplify $1 - \alpha - \varphi(1-\alpha) = (1-\alpha)(1-\varphi)$, so $g_K^* = n/(1-\varphi)$.
Hint 3. Convert to $g_y = g_K^* - n = n/(1-\varphi) - n = n\varphi/(1-\varphi)$. For small $n$, $g_{se} \approx g_y$ — agrees with the first derivation to first order ($\log(1+n)^{\varphi/(1-\varphi)} = \varphi/(1-\varphi) \cdot \log(1+n) \approx \varphi n/(1-\varphi)$).

In 3-4 sentences, explain why semi-endogenous growth collapses when the population stops growing, and link this back to the Ch 8 intuition that 'to utilise increasing returns, scale increases are required.' What does this imply about Cozzi's policy recommendation 'promote population growth!' Hint 1. $\lim_{n \to 0} (1+n)^{\varphi/(1-\varphi)} = 1$, so $g_{se} \to 0$. The model predicts zero long-run growth when population stops growing.
Hint 2. The intuition: increasing returns at the aggregate are useless unless something is growing to load them. The externality $A_t = K_t^\varphi$ only contributes growth if $K_t$ is growing, which in the semi-endogenous regime requires $n > 0$.
Solve on paper first.

Anchor: $\tilde y_t = \tilde k_t^\alpha$

Step 1 Divide both sides of $Y_t = K_t^\alpha (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}$ by $A_t L_t$:
$$\tilde y_t = \frac{Y_t}{A_t L_t} = \frac{K_t^\alpha (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}}{A_t L_t} = K_t^\alpha (A_t L_t)^{-\alpha}.$$
Step 2 Re-group using $x^\alpha y^{-\alpha} = (x/y)^\alpha$:
$$\tilde y_t = \left(\frac{K_t}{A_t L_t}\right)^\alpha = \tilde k_t^\alpha.$$
$$\tilde y_t = \tilde k_t^\alpha.$$

The constants that disappeared: every $A_t L_t$ factor is fully absorbed by the definition of per-effective-worker units. Note that this expression is identical in form to the basic Solow per-effective-worker PF — the externality has not yet shown up. It enters through the dynamics of $A_t$, not through $\tilde y$ as a function of $\tilde k$.

Main proof: $g_{se}$ from the BGP requirement

Step 1 (part a) At a steady state in $\tilde k$, $\tilde k_{t+1} = \tilde k_t$. Expand:
$$\frac{\tilde k_{t+1}}{\tilde k_t} = \frac{K_{t+1}/K_t}{(A_{t+1}/A_t)(L_{t+1}/L_t)} = \frac{1 + g_K^*}{(1 + g_{se})(1 + n)} = 1.$$

Rearranging:

$$1 + g_K^* = (1 + g_{se})(1 + n). \quad (\star)$$
Step 2 (part b) The externality $A_t = K_t^\varphi$ holds at every date. Take the ratio at $t+1$ over $t$:
$$\frac{A_{t+1}}{A_t} = \frac{K_{t+1}^\varphi}{K_t^\varphi} = \left(\frac{K_{t+1}}{K_t}\right)^\varphi.$$

Therefore $1 + g_t = (1 + g_K)^\varphi$ at every date. At the steady state $g_t = g_{se}$, $g_K = g_K^*$:

$$1 + g_{se} = (1 + g_K^*)^\varphi. \quad (\star\star)$$
Step 3 (part c) Substitute $(\star)$ into $(\star\star)$ to eliminate $g_K^*$:
$$1 + g_{se} = \big[(1 + g_{se})(1 + n)\big]^\varphi.$$
Step 4 Apply $(xy)^\varphi = x^\varphi y^\varphi$ and divide both sides by $(1+g_{se})^\varphi$:
$$\frac{1 + g_{se}}{(1+g_{se})^\varphi} = (1+n)^\varphi \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad (1+g_{se})^{1-\varphi} = (1+n)^\varphi.$$
Step 5 Take the $1/(1-\varphi)$ power on both sides (this is well-defined because $\varphi < 1$):
$$1 + g_{se} = (1+n)^{\varphi/(1-\varphi)}.$$
$$g_{se} = (1+n)^{\varphi/(1-\varphi)} - 1.$$

What is load-bearing in the derivation. Step 1 uses the definition of steady state in terms of $\tilde k$ (the per-effective-worker variable). Step 2 uses the structural form of the externality $A_t = K_t^\varphi$. The combination — semi-endogenous logic — is that the engine of $A_t$ growth is $K_t$ growth, which itself depends on $A_t L_t$ scaling, closing the loop. Without $n > 0$ to push $L_t$, the loop has no exogenous driver.

Method variation: $g_{se}$ from the aggregate PF and BGP

Step 1 Take logs of the aggregate PF $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)} L_t^{1-\alpha}$:
$$\log Y_t = [\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)] \log K_t + (1-\alpha) \log L_t.$$
Step 2 Differentiate w.r.t. $t$ (or equivalently take growth rates in continuous time / first-order discrete approximation):
$$g_Y = [\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)] g_K + (1-\alpha) n.$$
Step 3 On the BGP, $Y$ and $K$ grow at the same rate (the capital-output ratio is constant in steady state). Set $g_Y = g_K = g_K^*$:
$$g_K^* = [\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)] g_K^* + (1-\alpha) n.$$
Step 4 Move all $g_K^*$ terms to the left:
$$g_K^* \big\{1 - \alpha - \varphi(1-\alpha)\big\} = (1-\alpha) n.$$

Simplify the brace: $1 - \alpha - \varphi(1-\alpha) = (1-\alpha) - \varphi(1-\alpha) = (1-\alpha)(1 - \varphi)$.

Step 5 Solve for $g_K^*$:
$$g_K^* = \frac{(1-\alpha) n}{(1-\alpha)(1-\varphi)} = \frac{n}{1-\varphi}.$$
Step 6 Convert to growth in output per worker, $g_y = g_K^* - n$ (since on the BGP $g_y = g_K - g_L = g_K - n$):
$$g_y = \frac{n}{1-\varphi} - n = \frac{n - n(1-\varphi)}{1-\varphi} = \frac{n \varphi}{1-\varphi}.$$
Continuous-time / first-order: $g_y = \dfrac{n \varphi}{1-\varphi}$.

Agreement with the first derivation. The first derivation gave $g_{se} = (1+n)^{\varphi/(1-\varphi)} - 1$. Expand for small $n$ using $\log(1 + g_{se}) = \varphi/(1-\varphi) \cdot \log(1+n)$ and $\log(1+x) \approx x$:

$$g_{se} \approx \frac{\varphi}{1-\varphi} \cdot n = \frac{n\varphi}{1-\varphi}.$$

The two methods agree to first order in $n$. The exact discrete formula is $(1+n)^{\varphi/(1-\varphi)} - 1$; the continuous-time / BGP linearisation is $n\varphi/(1-\varphi)$. The continuous-time route is cleaner algebra; the discrete route gives the exact compounded answer that the model literally generates.

What does each method buy you? The first derivation (BGP requirement on $\tilde k$) is the textbook's route and shows that the engine is the $A$-$K$-$L$ coupling at steady state — it makes the externality structure central. The second derivation (aggregate PF + BGP requirement on $Y/K$) is more transparent: it shows that the growth rate is essentially $n$ divided by the gap to constant returns, $1 - \varphi$. Both methods give the same answer, but the second one makes the comparative statics on $\varphi$ vivid: as $\varphi \to 1$ the denominator shrinks and growth blows up — which is exactly the transition to the AK / truly endogenous regime studied in Exercise 3.

Counterfactual: $n \to 0$

Step 1 Take the limit in the exact formula:
$$\lim_{n \to 0} g_{se} = \lim_{n \to 0} \big[(1+n)^{\varphi/(1-\varphi)} - 1\big] = 1^{\varphi/(1-\varphi)} - 1 = 0.$$

Or equivalently, in the linearised form: $\lim_{n \to 0} n\varphi/(1-\varphi) = 0$.

Without population growth, the semi-endogenous model predicts $g_{se} = 0$ — zero long-run technological progress.

Economic intuition. Increasing returns to scale at the aggregate are useless without scale itself being increased. The model's growth engine is the externality $A_t = K_t^\varphi$. For $A_t$ to grow, $K_t$ must grow. But in the semi-endogenous regime ($\varphi < 1$), capital accumulates only as fast as effective labour $A_t L_t$ grows — which requires $n > 0$ as an exogenous driver. Set $n = 0$ and the loop has no input; the economy converges to a finite capital stock and stops growing. This is the formal counterpart of Cozzi's slogan 'to utilise increasing returns, scale increases are required' (Lec 4a p14).

Policy implication and the empirical puzzle. Cozzi (Lec 4a p15) deduces from this: 'to promote long-run economic growth, promote population growth.' But the cross-country evidence (p16-17) shows a clear negative correlation between population growth $n$ and GDP-per-capita growth — exactly the opposite of the model's prediction. The puzzle has two possible resolutions: (a) the model is wrong (semi-endogenous growth is the wrong frame; we need truly endogenous growth where $g$ does not depend on $n$); or (b) the data does not reflect steady states — countries are in slow transitions, and the relevant horizon for the semi-endogenous prediction is centuries, not decades. Both readings appear in the literature; Cozzi's lectures lean on (a) as the motivation for the AK model and the R&D-based Romer model in Lecture 4b.

Synthesis: what this exercise teaches

Semi-endogenous growth is the textbook's first true endogenous growth model — the long-run growth rate is determined by model parameters rather than imposed exogenously. But the result is bittersweet: while the model formally endogenises growth, it does so in a way that makes population growth the engine, and the cross-country empirical evidence is precisely the opposite of what this prediction would require. This exercise is therefore a study in both how the model works mechanically and why it fails empirically.

Lesson 1: the formula $g_{se} = (1+n)^{\varphi/(1-\varphi)} - 1$ has a clean structural reading. The denominator $1 - \varphi$ is the gap from constant returns to capital alone — the smaller this gap, the larger the leverage of $n$ on $g_{se}$. The numerator $\varphi$ is the externality strength — the larger, the more population growth gets amplified. As $\varphi \to 1$, the leverage diverges, marking the transition to the AK regime (Exercise 3) where the formula no longer applies. As $\varphi \to 0$, the leverage collapses, and the model converges to the basic Solow with no externality and no endogenous growth.

Lesson 2: $n > 0$ is structurally load-bearing. The counterfactual showed that $\lim_{n \to 0} g_{se} = 0$ — without population growth, the engine has no input and the economy stops growing. This is not a quirk; it is the mathematical signature of semi-endogeneity. Aggregate IRS need scale to be loaded; in this model, the only way to load scale is via $L$ growing. If population is constant, the externality $A_t = K_t^\varphi$ has nothing to compound on (capital would also stop growing because $\tilde k$ would be at a steady state). The model's growth depends on the same exogenous demographic forces it tries to make endogenous — a logical knot that the AK and R&D variants try to untie.

Lesson 3: two derivation methods, two pedagogical lenses. The BGP-on-$\tilde k$ method (Main proof) shows that the engine is the $A$-$K$-$L$ coupling at steady state — it makes the externality structure central. The BGP-on-$Y/K$ method (Method variation) is more transparent for comparative statics — it gives $g_y = n\varphi/(1-\varphi)$ directly, showing immediately how the answer depends on each parameter. Both methods agree to first order. The textbook uses the first method because it stays close to the model's dynamic structure; researchers doing comparative statics often prefer the second. Knowing both is the deepest level of understanding of the model.

Lesson 4: the empirical paradox is the model's defining feature. Cozzi (Lec 4a p15-17) shows the cross-country scatter of $g$ vs $n$ — a clear negative correlation, exactly the opposite of the model's prediction. There are three responses available:

(a) Reject the model. The semi-endogenous structure is wrong; we need truly endogenous growth where the savings rate enters the long-run growth rate. This leads to the AK model (Exercise 3) and the R&D-based Romer model (Exercises 4 and 5).

(b) Reject the steady-state interpretation. Countries are in slow transitions, and the relevant horizon for the model's prediction is centuries, not decades. This is the textbook's softer defence on Lec 4a p19 — if convergence is very slow, observed cross-country variation reflects transitional rather than steady-state behaviour.

(c) Reject the unit of analysis. The model is for the world, not country-by-country. World population and world growth have both been rising over the past 200 years, consistent with the model's prediction. This is the global, long-run argument Cozzi mentions on p19.

Each response leads to a different research direction. Cozzi's lectures lean on (a) — Module 4b (Romer R&D) and Module 5 (no-scale-effects models) are the formal alternatives. But (b) and (c) are not crazy positions and appear in the broader literature.

Main proof: $Y = AK$ and $g_e = sA - \delta$

Step 1 (part a) Start with the aggregate PF derived in Exercise 1:
$$Y_t = K_t^{\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)} L_t^{1-\alpha}.$$
Step 2 Substitute $\varphi = 1$. The exponent on $K_t$ becomes:
$$\alpha + 1 \cdot (1-\alpha) = \alpha + 1 - \alpha = 1.$$

So the PF reduces to $Y_t = K_t^1 L_t^{1-\alpha} = K_t L_t^{1-\alpha}$.

Step 3 Substitute $L_t = L$ (constant population, $n = 0$). Define the productivity constant:
$$A \equiv L^{1-\alpha} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad Y_t = A K_t.$$
$$Y_t = A K_t, \quad A = L^{1-\alpha}.$$

Output is linear in capital, with constant marginal product $A = L^{1-\alpha}$. There are no diminishing returns to capital at the aggregate — the externality $A_t = K_t^\varphi$ with $\varphi = 1$ exactly cancels the diminishing-returns-to-capital force from the original $K^\alpha$ component.

Step 4 (part b) Start with capital accumulation:
$$K_{t+1} = s Y_t + (1 - \delta) K_t.$$
Step 5 Substitute $Y_t = A K_t$:
$$K_{t+1} = s A K_t + (1 - \delta) K_t = \big[1 + sA - \delta\big] K_t.$$
Step 6 Compute the growth rate:
$$g_e \equiv \frac{K_{t+1} - K_t}{K_t} = sA - \delta.$$
$$g_e = sA - \delta.$$

All three variables grow at the same rate. Since $Y_t = A K_t$ with $A$ time-invariant, $Y_t$ grows at the same rate as $K_t$, namely $g_e$. With $L_t = L$ constant, $y_t \equiv Y_t/L$ also grows at $g_e$. We have a BGP from period 1: there is no transition, no convergence — the economy grows at $g_e$ from any initial $K_0$. This is the 'truly endogenous' character of AK: the savings rate $s$ enters the growth rate, not just the level.

Step 7 (part c) For positive growth, we need:
$$g_e > 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad sA > \delta.$$

Economically: gross investment per unit of capital ($sA = sY/K$) must exceed depreciation. If $sA < \delta$, depreciation outpaces investment and the capital stock shrinks — $g_e < 0$.

Comparison to semi-endogenous. In the semi-endogenous regime ($\varphi < 1$, Exercise 2), the growth rate depended only on $n$, $\varphi$ — the structural-policy variables $s$ and $\delta$ shifted only the level of the BGP, not its growth rate. The AK regime ($\varphi = 1$) breaks this: $s$ and $\delta$ now enter the growth rate itself. This is the central qualitative shift the AK model offers — and it is exactly why higher savings could permanently raise growth, an implication far stronger than the basic Solow's transitory boost.

Falsification probe: scale effect and US data

Step 1 (part a) Differentiate $g_e = s L^{1-\alpha} - \delta$ w.r.t. $L$:
$$\frac{\partial g_e}{\partial L} = s (1 - \alpha) L^{-\alpha} > 0.$$

(Positive because $s > 0$, $1 - \alpha > 0$, $L > 0$.)

Scale effect: $\partial g_e/\partial L > 0$. Larger labour force implies faster growth.
Step 2 (part b) Testable prediction: across countries with comparable $s$ and $\delta$, the larger country (more workers) should grow faster. Across decades within a single country with rising $L$, the growth rate should rise.
Step 3 (part c) US data confrontation. From 1900 to 2000, US labour force grew from $L_{1900} \approx 76$ million to $L_{2000} \approx 280$ million. The ratio is $280/76 \approx 3.7$. With $\alpha = 1/3$, so $1 - \alpha = 2/3$:
$$\frac{A_{2000}}{A_{1900}} = \left(\frac{L_{2000}}{L_{1900}}\right)^{1-\alpha} = 3.7^{2/3} \approx 2.4.$$
Step 4 Holding $s$ and $\delta$ fixed, the AK model predicts the productivity term $sA$ should rise by the same factor 2.4. If $g_e^{1900} \approx 2\%$ and we set $sA_{1900} = g_e^{1900} + \delta = 0.07$, then $sA_{2000} = 2.4 \cdot 0.07 = 0.168$, giving $g_e^{2000} = 0.168 - 0.05 = 0.118$ — about $11.8\%$ per year. Yet observed US per-capita growth was roughly constant at $\sim 2\%$ throughout the century. The model predicts almost six-fold acceleration; the data shows no acceleration at all.
Predicted: $g_e^{2000} \approx 11.8\%$ — observed: $\approx 2\%$. Massive falsification.

What this means for the AK model. The scale effect is the empirically falsified prediction of strictly endogenous AK growth. Cozzi flags it as 'a serious and problematic scale effect' in Lecture 4a (p23). The model survives only as a stylised case — useful for showing that endogenous growth from accumulable factors is mathematically possible — but it cannot be a quantitative description of any real economy's growth path over a century. Module 5 (no-scale-effects models) is the natural response: replace the externality structure so the scale variable cancels out of the long-run growth rate.

Numeric inversion: implied $s$ and the calibration failure

Step 1 (part a) Given $g_e = 0.02$, $\delta = 0.05$, $A = 1$, invert $g_e = sA - \delta$ for $s$:
$$s = \frac{g_e + \delta}{A} = \frac{0.02 + 0.05}{1} = 0.07.$$

Implied savings rate $s \approx 7\%$.

Step 2 (part b) Compare to observed OECD savings rates. The IMF / World Bank estimates put OECD gross savings rates at 20-30% of GDP. A 7% implied $s$ from the AK model is roughly one-third of the observed average. The model is mis-calibrated by a factor of 3-4.
Step 3 (part c) Reverse: plug in a realistic $s = 0.25$, $A = 1$, $\delta = 0.05$:
$$g_e = 0.25 \cdot 1 - 0.05 = 0.20 = 20\% \text{ per year}.$$

This is roughly $10\times$ observed long-run growth.

At realistic $s = 25\%$, AK predicts $g_e = 20\%$/yr — an order of magnitude above the data.

What this tells us. The AK model fails not just qualitatively (scale effect) but also quantitatively: it is impossible to choose $s$ and $A$ to simultaneously match (i) realistic savings rates and (ii) realistic growth rates. The mis-calibration is structural — the model predicts that the savings rate enters the growth rate, but in the data the savings rate clearly enters the level of GDP much more than the growth rate. A model with diminishing returns to broad capital (basic Solow, MRW, or semi-endogenous variants) escapes this trap because $s$ scales the steady-state level but leaves the steady-state growth rate determined by exogenous technological progress (or by $n$ in the semi-endogenous case). The AK result that 'higher $s$ permanently raises the growth rate' is the model's signature feature, but it is also exactly what the cross-country and cross-time evidence does not support at the magnitudes the model produces.

Synthesis: what this exercise teaches

The AK model is the textbook's purest demonstration of truly endogenous growth: a Solow-style framework where the long-run growth rate is determined by the savings rate and the depreciation rate, with no exogenous technological progress and no population growth required. It is, in many ways, the simplest endogenous-growth model — the algebra reduces to $Y = AK$ and $g_e = sA - \delta$, both elementary expressions. But its simplicity is a double-edged sword: the model's strongest prediction (the scale effect) is the one most sharply contradicted by the data, and its quantitative calibration to OECD economies is impossible.

Lesson 1: $\varphi = 1$ is structurally special. At this knife-edge value, the externality exactly cancels the diminishing-returns-to-capital force in the original Cobb-Douglas. The aggregate PF becomes linear in $K$, the marginal product of capital is constant, and the savings rate enters the growth rate directly. This is qualitatively different from the basic Solow (where $s$ shifts only the steady-state level) or the semi-endogenous Ch 8 model (where $s$ shifts the level and $n$ determines the growth rate). The AK regime is the textbook's argument that endogenous growth from accumulable factors is at least mathematically possible.

Lesson 2: the scale effect is the model's signature falsifiable prediction. $g_e = sA - \delta$ with $A = L^{1-\alpha}$ implies $\partial g_e/\partial L > 0$ — bigger countries grow faster, and within one country, growth accelerates as population rises. The US 20th-century test (a roughly 3.7× rise in population) implies the AK model predicts a 2.4× rise in the productivity term $sA$, which holding $s$ fixed would translate to a roughly six-fold rise in $g_e$. Yet observed US per-capita growth was approximately constant. This is one of the cleanest falsifications of a structural macroeconomic model — the prediction is precise, the data are clear, and the mismatch is large.

Lesson 3: the quantitative calibration is impossible. The numeric inversion showed that observed OECD growth rates of 2% require an implied $s \approx 7\%$, far below realistic savings rates of 20-30%. Conversely, plugging in realistic savings rates gives $g_e \approx 20\%$, an order of magnitude above observed growth. The AK model has no parameter combination that simultaneously matches realistic savings and realistic growth — a structural mis-calibration that no parameter-tuning can fix.

Lesson 4: what survives. Despite the empirical failures, the AK model is not useless. It is the textbook's argument that endogenous growth is mathematically possible — that a Solow-style model can in principle generate sustained per-capita growth without exogenous technological progress. The failures point to specific architectural features that need fixing: the scale effect points to the need for models where the long-run growth rate does not depend on the level of population (Module 5: no-scale-effects models). The mis-calibration points to the need for models with diminishing returns to broad capital, where $s$ enters the level but not the growth rate (basic Solow, MRW, semi-endogenous variants). Both directions are pursued in the rest of the course.

(2) R&D production function $$A_{t+1} - A_t = \rho A_t^\varphi L_{At}^\lambda.$$ Parameters: $\rho > 0$ (R&D productivity scaling), $\varphi$ ('standing on shoulders'), $\lambda$ ('stepping on toes'). The R&D sector uses labour $L_{At}$ and the existing knowledge stock $A_t$ to produce new ideas $A_{t+1} - A_t$. No capital input.
(3) Capital accumulation $$K_{t+1} = s Y_t + (1-\delta) K_t.$$ Parameters: $s \in (0,1)$ (savings rate), $\delta \in (0,1)$ (depreciation). Identical to the basic Solow capital accumulation.
(4) Population growth $$L_{t+1} = (1+n) L_t.$$ Parameter: $n$ (population growth rate). May be positive, zero, or negative depending on the variant we study.
(5) Labour allocation identity $$L_t = L_{Yt} + L_{At}.$$ No parameters. Labour used in one sector cannot be used in the other.
(6) R&D-share rule $$L_{At} = s_R L_t, \quad 0 < s_R < 1.$$ Parameter: $s_R$ (the R&D share of the labour force). Exogenous and constant.

State variables. The three state variables are $K_t, L_t, A_t$. Their evolution is determined by equations (3), (4), and (2) respectively. The other equations are constraints / shares that pin down the allocation of inputs and the structure of production.

Parameter inventory. $\alpha$ appears only in (1); $\rho, \varphi, \lambda$ only in (2); $s, \delta$ only in (3); $n$ only in (4); $s_R$ only in (6). The structural roles are cleanly separated by equation — useful when doing comparative statics on a single parameter without worrying about cross-equation interactions.

Main proof: the $g_t$ difference equation

Step 1 Take the R&D production function (eq. 2) and divide both sides by $A_t$:
$$\frac{A_{t+1} - A_t}{A_t} = \rho \frac{A_t^\varphi}{A_t} L_{At}^\lambda = \rho A_t^{\varphi - 1} L_{At}^\lambda.$$
Step 2 The left-hand side is precisely $g_t$ by definition:
$$g_t = \rho A_t^{\varphi - 1} L_{At}^\lambda.$$
Step 3 Substitute (6) $L_{At} = s_R L_t$:
$$g_t = \rho A_t^{\varphi - 1} (s_R L_t)^\lambda = \rho s_R^\lambda A_t^{\varphi - 1} L_t^\lambda.$$
Step 4 Write the same expression at date $t+1$. This is where constant $s_R$ matters: we use $L_{A,t+1} = s_R L_{t+1}$ with the same $s_R$:
$$g_{t+1} = \rho s_R^\lambda A_{t+1}^{\varphi - 1} L_{t+1}^\lambda.$$
Step 5 Form the ratio $g_{t+1}/g_t$. The constants $\rho$ and $s_R^\lambda$ cancel:
$$\frac{g_{t+1}}{g_t} = \left(\frac{A_{t+1}}{A_t}\right)^{\varphi - 1} \left(\frac{L_{t+1}}{L_t}\right)^\lambda.$$
Step 6 Substitute $A_{t+1}/A_t = 1 + g_t$ (by definition of $g_t$) and $L_{t+1}/L_t = 1 + n$ (from eq. 4):
$$\frac{g_{t+1}}{g_t} = (1+g_t)^{\varphi - 1} (1+n)^\lambda.$$
Step 7 Multiply both sides by $g_t$ to isolate $g_{t+1}$:
$$g_{t+1} = (1+n)^\lambda (1+g_t)^{\varphi - 1} g_t.$$
$$g_{t+1} = (1+n)^\lambda (1+g_t)^{\varphi - 1} g_t.$$

Interpretation. This is the independent dynamics of $g_t$: it depends on $g_t$ alone (plus the parameters $n, \lambda, \varphi$), not on $K_t$ or $\tilde k_t$. Capital and output have their own dynamics (the $\tilde k$ equation), but technology evolves on its own clock. Cozzi (Lec 4b p15) calls this 'one-way coupled': $g_t \to \tilde k_t$ but not vice versa. This decoupling is why the steady-state growth rate is determined purely by R&D-side parameters $(\rho, \varphi, \lambda, n)$ — savings $s$ does not appear in the long-run growth rate (though it does enter the long-run level).

Method variation: continuous-time derivation

Step 1 (part a) In continuous time, $\dot A_t = \rho A_t^\varphi L_{At}^\lambda$. Divide by $A_t$:
$$g_t \equiv \dot A_t / A_t = \rho A_t^{\varphi - 1} L_{At}^\lambda.$$
Step 2 Take the natural log of both sides:
$$\log g_t = \log \rho + (\varphi - 1) \log A_t + \lambda \log L_{At}.$$
Step 3 (part b) Differentiate w.r.t. time. The constant $\log \rho$ disappears. Use $\dot A_t / A_t = g_t$ and $\dot L_{At} / L_{At} = n$ (because $L_{At} = s_R L_t$ with $s_R$ constant means $L_{At}$ grows at the same rate as $L_t$, which is $n$):
$$\frac{\dot g_t}{g_t} = (\varphi - 1) g_t + \lambda n.$$
$$\frac{\dot g_t}{g_t} = (\varphi - 1) g_t + \lambda n.$$
Step 4 (part c) The continuous-time growth-rate-of-the-growth-rate is a linear combination: a negative pull-back term $(\varphi - 1) g_t < 0$ when $\varphi < 1$, and a positive forcing term $\lambda n$ when $n > 0$. The negative pull-back is the diminishing-returns-to-knowledge force; the positive forcing is the scale-of-labour force.
Step 5 (part d) Connection to the discrete result. Take the discrete expression $g_{t+1}/g_t = (1+g_t)^{\varphi - 1} (1+n)^\lambda$ and expand for small $g_t, n$:
$$(1+g_t)^{\varphi - 1} \approx 1 + (\varphi - 1) g_t, \quad (1+n)^\lambda \approx 1 + \lambda n.$$

Multiply (dropping the second-order term $(\varphi - 1) g_t \cdot \lambda n$):

$$\frac{g_{t+1}}{g_t} \approx 1 + (\varphi - 1) g_t + \lambda n.$$

Subtract 1 (this is the per-period change in log-$g$):

$$\Delta g_t / g_t \approx (\varphi - 1) g_t + \lambda n.$$

This is exactly the continuous-time $\dot g_t / g_t$ expression — discrete and continuous agree to first order, as they must.

Why bother with two methods? The discrete derivation is the literal model: it is what the equations say. The continuous-time linearisation is easier to manipulate analytically — for instance, to find fixed points and to compute convergence speeds (which we do in Exercise 5). Both methods are standard and the choice is a matter of taste plus what comparative-statics question you are asking. The discrete form gives the exact compounded answer; the continuous form gives the cleanest first-order intuition.

Counterfactual: $\varphi > 1$ explosive case

Step 1 (part a) Recall the dynamic $g_{t+1} = (1+n)^\lambda (1+g_t)^{\varphi - 1} g_t$. If $\varphi > 1$, then $\varphi - 1 > 0$, so $(1+g_t)^{\varphi - 1} > 1$ whenever $g_t > 0$. Therefore:
$$g_{t+1} > (1+n)^\lambda g_t \geq g_t \quad (\text{for } n \geq 0).$$

The growth rate is strictly increasing in time. Worse, the factor $(1+g_t)^{\varphi - 1}$ itself grows with $g_t$, so the rate of increase compounds. The continuous-time formulation makes the explosion transparent: $\dot g_t / g_t = (\varphi - 1) g_t + \lambda n > 0$ and grows linearly in $g_t$ — a positive feedback loop with no equilibrium.

Step 2 (part b) Empirical evidence. Long-run growth rates in the developed world have been remarkably stable at $\sim 2\%$/year for the past 100-200 years. If $\varphi > 1$ held with any non-trivial leverage, we would have seen growth rates of tens of percent per year by now. The $\varphi > 1$ regime is empirically falsified by the simple observation that growth has not accelerated dramatically.
Step 3 (part c) Theoretical interpretation. The parameter $\varphi$ captures the elasticity of new-ideas production with respect to the existing stock of ideas — Cozzi calls this 'standing on shoulders' (Lec 4b p11). $\varphi > 0$ means past knowledge helps current research (we don't have to rediscover calculus to invent better algorithms). $\varphi < 1$ means there is some diminishing return — perhaps because the 'easy' ideas get found first ('fishing out' would be a separate force, captured by $\varphi < 0$). $\varphi = 1$ is Romer's original assumption — constant returns to ideas in idea-production. $\varphi > 1$ would mean ideas spawn ideas at an accelerating rate, leading to a singularity-like prediction. Cozzi (Lec 4b p11) explicitly assumes $\varphi < 1$ throughout the textbook treatment, leaving $\varphi = 1$ as the boundary case used in the strictly-endogenous variant.

Synthesis: what this exercise teaches

The Romer model is the textbook's most ambitious endogenous-growth framework: it explicitly endogenises technological progress by writing down a production function for ideas. The six equations are the formal apparatus; the $g_t$ difference equation is the analytical workhorse. Together they show how a labour-intensive R&D sector — with its own non-rival output (ideas) and its own input (researchers) — interacts with the rest of the economy to generate sustained growth.

Lesson 1: the one-way coupled structure is central. The dynamic $g_{t+1} = (1+n)^\lambda (1+g_t)^{\varphi-1} g_t$ depends only on $g_t$ and parameters — not on $K_t$ or $\tilde k_t$. Technology evolves on its own clock. This decoupling is what allows us to analyse the technological dynamics in isolation and then study the capital dynamics separately. It also explains why the long-run growth rate is determined by R&D-side parameters ($\rho, \varphi, \lambda, n$) and not by capital-side parameters ($s, \delta$). The savings rate enters the long-run level of capital (via the $\tilde k$ equation) but not the long-run growth rate of technology.

Lesson 2: standing on shoulders vs stepping on toes. The parameters $\varphi$ and $\lambda$ have distinct economic interpretations. $\varphi$ is the productivity of the existing knowledge stock in producing new ideas — Newton's standing on the shoulders of giants, the idea that current research benefits from past discoveries. Plausible range: $0 < \varphi < 1$ (diminishing returns from past knowledge; the easy ideas get found first, the fishing-out force) or $\varphi = 1$ (constant returns; Romer's original assumption). $\lambda$ is the productivity of additional researchers — could be less than 1 (stepping on toes, duplicated effort) or equal to 1 (no duplication). Empirically, both parameters are estimated to be in $(0, 1)$. Module 5 introduces fractional-$\lambda$ models explicitly.

Lesson 3: the discrete-continuous duality. The Method variation derived the same $g_t$ dynamic in continuous time: $\dot g_t / g_t = (\varphi - 1) g_t + \lambda n$. To first order in $g_t, n$, the discrete and continuous results coincide. The continuous-time form is easier to manipulate analytically — to find fixed points (Exercise 5), compute convergence speeds, or extend with additional state variables. The discrete form is what the textbook prints because it is what the equations literally say. Both are correct; both are used in the broader literature. Knowing both — and seeing how they correspond — is what distinguishes deep from surface understanding of the model.

Lesson 4: the $\varphi > 1$ explosion is the textbook's argument for boundedness. If past knowledge multiplied faster than it accumulated, growth would accelerate without bound. We would see growth rates of tens or hundreds of percent per year, not the steady $\sim 2\%$/year observed over the past 200 years. So $\varphi \leq 1$ is empirically required. This is why Cozzi restricts attention to $\varphi < 1$ (semi-endogenous regime) and $\varphi = 1$ (strictly endogenous regime, only with $n = 0$) — the explosive case is empirically ruled out.

Main proof: both Romer fixed points

Step 1 (part a, semi-endogenous) Assume $0 < \varphi < 1$ and $n > 0$. Set $g_{t+1} = g_t = g^*$ in the dynamic:
$$g^* = (1+n)^\lambda (1+g^*)^{\varphi - 1} g^*.$$
Step 2 Divide both sides by $g^*$ (valid because $g^* > 0$ in the relevant regime):
$$1 = (1+n)^\lambda (1+g^*)^{\varphi - 1}.$$
Step 3 Rearrange to isolate $g^*$. Move $(1+g^*)^{\varphi - 1}$ to the left as $(1+g^*)^{1-\varphi}$:
$$(1+g^*)^{1-\varphi} = (1+n)^\lambda.$$
Step 4 Take the $1/(1-\varphi)$ power on both sides (well-defined because $\varphi < 1$):
$$1 + g_{se} = (1+n)^{\lambda/(1-\varphi)}.$$
$$g_{se} = (1+n)^{\lambda/(1-\varphi)} - 1.$$

Note. $g_{se}$ depends only on $n$, $\lambda$, $\varphi$ — none of $s$, $s_R$, $\rho$ appears. The R&D-side parameters $\rho$ and $s_R$ shift the level of $A_t$ in steady state but not the growth rate. This is the signature of semi-endogenous growth: structural policies on $s_R$ (or savings $s$) cannot raise long-run growth.

Step 5 (part b, strictly endogenous) Assume $\varphi = 1$ and $n = 0$. The dynamic $g_{t+1} = (1+n)^\lambda (1+g_t)^{\varphi-1} g_t$ becomes $g_{t+1} = 1 \cdot (1+g_t)^0 g_t = g_t$ — every $g$ is a fixed point, so the recursion alone does not pin down $g^*$. $$g_t = \rho A_t^{\varphi - 1} (s_R L_t)^\lambda.$$
Step 6 Specialise: $\varphi = 1$ gives $A_t^{\varphi - 1} = A_t^0 = 1$. And $L_t = L$ constant (since $n = 0$):
$$g_t = \rho (s_R L)^\lambda.$$
$$g_e = \rho (s_R L)^\lambda \quad (\varphi = 1, n = 0).$$

$g_e$ is constant in time — there is no transition. As soon as the R&D share $s_R$ and population $L$ are fixed, technology grows at $g_e$ forever from period 1. Note that $s_R$ now enters the growth rate (not just the level) — in contrast to the semi-endogenous case where $s_R$ shifted only the level.

Step 7 (part c) Critical assumptions:

(a) Semi-endogenous requires $n > 0$: with $n = 0$, $(1+n)^{\lambda/(1-\varphi)} = 1$ and $g_{se} = 0$. The model collapses to zero long-run growth.

(b) Strictly endogenous requires $n = 0$ paired with $\varphi = 1$: if $n > 0$ and $\varphi = 1$, the dynamic $g_{t+1} = (1+n)^\lambda g_t$ diverges (multiplicative factor strictly above 1). If $\varphi = 1$ and $n > 0$, ideas accumulate faster and faster — not a sustainable BGP.

Cross-module bridge: structural parallel with Ch 8

The two formulas:

Ch 8 (externality) $g_{se}^{(8)} = (1+n)^{\varphi/(1-\varphi)} - 1$.
Ch 9 (R&D) $g_{se}^{(9)} = (1+n)^{\lambda/(1-\varphi)} - 1$.

Structurally identical: $(1+n)^{(\text{numerator parameter})/(1-\varphi)} - 1$.

(a) The numerator parameter — same role, different identity. In Ch 8, $\varphi$ is the elasticity of the productivity externality with respect to aggregate capital. A higher $\varphi$ means a small increase in $K_t$ generates a larger productivity boost across the economy — stronger 'learning-by-doing' spillover. In Ch 9, $\lambda$ is the elasticity of R&D output with respect to R&D labour. A higher $\lambda$ means a small increase in researchers generates more ideas per period. The two parameters describe different mechanisms — externality magnitude vs R&D returns to labour — but they share the same economic role: the leverage of scale on productivity. Both measure how strongly an increase in the scaling variable (capital stock in Ch 8; researcher count in Ch 9) translates into faster technological progress.

(b) The denominator $1-\varphi$ — gap from constant returns. In Ch 8, $1 - \varphi$ is the gap from constant returns to capital alone in the aggregate PF. (At $\varphi = 1$, aggregate capital has constant returns; below it, diminishing.) In Ch 9, $1 - \varphi$ is the gap from constant returns to ideas in idea-production. (At $\varphi = 1$, ideas have constant returns in producing more ideas; below it, diminishing — 'fishing out' effect.) Both are 'gap to truly endogenous' measures: how far the reproducible factor is from being self-sustaining. A smaller gap (larger $\varphi$, closer to 1) means stronger leverage — small changes in scale variable translate to large changes in growth rate.

Structural read: $(1+n)^{\text{leverage}/\text{gap to constant returns}} - 1$. Same architecture, different mechanism.

(c) Why $\varphi = 1$ marks the transition. The denominator vanishes, so the formula blows up — formally undefined. Economically, $\varphi = 1$ is the threshold where the reproducible factor (capital in Ch 8, knowledge in Ch 9) has exactly constant returns. Below this, the factor diminishes and growth needs an exogenous driver ($n$); at this, the factor sustains itself and growth no longer needs $n$; above this, the factor accelerates ($\varphi > 1$, explosive case from Exercise 4). The vanishing denominator is the mathematical signature of the qualitative change in growth regime. In Ch 8 with $\varphi = 1, n = 0$, we get the AK model with $g_e = sA - \delta$ (Exercise 3). In Ch 9 with $\varphi = 1, n = 0$, we get strictly endogenous Romer with $g_e = \rho (s_R L)^\lambda$ (Step 6 above). Two different mechanisms, both crossing the same boundary, both producing endogenous growth that depends on accumulation-side parameters.

Methodology reflection: hemi-endogenous growth

Step 1 (part a) Setup: $\varphi < 1$, $n = 0$, $s_R = s_R(t)$ slowly rising. The static expression for $g_t$ is:
$$g_t = \rho A_t^{\varphi - 1} (s_R(t) L)^\lambda.$$
Step 2 Take logs:
$$\log g_t = \log \rho + (\varphi - 1) \log A_t + \lambda \log s_R(t) + \lambda \log L.$$
Step 3 Differentiate w.r.t. time. The constants ($\log \rho$, $\lambda \log L$) vanish. Use $\dot A_t / A_t = g_t$ and define $g_{s_R} \equiv \dot s_R / s_R$:
$$\frac{\dot g_t}{g_t} = (\varphi - 1) g_t + \lambda g_{s_R}.$$
Step 4 (part b) At the quasi-balanced path, $\dot g_t = 0$:
$$0 = (\varphi - 1) g_{he} + \lambda g_{s_R}.$$
Step 5 Solve for $g_{he}$:
$$g_{he} = -\frac{\lambda g_{s_R}}{\varphi - 1} = \frac{\lambda g_{s_R}}{1 - \varphi}.$$
$$g_{he} = \frac{\lambda}{1 - \varphi} g_{s_R}.$$

The hemi-endogenous growth rate is proportional to the rate of change of the R&D share, with leverage $\lambda/(1-\varphi)$ — the same structural leverage as in semi-endogenous Romer, but applied to $g_{s_R}$ instead of $n$.

Step 6 (part c, 200-year interpretation) $s_R$ is bounded above by 1 (the R&D share cannot exceed the entire labour force). As $s_R$ approaches its ceiling, $g_{s_R} = \dot s_R / s_R$ must decline — eventually to zero — because the denominator stops growing relative to a fixed (or shrinking) numerator. Therefore in the long run:
$$\lim_{t \to \infty} g_{he}(t) = 0.$$

The hemi-endogenous model predicts that long-run growth will tend to zero — but the convergence can be very slow. If $s_R$ has been rising from $0.0005$ to $0.005$ over 200 years, $g_{s_R} \approx \log(10)/200 \approx 1.15\%$/year, giving $g_{he} \approx \lambda \cdot 1.15\% / (1 - \varphi)$ — broadly compatible with observed TFP growth for plausible $\lambda, \varphi$. The model thus rationalises the past 200 years as the transient adjustment of a system whose long-run growth is zero.

Why 'hybrid'? Semi-endogenous growth has $n$ as the exogenous driver: long-run growth is non-zero only if population grows. Strictly endogenous growth has $s_R L$ as the engine: long-run growth depends on R&D share and population level. Hemi-endogenous growth has $g_{s_R}$ — the rate of change of the R&D share — as the driver. Cozzi calls it 'hybrid' because it borrows the diminishing-returns structure of semi-endogenous ($\varphi < 1$ so no scale effect) while finding a non-population engine, but at the cost that the engine itself must run down eventually (since $s_R$ cannot grow forever). It is not a permanent solution — it is a way to rationalise 200 years of observed growth as a transient. The full alternatives are explored in Module 5 (Jones 1995, Kortum, Segerstrom, Howitt, Peretto, Young, Cozzi 2017), each of which finds different escape routes from the scale-effect problem.

Synthesis: what this exercise teaches

This exercise integrates everything from Lecture 4b into a single comparative-statics tableau: two Romer fixed points, their structural parallel with Ch 8, the empirical failure of the scale effect, and the hemi-endogenous escape. It is the synthesis exercise of Module 4 — by the time you have completed it, you understand the full architecture of endogenous growth and where each variant fits in the landscape.

Lesson 1: the two Romer regimes are limits of one model. The Romer dynamic $g_{t+1} = (1+n)^\lambda (1+g_t)^{\varphi-1} g_t$ has a unique long-run behaviour determined by $\varphi$ and $n$. The two pure cases — semi-endogenous ($\varphi < 1, n > 0$) and strictly endogenous ($\varphi = 1, n = 0$) — are the two corners of the parameter space where the answer simplifies. In the interior, the model collapses to the semi-endogenous formula. At the boundary $\varphi = 1$, the dynamic equation degenerates and the model collapses to the strictly endogenous formula with $n = 0$. The two regimes are not different models; they are the two ways the same model can produce a finite long-run growth rate.

Lesson 2: the cross-module parallel is not a coincidence. The Ch 8 externality model and the Ch 9 R&D model produce structurally identical semi-endogenous formulas: $(1+n)^{(\text{leverage})/(1-\varphi)} - 1$, with leverage $\varphi$ in Ch 8 and $\lambda$ in Ch 9. Both models have the same architecture: a reproducible factor with elasticity strictly less than one in producing more of itself, driven by an exogenous scale variable (population). Whenever you have this architecture, the formula 'rate of exogenous driver scaled by leverage/(1-self-elasticity)' will appear. This generalises beyond the two specific models in this course — it is a feature of any endogenous-growth model with non-unitary self-elasticity. When you see the formula in a future paper, you will recognise the architecture immediately.

Lesson 3: the scale effect is the most empirically falsified prediction of strictly endogenous growth. The model predicts that bigger economies (or economies with more researchers) should grow faster. Jones (1995) documented that US R&D headcount grew 5-fold between 1950 and 1990 with no corresponding rise in TFP growth — a sharp empirical rejection. The Jones critique launched Module 5 of this course: no-scale-effects models (Jones 1995, Kortum 1997, Segerstrom 1998, Howitt 1999, Peretto 1998, Young 1998, Cozzi 2017) all share the goal of removing the scale variable from the long-run growth rate while preserving some form of endogeneity. Each takes a different architectural route, but they share the diagnosis: strictly endogenous Romer is too strong in its dependence on $L$.
