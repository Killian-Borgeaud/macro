The 4-part essay structure Cozzi expects

  1. Introduction — what question this model answers; why we care (~1 paragraph).
  2. Sketch the model — assumptions and key equations (the technical half; algebra woven into prose, not labor-by-labor).
  3. Consequences — what the model implies: comparative statics, steady-state predictions, BGP behavior.
  4. Intuition / main points — the economic story. Include a diagram if applicable. This is the differentiator between 4/6 and 6/6.
§ 01

M1 — Basic Solow

module 1
What the model is for. Explain cross-country income dispersion via capital accumulation alone, taking technology as given. Predicts levels of $y^*$, not long-run growth rates. The foundation everything else extends.
Lecture 1c p23 — CONCLUSIONS BASED ON THE BASIC SOLOW MODEL (Cozzi's own answer skeleton)
Lecture 1c p21 — Modified Solow diagram: $sBk^\alpha$ vs $(n+\delta)k$, intersection at $k^*$
Steady-state $k^*$
(1)$sBk^\alpha = (n+\delta)k$set $\Delta k = 0$
(2)$sBk^{\alpha-1} = n+\delta$divide by $k$
(3)$k^{1-\alpha} = sB/(n+\delta)$invert exponent (sign flip)
(4)$k^* = \left(sB/(n+\delta)\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)}$raise to $1/(1-\alpha)$
Result. $k^* = (sB/(n+\delta))^{1/(1-\alpha)}$, $y^* = B^{1/(1-\alpha)}(s/(n+\delta))^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$
Result. $r^* = \alpha(n+\delta)/s$ — independent of $B$
Result. $s^{**} = \alpha$ is the Golden Rule saving rate (maximizes steady-state consumption per worker)
Result. Convergence speed: $\lambda = (1-\alpha)(n+\delta) \approx 5\text{-}6\%$/year (basic Solow with $\alpha=1/3$, $n+\delta=0.06$)
Mock answer (Cozzi tone)

Introduction. The basic Solow model explains differences in income levels across countries through a parsimonious account of capital accumulation, taking technology as exogenously given. Its central insight is that an economy converges to a steady state determined by structural parameters — saving rate, population growth, depreciation — rather than by initial conditions.

Sketch. Let production be Cobb-Douglas, $Y_t = BK_t^\alpha L_t^{1-\alpha}$, with constant returns to scale and competitive factor markets. Capital evolves according to $K_{t+1} = sY_t + (1-\delta)K_t$, and labor grows at rate $n$. Substituting into per-worker variables gives the Solow equation $\Delta k_t \approx sBk_t^\alpha - (n+\delta)k_t$, where actual investment per worker $sBk_t^\alpha$ must equal break-even investment $(n+\delta)k_t$ at the steady state.

Consequences. Setting $\Delta k = 0$ gives $k^* = (sB/(n+\delta))^{1/(1-\alpha)}$ and $y^* = B^{1/(1-\alpha)}(s/(n+\delta))^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$. Long-run growth of $y$ is zero — the model predicts levels, not growth rates. The steady-state rental rate $r^* = \alpha(n+\delta)/s$ is independent of $B$, a quantitatively sharp prediction. Convergence to $k^*$ is monotonic from any positive initial $k_0$, driven by diminishing returns to capital.

Intuition. What this tells us is that high-saving, low-fertility countries should be richer at the steady state, and convergence is conditional on parameters — not absolute. Empirically, the model gets the direction right (richer countries do save more, have lower $n$) but it underpredicts the magnitude of cross-country dispersion of $y^*$, and it predicts a convergence speed near 5-6% per year when the data show closer to 2%. These two empirical failures — the rate-of-convergence puzzle and the investment-rate-effect puzzle — are what motivates the human-capital augmentation of Mankiw, Romer, and Weil (1992).

Quick pitfalls
  • $r^*$ is independent of $B$, not $\alpha$ — easy to confuse under time pressure.
  • Basic Solow predicts zero per-capita growth at steady state. Don't write that it predicts a positive long-run growth rate.

M2 — General Solow w/ Tech Progress

module 2
What this extension is for. Basic Solow predicts zero growth, but the data show persistent positive growth in $y$. Adding labor-augmenting technological progress at rate $g$ rescues the model: along the balanced growth path, $k$, $y$, $c$, $w$ all grow at $g$, while $r$ stays constant — matching the Kaldor facts.
Lecture 2b p29 — CONCLUSIONS, THE GENERAL SOLOW MODEL (4-bullet skeleton)
Lecture 2b p24 — Empirical convergence regression (65 countries, 1960-2003)
Steady-state $\tilde k^*$ in per-effective-worker form
(1)$Y = K^\alpha (AL)^{1-\alpha}$labor-augmenting tech specification
(2)$\tilde y = \tilde k^\alpha$divide by $AL$
(3)$\Delta \tilde k \approx s\tilde k^\alpha - (n+g+\delta)\tilde k$effective-worker dilution now includes $g$
(4)$\tilde k^* = (s/(n+g+\delta))^{1/(1-\alpha)}$same algebra as basic Solow with $n+\delta \to n+g+\delta$
Result. Along the BGP, $k_t$, $y_t$, $c_t$, $w_t$ grow at rate $g$; $\tilde k$, $\tilde y$, $r$ are constant.
Result. Convergence: $\lambda = (1-\alpha)(n+g+\delta) \approx 5\text{-}6\%$/year (still too fast vs data).
Result. Open-economy: $V = K + F$, $\text{CA} = S - I = \dot F$ (current account = net foreign investment).
Result. Open-economy stability: $s\bar r < n$ (with $\bar r$ exogenous world rate).
Mock answer (Cozzi tone)

Introduction. Basic Solow predicts zero per-capita growth at steady state, which contradicts the most basic stylized fact: per-worker output grows steadily over the long run. The general Solow model rescues this by adding exogenous labor-augmenting technological progress, allowing the economy to settle on a balanced growth path rather than a fixed steady state.

Sketch. Production becomes $Y_t = K_t^\alpha (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}$, with $A_{t+1} = (1+g)A_t$. Expressing in per-effective-worker variables $\tilde k = K/(AL)$, the law of motion is $\Delta \tilde k \approx s\tilde k^\alpha - (n+g+\delta)\tilde k$, structurally identical to basic Solow with the augmented dilution term $n+g+\delta$. Solving gives $\tilde k^* = (s/(n+g+\delta))^{1/(1-\alpha)}$.

Consequences. Along the balanced growth path, $\tilde k$ and $\tilde y$ are constant, but $k_t = A_t \tilde k^*$ and $y_t = A_t \tilde y^*$ grow at the rate of technology $g$. The real wage $w_t$ also grows at $g$, while the rental rate $r$ stays constant — exactly the Kaldor facts. Convergence to the BGP is governed by $\lambda = (1-\alpha)(n+g+\delta)$, which with standard calibration gives 5-6% per year. Empirically convergence is observed at roughly 2% per year, so a magnitude problem persists. In the open-economy version, an additional accounting identity $\text{CA} = S - I$ links the current account to the gap between national savings and investment.

Intuition. The general Solow model performs quite well qualitatively: it generates positive per-capita growth, predicts conditional convergence, and matches the Kaldor facts on factor shares and the constancy of $r$. But the empirical problem is one of magnitudes — both the steady-state elasticities of $y^*$ with respect to $s$ and $n+\delta$ are too small, and convergence is too fast. Crucially, the growth rate $g$ is left unexplained: the model takes technology as a black box. This unsatisfying feature is what later motivates the endogenous-growth literature.

Quick pitfalls
  • Use $\tilde k$ (per effective worker) only when labor-augmenting tech is in play. Don't mix with basic-Solow $k$ inside the same derivation.
  • $\text{CA} = S - I$ is an identity, not a behavioral equilibrium condition. Don't argue from it as if it were.

M3 — MRW (Human Capital)

module 3
What MRW is for. A single parameter change — replacing $\alpha$ with $\alpha + \phi$ as the broad-capital exponent — closes the rate-of-convergence puzzle and the investment-rate-effect puzzle simultaneously. The whole point of M3.
Lecture 3a p31 — CONCLUSIONS MRW (6-bullet skeleton, THE answer template)
Lecture 3a p17 — Phase diagram for the Solow model with human capital
Steady-state $\tilde k^*, \tilde h^*$
(1)$Y = K^\alpha H^\phi (AL)^{1-\alpha-\phi}$Cobb-Douglas with two accumulable factors
(2)$\Delta \tilde k = s_K \tilde k^\alpha \tilde h^\phi - (n+g+\delta)\tilde k$law of motion for physical capital per effective worker
(3)$\Delta \tilde h = s_H \tilde k^\alpha \tilde h^\phi - (n+g+\delta)\tilde h$symmetric law for human capital
(4)$\tilde k^* = \left(s_K^{1-\phi} s_H^\phi / (n+g+\delta)\right)^{1/(1-\alpha-\phi)}$solve the two-equation system at $\Delta \tilde k = \Delta \tilde h = 0$
Result. $\tilde y^* = (s_K^\alpha s_H^\phi)^{1/(1-\alpha-\phi)} / (n+g+\delta)^{(\alpha+\phi)/(1-\alpha-\phi)}$
Result. Elasticity of $\tilde y^*$ w.r.t. $s_K$ is $\alpha/(1-\alpha-\phi) \approx 1$ when $\alpha+\phi \approx 0.66$ (vs $\approx 0.5$ in basic Solow)
Result. Convergence: $\lambda \approx (1-\alpha-\phi)(n+g+\delta) \approx 2\%$/year — matches data
Result. Golden Rule: $s_K^{**} = \alpha$, $s_H^{**} = \phi$
Result. Empirical fit: $R^2$ rises from 0.63 (basic Solow) to 0.79 (MRW); slope estimates 0.59 and 0.93
Mock answer (Cozzi tone)

Introduction. Mankiw, Romer, and Weil (1992) propose a brilliant fix to the empirical failures of the basic Solow model. They observe that human capital — embodied in workers via schooling, training, and health — accumulates like physical capital and earns its own factor return. Adding it as a separate accumulable input lets a single parameter change close two empirical puzzles at once.

Sketch. Production becomes $Y = K^\alpha H^\phi (AL)^{1-\alpha-\phi}$ with $\alpha + \phi + (1-\alpha-\phi) = 1$, preserving constant returns to scale across the three factors. Two separate saving rates govern the accumulation of $K$ and $H$, giving the coupled law of motion $\Delta \tilde k = s_K \tilde k^\alpha \tilde h^\phi - (n+g+\delta)\tilde k$ and a symmetric equation for $\tilde h$. There is no simple diagrammatic argument for convergence in this two-variable system; one must rely on a phase-plane argument or numerical simulation. Steady-state $\tilde k^*$ and $\tilde h^*$ are obtained by setting both changes to zero and solving the resulting system.

Consequences. The crucial result is that the broad-capital exponent $\alpha + \phi$ now controls both the steady-state elasticities and the rate of convergence. Empirically MRW estimate $\alpha + \phi \approx 0.66$, which simultaneously raises the elasticity of $\tilde y^*$ with respect to $s_K$ to roughly 1 (matching the cross-country regression slope) and lowers the predicted convergence speed to $\lambda \approx (1-\alpha-\phi)(n+g+\delta) \approx 2\%$ per year — matching the data. The Golden Rule extends naturally: $s_K^{**} = \alpha$, $s_H^{**} = \phi$.

Intuition. The deep insight is that the same parameter $1 - \alpha - \phi$ wears two hats — it controls the elasticity of $\tilde y^*$ to investment and the speed of convergence. By making accumulable capital broader (adding $H$), MRW reduce $1 - \alpha - \phi$ from $\approx 0.67$ to $\approx 0.34$, which scales both empirical predictions toward the observed magnitudes. It is reassuring to find that one structural modification fixes both puzzles. What remains unexplained is why countries have such different saving rates $s_K, s_H$ — and why technology still grows exogenously. These open questions motivate Module 4.

Quick pitfalls
  • $\phi$ here is the human-capital exponent in MRW, not a CES parameter or any other Greek-letter reuse.
  • The full system is two-dimensional in $(\tilde k, \tilde h)$; you cannot draw a single-line Solow diagram. Use a phase diagram if asked for a graphical argument.

M3 — Solow with Land & Oil

module 3
What this extension is for. Introduce non-reproducible factors (fixed land $X$ and a depleting stock of oil $R$) to revisit classical pessimism. Growth becomes a tug-of-war between technology pushing $y$ up and population dilution + depletion pulling it down.
Lecture 3b p23 — CONCLUSION (3-bullet summing-up: growth optimism / pessimism / conditional)
Lecture 3b p20 — Growth rate under balanced growth (the three-drag formula)
Steady-state growth rate $g^y$ under BGP
(1)$Y_t = K_t^\alpha (A_t L_t)^\beta X^\kappa E_t^\varepsilon$with $\alpha + \beta + \kappa + \varepsilon = 1$
(2)$R_{t+1} = (1-s_E) R_t$, $E_t = s_E R_t$oil depletes geometrically
(3)Take logs, differentiate, impose BGPlinearize around the balanced growth path
(4)$g^y = \dfrac{\beta g - (\kappa + \varepsilon)(n + s_E)}{\beta + \kappa + \varepsilon}$ (modulo simplification)three drags vs technology push
Result. $g^y$ numerator: $\beta g$ (technology push) vs $(\kappa + \varepsilon)(n + s_E)$ (dilution + depletion drags)
Result. Classical (Malthusian) case: drags dominate, $g^y < 0$ — long-run per-capita decline
Result. Modern case: technology dominates, $g^y > 0$ — growth despite finite resources
Result. Cozzi's calibration (p21): $\alpha=0.2$, $\beta=0.6$, $\kappa=\varepsilon=0.1$, $s_E=0.005$, $n=0.01$, $g=0.02$ $\Rightarrow g^y \approx 1.5\%$/year
Mock answer (Cozzi tone)

Introduction. The Solow model with land and natural resources revisits the classical Malthusian worry that finite resources must eventually constrain economic growth. By distinguishing reproducible inputs (capital) from non-reproducible ones (fixed land, depleting oil), the model lets us derive precisely when technology dominates the resource drag and when it does not.

Sketch. Aggregate output is $Y_t = K_t^\alpha (A_t L_t)^\beta X^\kappa E_t^\varepsilon$ with the exponents summing to one, ensuring constant returns across all four inputs. Land $X$ is fixed; oil reserves $R_t$ deplete at extraction rate $s_E$, so $R_{t+1} = (1-s_E)R_t$ and current extraction is $E_t = s_E R_t$. Labor-augmenting technology grows at rate $g$ as before. The state variables are $K_t, A_t, L_t, R_t$, and the system can be reduced to a transition curve in $z = K/Y$.

Consequences. Linearizing around the balanced growth path yields the steady-state growth rate $g^y = (\beta g - (\kappa+\varepsilon)(n+s_E))/(\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon)$. The numerator captures the central tug-of-war: technology growth scaled by labor's share $\beta$ pushes per-capita output up; population dilution of fixed land and oil depletion both push it down. Cozzi's calibration with $\beta=0.6$, $\kappa=\varepsilon=0.1$, $g=2\%$, $n=1\%$, $s_E=0.5\%$ delivers $g^y \approx 1.5\%$ per year — modest but positive.

Intuition. The interesting result is that whether long-run per-capita growth is positive or negative depends entirely on a parametric race. In the classical case — low $g$, high $n$, high extraction $s_E$ — drags dominate and we recover Malthusian decline. In the modern case — high tech progress, low population growth, slow extraction — growth wins and per-capita output rises steadily despite finite resources. The model is reassuring for Western economies with declining population and rapid technology, but offers no guarantee for high-fertility, resource-dependent economies. To simulate, iterate the four state variables $K, A, L, R$ period by period, compute $Y$, $y$, $c$ at each step, and plot the resulting paths over $T = 100$ periods.

Quick pitfalls
  • The exponents must sum to 1 to preserve constant returns. If you drop one factor, redistribute its weight; don't change the production-function structure.
  • Oil depletion enters twice in $g^y$: directly via $\varepsilon s_E$ and indirectly via population dilution of the oil stock ($\varepsilon n$).

M4 — Endogenous Growth (Externalities + R&D)

module 4
What this module is for. Endogenize the growth rate of technology. Two flavors: productive externalities (Romer 1986 — knowledge as a by-product of capital accumulation) and explicit R&D (Romer 1990 — researchers produce ideas). Both deliver sustained growth without exogenous $g$.
Lecture 4a p27 — CONCLUSIONS (semi-endogenous vs truly-endogenous trade-off)
Lecture 4b p13 — THE COMPLETE MODEL (R&D Romer macromodel equations)
R&D Romer model core equations
(1)$Y = K^\alpha (A L_Y)^{1-\alpha}$production with labor in goods sector $L_Y$
(2)$\Delta A = \lambda L_A^\phi A^\theta$ideas production; researchers $L_A$, parameters $\lambda, \phi, \theta$
(3)$L_Y + L_A = L$, $K_{t+1} = sY_t + (1-\delta)K_t$labor allocation + capital accumulation
(4)BGP: $g_A = \lambda L_A^\phi A^{\theta-1}$growth of technology depends on researcher scale and idea-stock externality
Result. Semi-endogenous ($\theta < 1$): $g_A = \lambda n / (1 - \theta)$ — growth scales with population growth, not levels. Policy raises levels but not long-run growth.
Result. Truly endogenous ($\theta = 1$): $g_A = \lambda L_A$ — growth scales with research levels. Predicts scale effect (large countries grow faster), empirically false.
Result. Externality version (Romer 1986): $A_t = K_t^\eta$, growth depends on aggregate $K$ accumulation via spillover.
Result. Scale-effect critique (Jones 1995): the truly-endogenous prediction is testable, and the data reject it.
Mock answer (Cozzi tone)

Introduction. The endogenous-growth literature endogenizes the rate of technological progress that the Solow model takes as exogenous. Two distinct mechanisms emerged: knowledge as an externality of capital accumulation (Romer 1986), and an explicit research sector that produces ideas (Romer 1990). Both predict that economic policy can affect long-run growth, not merely income levels.

Sketch. In the R&D version, output is $Y = K^\alpha (AL_Y)^{1-\alpha}$, where $L_Y$ is labor in the goods sector. A separate ideas-production technology $\Delta A = \lambda L_A^\phi A^\theta$ converts researcher labor $L_A$ into new ideas, with $\phi \le 1$ capturing duplication ("stepping on toes") and $\theta$ capturing the idea-stock externality ("standing on shoulders"). The total labor constraint is $L_Y + L_A = L$. Combined with standard capital accumulation $K_{t+1} = sY_t + (1-\delta)K_t$, this gives a complete general-equilibrium system.

Consequences. On the balanced growth path, the growth rate of technology is $g_A = \lambda L_A^\phi A^{\theta-1}$, which differs sharply between two regimes. In the truly-endogenous case $\theta = 1$, growth is $g_A = \lambda L_A$ — proportional to the number of researchers in levels. This predicts a scale effect: larger economies should grow faster. In the semi-endogenous case $\theta < 1$, $g_A = \lambda n / (1-\theta)$ — growth scales with population growth, not population levels. Policy in this regime affects the level of the BGP path but not its slope.

Intuition. The economic story behind these models is the non-rivalry of ideas: once an idea exists, it can be used by everyone simultaneously, generating increasing returns at the social level. This mechanism naturally produces sustained growth without exogenous technology. The empirical scale effect, however, is sharply rejected by the data — large countries (China, India) do not grow systematically faster than small ones (Switzerland, Singapore). This empirical failure is the central motivation for the no-scale-effect refinements of Module 5.

Quick pitfalls
  • Two different $\phi$ conventions exist: the duplication parameter in R&D ($\phi \le 1$) is distinct from MRW's human-capital exponent. Cozzi uses both; track which you're in.
  • The truly-endogenous scale effect is the model's prediction, and the empirical failure of that prediction is the critique. Don't conflate.

M5 — No-Scale Endogenous + Cozzi (2017) Hybrid

module 5
What M5 is for. Fix the empirically failing scale effect from M4. Three competing solutions: Jones (1995) semi-endogenous, Howitt-Peretto-Young truly endogenous via product variety, and Cozzi (2017) hybrid combining both with a threshold. Q3 of two past exams is exactly Cozzi's hybrid.
Lecture 5b p17 — Summing Up Cozzi (2017) I
Lecture 5b p18 — Summing Up Cozzi (2017) II — policy implication + Lisbon example
Lecture 5b p19 — Summing Up Cozzi (2017) III — historical narrative
Cozzi hybrid threshold $\bar n$
(1)Two engines: vertical R&D (quality) + horizontal R&D (variety)shared researcher pool $L_A$
(2)Variety expansion neutralizes the scale effectà la Howitt/Peretto/Young
(3)Critical threshold $\bar n$: regime switches at $n = \bar n$below $\bar n$ truly endogenous, above semi-endogenous
(4)Policy parameter $s_R$ affects $\bar n$ itselfshifting R&D portfolio shifts the threshold
Result. For $n < \bar n$: growth is truly endogenous, scale-free, depends on R&D policy parameters (subsidies, IP).
Result. For $n > \bar n$: growth is semi-endogenous, scales with $n$ à la Jones.
Result. Policy can shift $\bar n$: by reallocating $L_A$ between vertical and horizontal R&D via differential subsidies. "Policy could make policy itself more effective for growth!"
Result. Lisbon Strategy example: raising $s_R$ from 1% to 3% shifts $\bar n$ from 0.44% to 1.18% (Lecture 5b p18).
Mock answer (Cozzi tone)

Introduction. Cozzi (2017) builds a hybrid R&D growth model that combines two previously rival mechanisms: the semi-endogenous engine of Jones (1995), where growth scales with population, and the truly-endogenous engine of Howitt-Peretto-Young, where horizontal variety expansion eliminates the scale effect. The paper shows that the two engines can coexist within a single R&D sector, generating a threshold-driven regime structure where one engine dominates depending on parameter values.

Sketch. The model has two complementary R&D activities — vertical (improving the quality of existing varieties) and horizontal (introducing new varieties) — competing for the same researcher pool $L_A$. The allocation between them is endogenous and responds to relative profitability. With population growing at rate $n$, the model produces a critical threshold $\bar n$ at which the active growth engine switches. Cozzi derives the closed form of $\bar n$ from the equilibrium R&D allocation condition.

Consequences. For $n < \bar n$, the truly-endogenous engine dominates: growth is scale-free and depends on policy parameters such as the R&D subsidy rate $s_R$. For $n > \bar n$, the semi-endogenous engine takes over and growth scales with $n$ à la Jones; policy raises levels but not growth. Crucially, $\bar n$ itself depends on $s_R$ and on the R&D-portfolio split: raising the R&D subsidy from 1% to 3% shifts $\bar n$ from 0.44% to 1.18%, potentially moving an economy from one regime to the other.

Intuition. The signature policy implication is that policy can make policy itself more effective for growth. In a world where conventional wisdom following Jones says "R&D subsidies only raise levels, not growth rates", Cozzi's hybrid says: a subsidy that tilts the R&D portfolio toward variety expansion enough to cross the threshold can move the economy into a regime where further subsidies do raise growth. This makes the composition of R&D policy — and not only its size — a central choice for governments aiming to lift long-run growth. Historically, Cozzi (p19) reads the demographic transition through this lens: Malthusian regime, then industrial-revolution semi-endogenous regime, then post-transition truly-endogenous regime as fertility fell below $\bar n$.

Quick pitfalls
  • $s_R$ here is the R&D subsidy / research share — not the saving rate $s$.
  • The threshold $\bar n$ is endogenous to policy. Don't treat it as fixed when discussing policy implications.

M6 — Efficiency Wages

module 6
What this module is for. Explain why involuntary unemployment exists in long-run equilibrium. Firms set wages above market-clearing to elicit effort, employment adjusts to the resulting wage, and unemployment is structural — not a friction.
Lecture6b p5 — derivation: $w \cdot a'(w)/a(w) = 1$ (the famous Solow condition)
Lecture6b p11 — MODEL IMPLICATIONS (6-bullet skeleton)
Derivation of the Solow conditions
(1)$Y = F(K, a(w)L)$ with $a'(w) > 0$, $a''(w) < 0$effort $a(w)$ increasing concave in wage
(2)Firm minimizes cost per efficiency unit: $\min_w w/a(w)$effective wage per unit of labor input
(3)FOC: $a(w) - w a'(w) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; w a'(w)/a(w) = 1$elasticity of effort wrt wage equals 1
(4)Employment FOC: $F_L(K, a(w^*)L^*) = w^*$marginal product of effective labor equals real wage
Result. First Solow condition: $a'(w^*) w^* / a(w^*) = 1$ — elasticity of effort wrt wage equals 1 at the optimum.
Result. Second Solow condition: $F_L(K, a(w^*) L^*) = w^*$ — marginal product of effective labor equals the real wage.
Result. Wage rigidity: $w^*$ is determined by the shape of $a(\cdot)$, NOT by the supply and demand for labor.
Result. Equilibrium unemployment: labor market does not clear at $w^*$; $u > 0$ is structural and persistent.
Mock answer (Cozzi tone)

Introduction. The efficiency-wage model explains why structural unemployment can persist in long-run equilibrium even with fully flexible wages. The key idea is that worker effort is not exogenous but responds to the wage paid: firms therefore have an incentive to set wages above the market-clearing level to elicit higher productivity, and the resulting wage rigidity creates involuntary unemployment as an equilibrium outcome.

Sketch. Let production be $Y = F(K, a(w)L)$ where $a(w)$ is the effort function, increasing and concave in the real wage. The firm chooses both the wage $w$ and the employment level $L$ to maximize profits, equivalent to minimizing the cost per efficiency unit $w/a(w)$ over $w$, and then choosing $L$ via the standard marginal-product condition. Taking the first-order condition with respect to $w$ gives $a(w) - w a'(w) = 0$, which rearranges to $w a'(w)/a(w) = 1$ — the famous Solow condition.

Consequences. The first Solow condition states that the elasticity of effort with respect to the wage equals one at the optimum: a 1% wage increase buys exactly 1% more effort. The second Solow condition $F_L = w^*$ then determines optimal employment given $w^*$. The radical consequence is that $w^*$ depends entirely on the shape of $a(\cdot)$ — on worker preferences and the labor-market environment that shapes shirking incentives — and not on the marginal supply and demand for labor. Any worker willing to accept $w^*$ but not hired is involuntarily unemployed, and unemployment persists in long-run equilibrium.

Intuition. Below the optimum $w < w^*$, raising the wage buys effort more than proportionally (elasticity $> 1$) — the firm gets cheaper effective labor and so wants to raise $w$. Above the optimum, the elasticity is less than 1 and the firm wants to cut $w$. The fixed point is exactly where the two pressures balance. The model implies that unemployment is structural rather than frictional: wage cuts by unemployed workers would not clear the market because firms internally prefer $w^*$. Comparative statics follow easily: a rise in unemployment benefits $b$ shifts the no-shirking constraint up, raising $w^*$ and reducing employment; increased product-market competition compresses firm profits and tightens the effort-wage trade-off, with effects on $u$ that depend on the specific functional form of $a(\cdot)$.

Quick pitfalls
  • The Solow condition is about effort elasticity, not about wage levels. The $w^*$ that emerges is whatever wage satisfies elasticity = 1.
  • Be precise about which Solow: the condition $w a'(w)/a(w) = 1$ is the first Solow condition; $F_L = w^*$ is the second. Cozzi treats both as a pair.
§ 08

Cross-cutting — Convergence Speeds Compared

comparison
The same algebra, three exponents. The convergence-speed formula has the same shape across Solow-family models — only the exponent on accumulable capital changes. This is the single most important cross-model connection.
ModelConvergence speed $\lambda$Calibration ($\alpha=1/3$, $n+g+\delta=0.06$)Match with data?
Basic Solow$(1-\alpha)(n+\delta)$$\approx 4\%$/yeartoo fast (data: 2%)
General Solow (w/ tech)$(1-\alpha)(n+g+\delta)$$\approx 4\%$/yearstill too fast
MRW (w/ human capital, $\alpha+\phi \approx 0.66$)$(1-\alpha-\phi)(n+g+\delta)$$\approx 2\%$/yearmatches

Why this matters. The whole empirical case for MRW is that the same parameter $1 - \alpha - \phi$ controls both convergence speed (which must be small to match data) and the elasticity of $y^*$ with respect to $s$ (which must be large to match cross-country dispersion). Reducing $1 - \alpha - \phi$ from $\approx 0.67$ to $\approx 0.34$ fixes both simultaneously. This is the single most elegant model-empirics connection in the course.

§ 09

Cross-cutting — Golden Rule Across Models

comparison
The Golden Rule maximizes steady-state consumption per worker. Across models, the result is the same form: saving rate equals the elasticity of output with respect to the corresponding accumulable factor.
ModelGolden RuleDerivation
Basic / General Solow$s^{**} = \alpha$Maximize $c^* = (1-s) y^*(s)$ wrt $s$; FOC gives $\text{MPK} = n + \delta$ (or $n+g+\delta$).
MRW (human capital)$s_K^{**} = \alpha,\; s_H^{**} = \phi$Separate FOCs for each saving rate; each equals the corresponding exponent.
Solow with land/oil$s^{**} = \alpha$ (still, for reproducible capital)Land and oil are non-reproducible — they enter the consumption-maximization problem only via their fixed/depleting paths.

Why this matters. The Golden Rule is one of the few results where extending the model just adds more conditions of the same form — saving rate = exponent. Cozzi loves this kind of structural elegance. Be ready to state $s^{**}$ for any model variant on the exam.

comparison
Three R&D growth models, each fixing a flaw in the previous. Romer 1990 has a scale effect (empirically wrong). Jones 1995 fixes it by reducing the idea-stock externality. Howitt-Peretto-Young + Cozzi hybrid eliminate scale via variety expansion.

Cross-cutting — Simulation Algorithm Template

reference
Sketch-the-simulation question appears in 5/5 past exams. The same algorithm shape works for every Solow-family model. Memorize the skeleton; for any specific model, just fill in the state variables and the production / accumulation rules.
Generic Solow-family simulation algorithm
(0)Set parameters and initial values$\alpha, n, \delta, s, \ldots$ and $k_0, A_0, R_0, \ldots$
(1)For each $t = 0, 1, \ldots, T$:loop body below
(a)Compute $Y_t$ from production fn at current state$Y_t = F(K_t, L_t, A_t, R_t, \ldots)$
(b)Compute derived outputs: $y_t = Y_t/L_t$, $c_t = (1-s)y_t$, growth ratesstore for plotting
(c)Update state variables$K_{t+1} = sY_t + (1-\delta)K_t$, $A_{t+1} = (1+g)A_t$, $L_{t+1} = (1+n)L_t$, $R_{t+1} = (1-s_E)R_t$, etc.
(2)Plot $y_t$, $c_t$, growth rate $(y_t - y_{t-1})/y_{t-1}$ over $t$identify steady state / BGP
(3)If shock: rerun with new parameters from $t = t_0$compare pre- and post-shock trajectories

Names and stylized facts

TermWhat it means
Kaldor's stylized factsSix long-run regularities the basic Solow model is built to match: (1) per-worker output grows roughly steadily; (2) capital per worker grows steadily; (3) capital-output ratio $K/Y$ is roughly constant; (4) real interest rate $r$ shows no trend; (5) labor and capital income shares are roughly constant; (6) growth rates differ substantially across countries.
Cozzi's seven stylized factsCozzi's M1 numbered list extends Kaldor's. Includes the falling labor share controversy, the rise of intangibles, and cross-country dispersion patterns. See Lecture 1a p19-22.
Solow residualThe growth-accounting term: the part of output growth NOT explained by capital and labor inputs. Interpreted as TFP growth, but really a measure of our ignorance.
Rate-of-convergence puzzleBasic Solow predicts $\lambda \approx 5\text{-}6\%$; data show $\approx 2\%$. The mismatch motivates MRW.
Investment-rate-effect puzzleBasic Solow predicts elasticity of $y^*$ to $s$ is $\alpha/(1-\alpha) \approx 0.5$; cross-country data show $\approx 1.5$. Also motivates MRW.
Absolute convergenceHypothesis that all countries converge to the SAME income level. Basic Solow does NOT predict this — only conditional convergence.
Conditional convergenceCountries converge to their OWN steady state, which depends on country-specific parameters. Solow's actual prediction.
Balanced growth path (BGP)A trajectory along which all variables grow at constant rates (possibly different from each other) and ratios like $K/Y$ are constant.
Feldstein-Horioka puzzleEmpirical regression of national investment on national saving across countries: should be flat in a fully open economy, but slope is close to 1. Used as a measure of capital immobility.
Euler's theorem (for CRS)If $F$ is homogeneous of degree 1, then $F_K K + F_L L = Y$. Combined with competitive factor markets, gives $rK + wL = Y$, i.e. factor payments exhaust output.
Hicks-neutral vs labor-augmenting tech$Y = BK^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}$ (Hicks: $B$ multiplies everything) vs $Y = K^\alpha (AL)^{1-\alpha}$ (labor-augmenting: $A$ multiplies only $L$). Only labor-augmenting is consistent with a Cobb-Douglas BGP under sustained growth.
Scale effect (in R&D models)Prediction that larger economies grow faster (more researchers $\to$ more ideas $\to$ more growth). Empirically false; motivates Jones (1995) and the M5 literature.
"Standing on shoulders"The idea-stock externality in R&D production: $\theta > 0$ in $\Delta A = \lambda L_A^\phi A^\theta$. Previous ideas make new ones easier.
"Stepping on toes"The duplication penalty in R&D: $\phi < 1$ in the same equation. More researchers means more duplication, diminishing marginal returns to $L_A$.
Non-rivalry of ideasIdeas can be used simultaneously by many agents without depletion — the foundation of endogenous growth. Distinguishes ideas from physical capital.
Golden RuleThe saving rate that maximizes steady-state consumption per worker. In basic Solow, $s^{**} = \alpha$, equivalent to MPK = $n+\delta$.
Cozzi 2017 hybrid threshold $\bar n$Population-growth threshold separating the truly-endogenous regime (below $\bar n$) from the semi-endogenous regime (above). Policy-affectable via the R&D portfolio split.
Solow conditions (efficiency wages)The pair (1) $w a'(w)/a(w) = 1$ and (2) $F_L = w^*$ that jointly determine the optimal wage and employment level in the efficiency-wage model.
Structural vs cyclical unemploymentStructural = long-run, from real wage rigidity (efficiency wages, trade unions, search frictions). Cyclical = short-run, from nominal rigidities. Out of scope (Ch 12+).
