Q2What it measures

Under Cobb-Douglas production with profit-maximizing firms in competitive factor markets, the labour income share $\dfrac{w_t L_t}{Y_t}$ is mathematically equal to which object?

A
The marginal product of labour, $\partial Y/\partial L$.
Confuses the (dimensionless) share with the marginal product itself, which has units of output per worker.
B
The labour-augmenting productivity term in the production function.
Confuses the elasticity (a Cobb-Douglas exponent) with the level term $B_t$ (TFP).
C
The output elasticity of labour, $(\partial Y/\partial L)(L/Y)$.
Recognises the income-share = elasticity result: under CRS + competition, factor share equals output elasticity (Euler decomposition).
D
The ratio of the real wage to the marginal product of capital.
Builds a plausible-looking ratio that does not correspond to any equilibrium object in the model.

Combine $w_t = F'_L$ (FOC) with the share definition: $w_t L_t / Y_t = F'_L \cdot L / F = $ output elasticity of $L$. For Cobb-Douglas $Y = BK^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}$, this equals the constant $1-\alpha$. The 'big lesson' of Lecture 1b's income-distribution slide is this elasticity-share identity, not just the numerical value $\approx 2/3$.

Q4Cross-topic

The basic (closed-economy) Solow model implies $r^* = \alpha(n+\delta)/s$ — countries with higher savings have lower steady-state interest rates. In the small-open-economy extension of Module 2, this $s \to r$ link is severed. Which mechanism is responsible?

A
The world interest rate $r^w$ exogenously pins down $k$, severing the $s \to r$ link.
Identifies the binding constraint of the small-open extension: $F_K(k) = r^w + \delta$ instantly fixes $k$, so $s$ no longer drives it.
B
The depreciation rate $\delta$ becomes endogenous when the economy opens to trade.
Picks 'something becomes endogenous' without identifying which — depreciation is exogenous in both models.
C
Cobb-Douglas no longer applies because aggregating traded goods changes the form.
Misattributes the change to functional form — Cobb-Douglas survives the open-economy extension intact.
D
Households become forward-looking, making $s$ endogenous to the world interest rate.
Imports a Ramsey/optimization framing — a different model. In small-open Solow, $s$ stays exogenous.

Open small-Solow lets capital flow at the world rate $r^w$. The FOC $F_K(k) = r^w + \delta$ pins $k$ in one period, regardless of domestic savings. What $s$ now controls is the path of the net foreign asset position $v - k$, not domestic $k$. This is the 'two-speed dynamics' result: $k$ jumps, $v$ crawls.

Connection: Closed Solow: $s$ pins $k^* = (Bs/(n+\delta))^{1/(1-\alpha)}$, which determines $r^*$. Open Solow: $r=r^w$ is given, so $k$ is pinned by the FOC instead; saving instead determines whether foreign assets accumulate. The two models share the same factor-price FOCs but differ in which variable adjusts to clear the capital market.
Q5Boundary

The labour income share $wL/Y$ is constant along the growth path.
Implied: $w$ grows at $g$ and $y$ grows at $g$, so $w/y$ — hence $wL/Y$ — is constant.

The rate of return on capital $r$ shows no long-run trend.
Stated directly in (iv).

Q6What it measures

In the basic Solow steady state, $r^* = \alpha(n+\delta)/s$. The parameter $\alpha$ that appears in this expression represents which object?

A
The output elasticity of capital, also equal to capital's income share.
Recognises the dual role of $\alpha$ in Cobb-Douglas: the exponent on $K$ is both the elasticity and (with profit maximization) the income share.
B
An aggregate of the depreciation rate and population growth.
Reads $\alpha$ as the $(n+\delta)$ factor that also appears in the formula — wrong slot.
C
The empirical value of the steady-state interest rate itself.
Confuses a parameter ($\alpha$) with the quantity being computed ($r^*$).
D
The discount rate households apply to future consumption.
Imports a Ramsey/preference parameter that does not exist in the basic Solow specification.

In Cobb-Douglas $Y = BK^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}$, the exponent $\alpha$ is the output elasticity of capital. Under CRS and competition (Euler + FOC), it is also capital's income share. Both roles enter the $r^*$ formula: $r_t = F'_K = \alpha B (K/L)^{\alpha-1}$, so at steady state $r^* = \alpha (n+\delta)/s$ after substituting $k^*$.

The Cobb-Douglas form of the aggregate production function $Y_t$.

Line 3 substitutes $K_{t+1} = k_{t+1} L_{t+1} = k_{t+1}(1+n)L_t$. That's exactly the labour-force law combined with the definition $k \equiv K/L$.

The profit-maximization first-order condition $F'_K(K_t,L_t) = r_t$.

Q9Boundary

The golden-rule savings rate $s^{**}$ is defined as the value of $s$ that maximises steady-state consumption per worker $c^*$. In the basic Solow model this equals $s^{**} = \alpha$. Which of the following is NOT a property of $s^{**}$?

A
At $s = s^{**}$, the marginal product of capital equals $n + \delta$.
Standard FOC of the golden rule: $\partial c^*/\partial s = 0$ gives $f'(k^*) = n+\delta$, equivalently $r^* = n+\delta$.
B
For $s < s^{**}$, raising $s$ permanently raises steady-state consumption $c^*$.
True — $c^*(s)$ is concave around $s^{**}$, so $s < s^{**}$ implies $\partial c^*/\partial s > 0$.
C
The economy is dynamically efficient if and only if $s = s^{**}$.
Wrong direction: $s = s^{**}$ maximises $c^*$, but dynamic efficiency holds for any $s \le s^{**}$. At $s < s^{**}$ the economy is still efficient — just consuming less than possible.
D
At $s = s^{**}$, capital's income share coincides with the savings rate.
True by construction: $s^{**} = \alpha$ = capital's share under Cobb-Douglas.

Dynamic efficiency means the economy is not over-accumulating (consumption couldn't be raised in every period by reducing $s$). This holds whenever $s \le s^{**}$, with strict inefficiency only for $s > s^{**}$. So 'iff' in (C) is too strong: $s = s^{**}$ is the consumption-maximising point, not the boundary between efficient and inefficient. (A), (B), (D) all follow directly from $s^{**} = \alpha$.

Q10Spot the flaw

A student writes: 'Solow predicts that if government consumption permanently rises so that $s$ falls, then on impact GDP per worker $y_t$ drops immediately. In the long run, $y_t$ falls further to a new lower steady state. So a fiscal expansion is contractionary in both the short and the long run.' What is wrong with this argument?

A
The long-run conclusion is correct; the short-run conclusion is wrong because $K_t, L_t$ are predetermined.
Catches the timing: capital and labour are state variables, so $y_t = BK_t^\alpha/L_t^{1-\alpha}$ is fixed on impact. The decline happens gradually during transition.
B
The short-run conclusion is correct; the long-run conclusion is wrong because households re-optimise $s$.
Mistakes Solow for Ramsey. In basic Solow $s$ is a parameter — there is no re-optimisation.
C
Both directions are right, but the student inverts the sign — a lower $s$ raises $y^*$.
Sign error: $y^* \propto s^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$ rises with $s$, so a lower $s$ unambiguously lowers $y^*$.
D
The argument is correctly stated and follows from the basic Solow model.
Misses the predetermined-state-variables point that is the key teaching of the comparative-analysis slide in Lecture 1c.

$Y_t = BK_t^\alpha L_t^{1-\alpha}$ depends only on state variables in period $t$. A change in $s$ does not shift $K_t$ or $L_t$ at the moment of impact — only savings (and hence $K_{t+1}$) drops. So $y_t$ is unchanged on impact; the lecture calls this 'full crowding out on impact'. During the transition $y_t$ falls toward the lower steady state. Lecture 1c labels the full-transition effect 'more than full crowding out'.

Q12Deep-short

Compared to the old steady state, the long-run real wage $w^*$ in the new steady state will be:

A
Higher, in direct proportion to the ratio of new to old $k^*$.
Treats $w^*$ as linear in $k^*$. But $w^* = (1-\alpha)Bk^{*\alpha}$ — concave, not linear.
B
Higher, by the factor $(s'/s)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$ — about 58% above the old level.
Correct elasticity: $\frac{d\ln w^*}{d\ln s} = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} = 1/2$, so $w^{*\prime}/w^* = (s'/s)^{1/2} = 2.5^{0.5} \approx 1.58$.
C
Unchanged, because $w^*$ depends only on technology and the labour force.
True that $w^*$ depends on $B$ — but via $k^*$, which itself depends on $s$. So $s$ does shift $w^*$.
D
Lower, because higher $s$ reduces the real interest rate $r^*$.
Confuses the joint movement: lower $r^*$ and higher $w^*$ both follow from higher $s$. They do not cancel — they are two sides of the same factor reallocation.

From $w^* = (1-\alpha) y^*$ and $y^* \propto s^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$, we get $\ln w^{*\prime} - \ln w^* = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}(\ln s' - \ln s) = \frac{1}{2}\ln(2.5) \approx 0.46$, i.e., $w^{*\prime}/w^* \approx e^{0.46} \approx 1.58$. The wage and the interest rate move in opposite directions, but both reflect the same diminishing-returns reallocation as $k$ rises.

Q13Boundary

Comparing the new steady state to the old, which quantity remains UNCHANGED?

A
The steady-state growth rate of $y_t$, namely $g^{y*}$.
Correct: in any basic-Solow steady state $g^{y*} = 0$. Saving raises levels, never the long-run growth rate.
B
The steady-state real wage $w^*$.
Wage rises with $k^*$ — see Q12.
C
The steady-state output per worker $y^*$.
$y^*$ rises by factor $(s'/s)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)} \approx 1.58$.
D
The steady-state real interest rate $r^*$.
$r^* = \alpha(n+\delta)/s$ falls when $s$ rises — strictly decreasing in $s$.

The 'level vs growth' lesson of basic Solow: $s$ shifts the level of the balanced-growth path but has zero long-run growth effect. In steady state $\Delta k = 0$, so $g^k = g^y = 0$ before and after. Levels $k^*, y^*, w^*$ all rise; $r^*$ falls; only the long-run growth rate of $y$ stays put.

Q15What it measures

In the basic Solow steady state, the rental rate of capital is $r^* = \alpha(n+\delta)/s$. The 'natural' real interest rate $\rho^*$ — the rate that compensates households for postponing consumption — relates to $r^*$ by which identity?

A
$\rho^* = r^* - \delta$, because $\rho^*$ is the net-of-depreciation real rate.
Lecture 1c slide: $\rho^* = \alpha(s/(n+\delta))^{-1} - \delta = r^* - \delta$. The interest rate nets out the wear and tear on capital.
B
$\rho^* = r^* + \delta$, because $r^*$ measures only the net rental of capital.
Sign inversion. Adds depreciation instead of subtracting; would imply $\rho^* > r^*$, which is the wrong ordering.
C
$\rho^* = r^*$, with depreciation absorbed entirely by the firm side.
Confuses interpretations: even when firms own capital, the relation between gross rental and net rate is still $\rho = r - \delta$.

Q16Derivation · source

The basic Solow model implies the steady-state identity $r^* = \alpha(n+\delta)/s$. Which derivation path correctly produces this identity?

A
Apply the household Euler condition $u'(c) = \beta(1+r)u'(c)$ at steady state.
Imports Ramsey machinery (intertemporal optimisation, Euler equation) absent from basic Solow.
B
Set $r^* = n+\delta$ via the golden-rule condition $s^{**} = \alpha$.
Confuses the golden rule (which fixes $s$ at a specific value) with the steady-state expression for $r^*$ (which holds at any $s$).
C
Combine the resource constraint $Y = C + I$ with $I = sY$ at steady state.
Uses identities that hold but do not pin down the level of $r^*$ or $k^*$.
D
Substitute the steady-state $k^*$ into the FOC $r_t = \alpha B k_t^{\alpha-1}$ and simplify.
Correct path: $r^* = \alpha B (k^*)^{\alpha-1}$ with $k^* = (Bs/(n+\delta))^{1/(1-\alpha)}$ simplifies (the $B$ cancels) to $\alpha(n+\delta)/s$.

The factor-price FOC gives $r_t = \alpha B (K/L)^{\alpha-1} = \alpha B k_t^{\alpha-1}$. Plug in $k^* = (Bs/(n+\delta))^{1/(1-\alpha)}$ and the $B$ factors cancel: $r^* = \alpha B \cdot (Bs/(n+\delta))^{-1} = \alpha (n+\delta)/s$. Note no preference parameters appear — this is a pure technology + savings result. (A) is the Ramsey derivation; (B) and (C) confuse special cases or identities with the general result.

Q17Cross-topic

The basic Solow model predicts an elasticity of $y^*$ with respect to $s$ of $\alpha/(1-\alpha) \approx 1/2$ (with $\alpha=1/3$). Cross-country regressions find a much larger empirical elasticity. MRW (1992) resolve this by extending production to $Y = K^\alpha H^\varphi (AL)^{1-\alpha-\varphi}$. Which feature of this extension drives the larger predicted elasticity?

A
The production function loses constant returns to scale when $H$ is added.
MRW keeps CRS — exponents still sum to 1. The fix is not about CRS.
B
Effective capital share becomes $\alpha+\varphi$, raising the saving-rate elasticity of $y^*$.
Correct mechanical link: $\frac{\partial \ln y^*}{\partial \ln s_K} = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha-\varphi}$, which exceeds basic-Solow $\alpha/(1-\alpha)$.
C
Human capital depreciates at a different rate than physical capital does.
Differential depreciation is a feature of some MRW variants but is not what drives the elasticity result.
D
Adding $H$ removes the diminishing returns to physical capital $K$.
Diminishing returns to $K$ still hold in MRW; the model just adds a second reproducible factor.

Both basic Solow and MRW have the same algebraic backbone: $y^*$ is a power function of saving rates, with the exponent equal to (capital share)/(1 - capital share). In basic Solow that share is $\alpha$; in MRW it's $\alpha + \varphi$ (broad capital). With $\alpha = \varphi = 1/3$, the elasticity rises from $\alpha/(1-\alpha) = 0.5$ to $\alpha/(1-\alpha-\varphi) = 1.0$ — large enough to fit the cross-country data without requiring an implausibly high physical-capital share.

Connection: Basic Solow: $\ln y^* = \text{const} + \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}[\ln s - \ln(n+\delta)]$. MRW: same structure but with $\alpha + \varphi$ replacing $\alpha$ in the denominator. The fix is not new physics — it's redefining 'capital' to include $H$, which mechanically raises the elasticity.
Q18Deep-short

In the basic Solow model, the firm's profit-maximisation conditions plus competitive markets imply $w_t L_t + r_t K_t = Y_t$ — total factor income exhausts output (no pure profits). This identity follows from which property of the production function?

A
Cobb-Douglas form with elasticities $\alpha$ and $1-\alpha$.
Cobb-Douglas is one CRS example, but the identity holds for any CRS function — not specifically Cobb-Douglas.
B
Diminishing marginal products of both factors.
Diminishing returns are independent; they don't imply an additive decomposition of $Y$.
C
Constant returns to scale (via Euler's theorem on homogeneous functions).
Correct: Euler's theorem for degree-1 homogeneous $F$ gives $F'_K K + F'_L L = F$. Combined with the FOCs $F'_K = r$, $F'_L = w$, this is exactly the no-pure-profits identity.
D
Strict concavity in $(K,L)$ jointly.
Concavity follows from diminishing returns but does not by itself imply Euler's identity (e.g., $F = K^{1/2} L^{1/3}$ is concave but not CRS).

Euler's theorem: if $F$ is homogeneous of degree 1 (CRS), then $K F'_K + L F'_L = F$. Under competition, $F'_K = r$ and $F'_L = w$, so $rK + wL = Y$. The result requires only CRS, not Cobb-Douglas specifically. This is the 'no pure profits' result on the income-distribution slide of Lecture 1b.

Q20Derivation · why

The basic Solow identity $r^* = \alpha(n+\delta)/s$ shows that the steady-state interest rate is decreasing in $s$. A naive intuition ('more savings means more demand for capital, so $r$ should rise') points the wrong way. Which mechanism actually explains the negative relationship?

A
Saving $S = sY$ reduces aggregate demand for capital, lowering $r$.
Reads $S = sY$ as a demand-side claim — but Solow's $S$ is the supply of saving (and hence of capital), not demand.
B
Depreciation $\delta$ is endogenously reduced by higher saving.
Depreciation is a fixed parameter in basic Solow; nothing makes it endogenous.
C
Higher $s$ raises $w^*$, which mechanically lowers $r^*$ via the Euler identity $wL + rK = Y$.
Euler's identity decomposes total income additively. The wage and rental rate both adjust to factor abundance, but $w^*$ does not 'mechanically lower' $r^*$ through this accounting identity.
D
Higher $s$ raises $k^*$, and the marginal product of capital $\alpha B k^{\alpha-1}$ falls as $k$ rises (diminishing returns).
Correct mechanism: more saving raises $k^*$; under diminishing returns the marginal product of capital — which by competition equals $r$ — falls. The 'price of capital' drops as capital becomes more abundant.

The chain is: higher $s$ → higher $k^*$ (more capital per worker) → lower $F'_K = r$ (diminishing returns). The supply of capital relative to labour rises, and its price drops — exactly the standard scarcity-price logic, applied to a stock that the savings rate determines.
