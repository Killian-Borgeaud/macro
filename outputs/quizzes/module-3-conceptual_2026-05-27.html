Q1Multi true-false

Mankiw, Romer and Weil (1992) augment the general Solow model with human capital. The production function is $Y_t = K_t^\alpha H_t^\phi (A_tL_t)^{1-\alpha-\phi}$ with $\alpha, \phi > 0$ and $\alpha+\phi < 1$. Let $\tilde k_t \equiv K_t/(A_tL_t)$ and $\tilde h_t \equiv H_t/(A_tL_t)$. Evaluate each statement.

Think through the setup first.
AIn per-effective-worker form, $\tilde y_t = \tilde k_t^\alpha \tilde h_t^\phi$.
Divide $Y = K^\alpha H^\phi (AL)^{1-\alpha-\phi}$ by $AL$: the exponents of $K$ and $H$ stay, the $(AL)^{1-\alpha-\phi}$ cancels against the divisor (correctly normalised). A standard MRW manipulation.
BThe labour share of national income is $1-\alpha-\phi$.
The labour share is $1-\alpha$, not $1-\alpha-\phi$. Workers receive both raw-labour compensation AND the return on human capital (since $H$ cannot be separated from $L$). Picked by students who mechanically read off the exponent on $(AL)$ as 'labour share'.
CThe model exhibits constant returns to $(K, H, L)$ jointly.
The sum of exponents on $K, H$, and $L$ (note: $L$ appears inside $(AL)^{1-\alpha-\phi}$, so its exponent is $1-\alpha-\phi$) is $\alpha + \phi + 1-\alpha-\phi = 1$. Standard replication argument.
DThere are decreasing returns to $K$ alone (holding $H, L, A$ fixed), but constant returns to $(K, H)$ jointly.
Holding $L$ fixed there are decreasing returns to $(K,H)$ jointly because $\alpha+\phi < 1$. Picked by students who forget that the constant-returns property holds over ALL three accumulable+labour inputs, not over capital subaggregates.
Pick T or F for each statement.

The cluster targets four common MRW confusions: (i) the mechanics of converting to effective-labour units, (ii) the subtle distinction that the labour share is $1-\alpha$ (since $H$'s return accrues to workers) and not $1-\alpha-\phi$, (iii) what 'constant returns' applies to, and (iv) the failure of CRS over capital subaggregates because $\alpha + \phi < 1$.

Q2Multi true-false

The MRW law of motion is a coupled pair of difference equations in $(\tilde k_t, \tilde h_t)$: $$\tilde k_{t+1} = \tfrac{1}{(1+n)(1+g)}\big[s_K \tilde k_t^\alpha \tilde h_t^\phi + (1-\delta)\tilde k_t\big], \quad \tilde h_{t+1} = \tfrac{1}{(1+n)(1+g)}\big[s_H \tilde k_t^\alpha \tilde h_t^\phi + (1-\delta)\tilde h_t\big].$$ Evaluate each statement.

AAt the steady state, $\tilde h^* / \tilde k^* = s_H / s_K$.
Setting $\Delta \tilde k = \Delta \tilde h = 0$ gives $s_K \tilde y^* = (n+g+\delta+ng) \tilde k^*$ and $s_H \tilde y^* = (n+g+\delta+ng) \tilde h^*$; divide one by the other and the common factor cancels.
BConvergence in the MRW model can be analysed with a single one-dimensional Solow diagram (as in basic Solow).
The state is two-dimensional $(\tilde k, \tilde h)$, requiring a phase diagram (Lecture 3a p17). Cozzi explicitly notes 'there is no easy diagrammatic way' to show convergence in one dimension. Picked by students projecting basic-Solow intuition.
CThe two $\Delta\tilde k = 0$ and $\Delta\tilde h = 0$ loci intersect at a unique positive point.
Both loci pass through the origin and are upward-sloping; the relative slopes (one steeper than the other) ensure a unique positive crossing. Cozzi verifies this analytically and via simulation (p15-18).
DIn steady state, $\tilde k_t$ and $\tilde h_t$ both grow at rate $g$.

Targets: (i) the symmetric structure $\tilde h^*/\tilde k^* = s_H/s_K$ from dividing the two steady-state equations; (ii) that MRW dynamics live in 2D, not 1D; (iii) the unique positive steady-state intersection; (iv) the per-effective-worker variables are stationary, while the level variables grow at $g$.

Q3Multi true-false

Solving the MRW steady-state system gives $y_t^* = A_t \big(s_K^\alpha s_H^\phi / (n+g+\delta)^{\alpha+\phi}\big)^{1/(1-\alpha-\phi)}$ (ignoring the tiny $ng$ cross term). Take $\alpha = \phi = 1/3$ as the canonical calibration. Evaluate each statement about the elasticities of $y^*$ wrt parameters.

Think through the setup first.
AThe elasticity of $y^*$ with respect to $s_K$ is $\alpha/(1-\alpha-\phi) \approx 1$.
Take logs: $\ln y^* = \text{const} + \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha-\phi}\ln s_K + \cdots$. With $\alpha = 1/3, \alpha+\phi = 2/3$: $(1/3)/(1/3) = 1$. Roughly twice the basic-Solow figure of $\alpha/(1-\alpha) = 0.5$.
BThe elasticity of $y^*$ with respect to $s_H$ has the same sign and roughly the same magnitude as the elasticity wrt $s_K$.
By the symmetry of the steady-state formula, the $s_H$ elasticity is $\phi/(1-\alpha-\phi)$; with $\alpha = \phi$ it equals the $s_K$ elasticity. This is exactly Cozzi's conclusion 'the effect of $s_H$ is of the same size as the effect of $s_K$' (p20).
CThe elasticity of $y^*$ wrt $(n+g+\delta)$ is approximately $-0.5$ (same as basic Solow).
It is $-(\alpha+\phi)/(1-\alpha-\phi) \approx -2$, FOUR times the basic-Solow value. Picked by students who don't apply the elasticity formula to MRW separately.
DThese elasticities resolve the cross-country investment-rate puzzle that the basic Solow model could not explain.
The empirical cross-country slope of $\ln y$ on $\ln s$ is around 1.5 (Lecture 2b regression); basic Solow predicts 0.5 (too small); MRW predicts ~1 (much closer). That's the main empirical motivation for adding $H$.
Pick T or F for each statement.

All four elasticity facts come from the same log-differentiation of the steady-state $y^*$ formula. The qualitative jump from basic Solow ($\alpha/(1-\alpha) \approx 0.5$) to MRW (~1) is doubled, and the $n+g+\delta$ elasticity is quadrupled — both shifts move predictions toward observed cross-country patterns.

Q4Multi true-false

The MRW Golden Rule maximises steady-state consumption per worker over $(s_K, s_H)$. Cozzi states (Lecture 3a p20): $s_K^{**} = \alpha$, $s_H^{**} = \phi$. Evaluate each statement.

Think through the setup first.
AAt the MRW Golden Rule, the total share of output saved is $\alpha + \phi$.
$s_K^{**} + s_H^{**} = \alpha + \phi \approx 2/3$ under canonical calibration — much higher than the basic-Solow Golden Rule of $\alpha \approx 1/3$. The model recommends saving twice as much when human-capital investment is taken seriously.
B$s_K^{**} = \alpha$ in MRW for exactly the same reason that $s^{**} = \alpha$ in basic Solow: each one is the output elasticity of the corresponding capital.
The Golden Rule equates the marginal product of the relevant capital, net of depreciation, to the effective growth rate; by Cobb-Douglas, MPK = $\alpha y/k$. Solving gives $s = \alpha$. Same derivation, applied separately to each capital good.
CUnder the MRW Golden Rule, the consumption share of output is $1-\alpha-\phi$, which is approximately the labour share.
$c = (1 - s_K - s_H)y = (1 - \alpha - \phi)y \approx 1/3$. Numerically this equals the share NOT going to either capital — a clean parallel to basic Solow where Golden-Rule consumption equals the labour share.
DBecause both Golden-Rule conditions are met at $s_K = \alpha, s_H = \phi$, real-world economies that achieve the Golden Rule for physical capital also achieve it for human capital.
The two are INDEPENDENT structural parameters; achieving one says nothing about the other. Empirically OECD physical-capital savings rates ($\sim 20$-$25\%$) are below $\alpha \approx 1/3$; education spending is even further from $\phi \approx 1/3$. Picked by students who think 'Golden Rule' is a binary state.
Pick T or F for each statement.

The Golden Rule applies separately to each accumulable factor, giving $s_K^{**} = \alpha$, $s_H^{**} = \phi$. Total Golden-Rule saving is $\alpha + \phi$ (≈ 2/3 under canonical calibration), and the remaining consumption share matches the labour share — a structural symmetry between the two-capital MRW and the one-capital basic Solow.

Q6Multi true-false

The Solow model with land (Lecture 3b) uses $Y_t = K_t^\alpha (A_tL_t)^\beta X^\kappa$ with $\alpha + \beta + \kappa = 1$ and $X$ a fixed factor. The balanced-growth rate of GDP per capita is $g^y = (\beta g - \kappa n)/(\beta + \kappa)$. Evaluate each statement.

Think through the setup first.
AThe 'classical case' of the model ($g = 0$, $n > 0$) predicts $g^y < 0$ — Smith/Ricardo/Malthus pessimism is mechanically built in.
Plug $g=0$: $g^y = -\kappa n / (\beta+\kappa) < 0$. With limited land and growing population, the land-per-worker $X/L$ falls, and constant returns to $K, L$ alone do not exist (it's CRS to $K,L,X$ jointly). Cozzi's growth-drag intuition.
BThe 'modern case' ($g > 0$, $n = 0$) predicts $g^y = g$ exactly — technology fully overcomes the land constraint.
$g^y = \beta g/(\beta+\kappa) < g$ — there's still a drag, just a smaller one. With $\beta = 0.6, \kappa = 0.2$: $g^y = 0.75g$, i.e., 25% of technology's effect is 'lost' to diminishing returns. Picked by students who think zero population growth eliminates the drag entirely.
CAbundance of land (high $X/L$) raises the LEVEL of the steady-state growth path but not the growth RATE.
From the per-capita production function, $y_t$ depends on $x_t = X/L_t$ to the power $\kappa$ — a level effect. The growth-rate formula $g^y = (\beta g - \kappa n)/(\beta+\kappa)$ does not contain $X$. Cozzi explicitly states 'it is good to have plenty of land' for levels (p15).
DFor typical OECD parameters ($g = 2\%$, $n = 1\%$, $\beta = 0.6$, $\kappa = 0.2$), the model predicts negative per-capita growth.
$g^y = (0.6 \cdot 0.02 - 0.2 \cdot 0.01)/0.8 = (0.012 - 0.002)/0.8 = 0.0125$ = 1.25% — positive. Cozzi's whole point: 'the classics were right' but technological progress saves us in practice. Picked by students who don't plug in actual numbers.
Pick T or F for each statement.

Targets: classical pessimism falls out of the model when $g=0$; the modern case still has a drag (just smaller); abundance of land affects levels, not growth rates; and with realistic Western parameters, technological progress wins.

Q7Multi true-false

The Solow model with land AND oil adds an exhaustible resource: $Y_t = K_t^\alpha (A_tL_t)^\beta X^\kappa E_t^\epsilon$ with energy $E_t = s_E R_t$ extracted from a finite reserve $R_t = (1-s_E)^t R_0$. Evaluate each statement.

Think through the setup first.
AThe reserve depletes at a constant geometric rate $s_E$ per period, so $R_t \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$.
$R_{t+1} = R_t - E_t = R_t - s_E R_t = (1-s_E)R_t$. Standard geometric decay. For $s_E = 0.005$ per year, the reserve halves every $\ln 2 / s_E \approx 139$ years.
BThe balanced-growth rate of $y$ subtracts a growth drag $\epsilon \cdot s_E / (\beta + \kappa + \epsilon)$ on top of the land drag.
Energy use shrinks at rate $s_E$ (since $g_E = -s_E$), which contributes a negative term $-\epsilon s_E$ inside the same divisor $1 - \alpha = \beta + \kappa + \epsilon$. Cozzi writes the full formula on p20-21.
CRaising the extraction rate $s_E$ unambiguously raises steady-state per-capita growth $g^y$, because more energy means more output.
FALSE — raising $s_E$ depletes the reserve FASTER, lowering long-run $g_E$ and hence lowering $g^y$. The instantaneous output flow is higher, but the BGP growth rate is lower. Picked by students confusing levels with growth rates.
DWith $\alpha = 0.2$, $\beta = 0.6$, $\kappa = \epsilon = 0.1$, $g = 2\%$, $n = 1\%$, $s_E = 0.005$, the model still predicts positive long-run per-capita growth.
$g^y = (\beta g - \kappa n - \epsilon(n+s_E))/(\beta+\kappa+\epsilon) = (0.012 - 0.001 - 0.0015)/0.8 = 0.00963$ = ~1%. Positive — Cozzi calls this 'growth optimism' (p21).
Pick T or F for each statement.

Oil depletes geometrically, and the depletion shows up as an extra growth drag inside the BGP formula. Faster extraction worsens the drag (counterintuitive for those thinking in flow terms). Calibrated Western values still leave room for positive long-run growth — but developing-country population growth can flip the sign.

Q8Multi true-false

Cozzi argues (Lecture 3a p21-22) that subsidising education or physical-capital investment requires a market-imperfection justification. Evaluate each statement about which imperfections justify public subsidies for education.

Think through the setup first.
AImperfect credit markets justify education subsidies because adverse selection limits poor students' borrowing capacity.
Credit-market frictions mean even high-return-to-education students cannot borrow against future earnings. Cozzi lists this as the first imperfection (p22).
BImperfect insurance against future income risk discourages individuals from over-investing in education, justifying public action.
Moral hazard prevents private full-income insurance, so risk-averse students under-invest. Listed second on Cozzi's p22 list.

This is the core trap. The Solow model gives a Golden Rule LEVEL for $s_H$ but says nothing about whether private decisions DELIVER that level. Q9Boundary

The MRW Solow-with-human-capital model resolves two of the basic Solow model's empirical problems. Which of the following is NOT addressed by MRW?

Try answering in your head first.
A
The cross-country slope of $\ln y$ on $\ln s$ being larger than basic Solow predicts.
MRW does address this — raising broad-capital elasticity from $\alpha$ to $\alpha+\phi$ approximately doubles the predicted slope.
B
The observed cross-country rate of convergence being slower than the basic Solow model predicts.
MRW also addresses this — the convergence formula becomes $\lambda = (1-\alpha-\phi)(n+g+\delta) \approx 2\%$, matching observation.
C
Why countries differ in their savings rates $s_K$ and $s_H$ and in their rate of technological progress $g$.
Correct — MRW takes $s_K, s_H, g$ as exogenous parameters and offers no theory of WHY they differ. Cozzi (p31) is explicit: 'important parameters are unexplained.'
D
The strong negative empirical effect of $n$ on $y$.
MRW addresses this too — the elasticity of $y^*$ wrt $(n+g+\delta)$ rises in magnitude from $-0.5$ to $-2$, matching the strong observed negative cross-country correlation.

MRW resolves both Solow puzzles via one structural change. What it leaves untouched is the determination of the structural parameters themselves — that's the gap endogenous-growth theory (Modules 4-5) tries to fill. The Cozzi quote 'we have understood almost everything, and yet almost nothing' captures exactly this boundary.

Q10Spot the flaw

A student argues: 'The MRW model adds human capital and gets sustained long-run growth in $y$ even though all capital experiences diminishing returns. So MRW is an endogenous growth model — growth is generated from inside the model.' What is wrong with this argument?

Try answering in your head first.
A
The argument is correct — MRW is the simplest example of an endogenous growth model and is the prototype for Module 4's R&D-based models.
Tempting because MRW does generate higher and more realistic growth predictions, but it's not endogenous growth — the long-run rate is still pinned by exogenous $g$.
B
In MRW, the long-run growth rate of $y$ is still $g$ — the exogenous technological progress rate. The model explains LEVELS better than basic Solow, but not the existence of growth itself.
Correct. $y_t^* = A_t (\cdot)^{1/(1-\alpha-\phi)}$ grows at the rate of $A_t$, which is $g$ — exogenous. MRW raises the level of the growth path; endogenous growth would mean the rate itself emerges from inside.
C
MRW's long-run growth rate is $g/(1-\alpha-\phi)$, which is endogenous because it depends on $\phi$.
Wrong formula. The growth rate of $y$ is $g$ exactly, not $g/(1-\alpha-\phi)$. The elasticity formula confuses level elasticity with growth-rate determination.
D
The argument is wrong because human capital exhibits constant returns when combined with physical capital, contradicting diminishing returns.
Misstates the production function: $K^\alpha H^\phi (AL)^{1-\alpha-\phi}$ has diminishing returns to capital subaggregates, exactly as the student noted. The flaw is elsewhere.

The critical distinction: MRW improves LEVEL predictions (the steady-state level of the growth path) but the long-run GROWTH RATE is still $g$ — the exogenous technology trend. Endogenous growth (Module 4+) means the rate itself is determined inside the model. This distinction is the hinge between Solow-style and Romer-style growth theory.

Q11What it measures

In the MRW analysis, two human-capital symbols appear: $h_t \equiv H_t/L_t$ (human capital per worker) and $\tilde h_t \equiv H_t/(A_tL_t)$ (human capital per effective worker). What is $\tilde h_t$?

Try answering in your head first.
A
The amount of human capital per unit of effective labour input, used to make the dynamic system stationary along the BGP.
Correct interpretation. Just as $\tilde k_t = k_t/A_t$ in the general Solow model makes the system stationary by normalising out the technology trend, $\tilde h_t$ does the same for human capital — both $\tilde k$ and $\tilde h$ converge to constants in steady state.
B
The total human capital embodied in a single representative worker, including learning-by-doing.
That's $h_t$ (per-worker), not $\tilde h_t$. Picked by students reading the tilde as a 'fancier' version of $h$ without understanding the normalisation.
C
Human capital adjusted for inflation, expressed in real terms.
All Solow-model variables are already in real terms; $A_t$ is productivity, not a price index. Confusion with finance-style notation.
D
The marginal product of human capital in equilibrium.
That would be a price/return; $\tilde h_t$ is a quantity. Picked by students who associate tildes with shadow prices.

The tilde normalisation $\tilde X_t \equiv X_t/(A_tL_t)$ is the standard trick that makes a system with persistent technological growth analytically tractable: in steady state $\tilde X^*$ is constant, even though $X_t$ itself grows. Once you see this, the entire MRW phase-diagram analysis becomes a generalisation of the Lecture 2b general-Solow approach.

Q12Deep-short

B
...$2$ — MRW convergence is twice as fast as basic Solow.

C

Q13Numeric

Suppose the empirical cross-country convergence speed is $\lambda = 2\%$ per year, and $n + g + \delta = 8\%$ per year. The MRW formula is $\lambda = (1 - \alpha - \phi)(n+g+\delta)$. What is the implied broad-capital elasticity $\alpha + \phi$?

A
$\alpha + \phi = 0.25$
Would give $\lambda = 0.75 \cdot 0.08 = 6\%$, much too high. Picked by students who solve $(1-x) \cdot 0.08 = 0.02$ as $1 - x = 0.25$ instead of correctly getting $1-x = 0.02/0.08 = 0.25$ and then $x = 0.75$. The arithmetic-direction error.
B
$\alpha + \phi = 0.5$
Gives $\lambda = 0.5 \cdot 0.08 = 4\%$, twice the observed value. Picked by students who pick a 'round' middle value without solving.

Close to MRW's canonical calibration ($\alpha = \phi = 1/3$, so $\alpha + \phi = 2/3$) but gives $\lambda \approx 0.33 \cdot 0.08 = 2.7\%$ — too high by a third. Picked by students who recall the canonical $\alpha = \phi = 1/3$ but don't check the calibration.

Q14Cross-topic

A
Adding labour-augmenting technology $A_t$ to the production function, which makes the convergence formula $(1-\alpha)(n+g+\delta+ng)$ — slightly slower due to the cross term.
This is the basic-to-general Solow step (Module 2 itself), not the MRW step. The $ng$ term is empirically tiny and does not close the 5%-vs-2% gap. Picked by students who confuse Module 2's reformulation with Module 3's extension.
B
Replacing the Cobb-Douglas with a CES production function, which mechanically lowers the convergence speed when elasticity of substitution is below 1.
Mathematically possible but not what MRW does. MRW keeps Cobb-Douglas and adds a new factor.
C
Adding human capital as a separate accumulable factor, raising 'broad capital' elasticity from $\alpha$ to $\alpha+\phi$ and making the convergence coefficient $(1-\alpha-\phi)$ smaller — exactly halving $\lambda$ at canonical calibration.
Correct. The mechanism is that the convergence speed depends on the elasticity of OUTPUT with respect to all DIMINISHING-RETURNS capital factors taken jointly. Adding $H$ shrinks the gap between $\alpha+\phi$ and $1$, slowing decay.

Choice D fails because the structural-vs-income-share consistency requirement forbids it.

MRW resolves this by re-interpreting that $\alpha$ as $\alpha + \phi$, where $\phi$'s return accrues to workers (preserving labour share = $1-\alpha$) but enters the diminishing-returns formula. Same mathematical fix, structurally legitimate.

B
Substitution of $\tilde y^* = \tilde k^{*\alpha}\tilde h^{*\phi}$ into both equations.
Plausible-sounding but wrong — line (3) does NOT substitute the production function (the $\tilde y^*$ symbol still appears on both sides). Picked by students who pattern-match on 'something must use the production function'.
C
Application of L'Hôpital's rule at the steady-state intersection.
Irrelevant — no limit is being taken. Picked by students who confuse linear-system manipulation with calculus.
D
Term-by-term division of equation (1) by equation (2), valid because $\tilde y^*$ and $(n+g+\delta+ng)$ are both nonzero.
Correct. The step is a legitimate algebraic division as long as the divisors are nonzero — and both are guaranteed positive at any economically meaningful steady state.

Line (3) is pure algebra — dividing the two steady-state conditions. The key insight: the common factors $\tilde y^*$ and $(n+g+\delta+ng)$ cancel cleanly, leaving the ratio $\tilde k^*/\tilde h^* = s_K/s_H$. This holds for ANY parameter values, not just Golden-Rule ones — that's why it's a useful intermediate identity for solving the steady-state system.

Setup. Country X uses the Solow model with land and oil: $Y_t = K_t^\alpha (A_tL_t)^\beta X^\kappa E_t^\epsilon$ with $\alpha = 0.2$, $\beta = 0.6$, $\kappa = \epsilon = 0.1$. With the chain's parameters and an extraction rate $s_E = 0.005$, what is the predicted per-capita growth rate $g^y$? (Round to two decimal places of a percent.)

Try answering in your head first.
A
$g^y \approx 2.00\%$ — the BGP growth rate equals $g$.
Wrong — that would be true only if there were no drags. Picked by students who treat technology as fully effective.
B
$g^y \approx 1.50\%$ — close to $0.75g$.
Wrong — that's the LAND-only model's prediction with $n=0$. Doesn't account for the additional energy and population drags.
C
$g^y \approx 1.19\%$.
Correct. $g^y = (0.6 \cdot 0.02 - 0.1 \cdot 0.01 - 0.1 \cdot (0.01 + 0.005))/0.8 = (0.012 - 0.001 - 0.0015)/0.8 = 0.0095/0.8 = 0.0119 = 1.19\%$.
D
$g^y \approx -0.50\%$ — the depletion drag dominates.
Wrong — depletion ($\epsilon s_E$) contributes only $-0.000625/0.8 = -0.078\%$. Picked by students overweighting the energy drag.

Plug-and-evaluate: $g^y = (0.012 - 0.001 - 0.0015)/0.8 = 0.0119$. The breakdown: technology contributes $0.012/0.8 = 1.5\%$; land drag $-0.125\%$; energy+population drag $-0.1875\%$. Net ~1.19%. Despite finite resources, sustained positive growth is feasible.

Q18Deep-short

Continuing from Q17. If country X DOUBLES its extraction rate to $s_E = 0.010$ (mining the oil twice as fast), what happens to the long-run per-capita growth rate $g^y$?

Try answering in your head first.
A
$g^y$ rises — more energy in production raises output growth.
Wrong. Higher $s_E$ raises CURRENT energy flow but depletes the reserve faster; the long-run BGP rate $g^y$ depends on the depletion rate $s_E$ negatively through the term $-\epsilon(n+s_E)$.
B
$g^y$ falls by $\epsilon \cdot 0.005 / (\beta+\kappa+\epsilon) = 0.0625$ percentage points.
Correct. The derivative $\partial g^y / \partial s_E = -\epsilon/(\beta+\kappa+\epsilon) = -0.125$. Doubling $s_E$ from 0.005 to 0.010 adds $\Delta s_E = 0.005$, so $\Delta g^y = -0.125 \cdot 0.005 = -0.000625 = -0.0625$ pp.
C
$g^y$ is unchanged — the BGP rate depends only on technology $g$.
Wrong — that's the basic Solow / general Solow result, NOT the Solow-with-oil result. The whole point of Chapter 7 is that resource extraction enters the growth-rate formula.
D
$g^y$ doubles — extraction effort directly scales output growth.
Wrong in both direction (higher extraction lowers growth) and magnitude (effect is small in percentage-point terms).

Higher $s_E$ depletes the reserve faster, which lowers the long-run energy flow growth from $-s_E$ to $-2s_E$. In the BGP formula, this enters as $-\epsilon s_E/(\beta+\kappa+\epsilon)$. The Cozzi take-away: faster extraction trades short-run output for long-run growth. The optimal extraction policy is a non-trivial question (touched on in Hotelling-type analyses outside this course).

Q20Deep-short

In the Solow-with-land model, $g^y = (\beta g - \kappa n)/(\beta+\kappa)$. The 'classical case' ($g = 0$) and the 'modern case' ($n = 0$) are stylised limits. Which statement correctly captures both?

Try answering in your head first.
A
Classical case predicts $g^y = 0$ (Smith equilibrium); modern case predicts $g^y = g$ (technology fully offsets land).
Wrong on both — classical gives strictly negative growth (Malthus's pessimism); modern still has the $\beta/(\beta+\kappa) < 1$ drag.
B
Classical case predicts $g^y = -\kappa n/(\beta+\kappa) < 0$ (Malthusian decline); modern case predicts $g^y = \beta g/(\beta+\kappa) < g$ (technology partially blunted by land scarcity).
Correct. Classical case: with no technological progress and positive population, growing labour presses on fixed land and per-capita output must fall. Modern case: with no population growth and positive $g$, technology raises output, but per-capita gain is less than $g$ because of the diminishing-returns drag.
C
Classical case predicts $g^y < 0$ (correct); modern case predicts $g^y > g$ because zero population growth amplifies per-capita gains.
Wrong on modern — $g^y$ is bounded above by $g$, never exceeds it; $\beta/(\beta+\kappa) < 1$ since $\kappa > 0$. Picked by students applying basic Solow intuition where $n=0$ removes the dilution drag.
D
Both cases predict $g^y$ depends only on the labour-share parameter $\beta$, not on $\kappa$.
Wrong — $\kappa$ appears in both numerator (classical case) and denominator (both cases). Land's exponent matters for the magnitude of the drag.

The two limits decompose into Malthusian decline (classical) and partially-realised technology (modern). The key insight is that even when $g > 0$, land scarcity caps the per-capita gain at $g \cdot \beta/(\beta+\kappa) < g$ — never recovering the full technological progress rate. Cozzi's running theme: 'the classics were right' about the structural pressure, but historical Western experience escaped it because $g$ exceeded the relevant thresholds.
