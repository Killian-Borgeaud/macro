Q1Multi true-false

In the productive-externality model (Ch 8), the individual firm's production function is $y_t^d = (K_t^d)^\alpha (A_t L_t^d)^{1-\alpha}$ with $A_t = K_t^\varphi$ in equilibrium, where the firm treats $A_t$ as given. Evaluate each statement.

Think through the setup first.
AAt the firm level, the production function has constant returns to scale in $(K_t^d, L_t^d)$.
Replication argument: with $A_t$ fixed, doubling firm inputs doubles firm output. This is what allows perfect competition with zero pure profits to coexist with the aggregate spillover.
BAt the aggregate level (with $A_t = K_t^\varphi$) the production function exhibits increasing returns when $\varphi > 0$.

CThe equilibrium capital share of national income equals $\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)$.

Pick T or F for each statement.

The cluster targets four core micro-features of the externality model: (i) firm-level CRS via replication; (ii) aggregate IRS as a consequence of the spillover; (iii) the subtle point that capital share equals $\alpha$ (firm exponent), not $\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)$ (aggregate exponent), because the externality is uncompensated; (iv) the atomistic-firm assumption that generates the externality in the first place. Item (iii) is the most common error — and the reason this model preserves perfect competition despite the spillover.

The three regimes are the conceptual spine of Ch 8. Reducing $\varphi$ smoothly walks the model from basic Solow ($\varphi=0$, no growth) to semi-endogenous ($0<\varphi<1$, needs $n>0$) to AK ($\varphi=1$, growth from $s$). This is the definition of endogenous growth. Always implied.
B

If $g$ depends on parameters and policy moves parameters, then policy affects $g$. Implied.
C

Q4What it measures

In the productive-externality model, $A_t = K_t^\varphi$. The parameter $\varphi$ represents:

Try answering in your head first.
A
Capital's share of aggregate national income.
Wrong: capital share equals $\alpha$ from firm-level factor pricing; $\varphi$ is unrelated to functional distribution. Students conflate spillover strength with income shares.
B
The endogenous growth rate of aggregate technology.
Wrong: $g_A$ is an OUTPUT of the model, not a parameter. Confuses the parameter governing spillover strength with the growth rate it endogenises.
C
Elasticity of $A_t$ with respect to aggregate $K_t$.
CORRECT — by direct differentiation of $A_t = K_t^\varphi$: $\partial \ln A_t / \partial \ln K_t = \varphi$. This is the spillover strength.
D

$\varphi$ is the elasticity of technology with respect to aggregate capital — by definition of the spillover function $A_t = K_t^\varphi$. It does NOT equal capital's income share (which is $\alpha$), the growth rate of technology (an endogenous output), or anything about savings.

Q5Multi true-false

The AK model (Ch 8, $\varphi=1$) assumes constant population, so $A = L^{1-\alpha}$ is a constant, and output is $Y_t = AK_t$. Evaluate each statement.

Think through the setup first.
A$k_t$ converges to a constant steady state $k^*$ analogous to the basic Solow steady state.
FALSE — no convergence. $k_{t+1}/k_t = 1 + sA - \delta$ is constant, so $k_t$ grows at rate $g_e$ forever. Tempting because students reflexively look for a steady state.
BA permanent increase in $s$ permanently raises the growth rate of $y_t$.
$g_e = sA - \delta$ depends positively on $s$. This is the most plausible empirical prediction of AK — Bernanke-Gürkaynak supports it across countries.
CA larger constant population $L$ produces a higher long-run growth rate.
$A = L^{1-\alpha}$, so bigger $L$ → bigger $A$ → bigger $g_e = sA-\delta$. This is the (empirically problematic) scale effect — motivation for Module 5.
DThe transitional dynamics resemble the general Solow model with exogenous tech: $\tilde k_t$ approaches a steady state at a positive convergence rate.
FALSE — AK has NO transition: $k_t$ jumps onto the BGP immediately and grows at $g_e$ forever. Students conflate 'balanced growth' with 'transitional convergence'.
Pick T or F for each statement.

AK is the simplest truly-endogenous model. Its defining features: (i) no steady-state level for $k$ — only a balanced-growth path; (ii) $s$ affects long-run growth (good); (iii) scale effect in $L$ (bad — empirically refuted); (iv) zero convergence dynamics. Distinguishing AK's BGP from the general Solow's BGP is essential — both feature constant-rate growth, but only general Solow has convergence in $\tilde k$.

Q8What it measures

In Romer's R&D production function $\Delta A_t = \rho A_t^\varphi L_{At}^\lambda$, the parameter $\lambda < 1$ captures:

Try answering in your head first.
A
The 'standing on shoulders' effect: prior ideas boost research.
Wrong attribution. Standing on shoulders is captured by $\varphi > 0$, not $\lambda$. Students conflate the two extras in the R&D function.
B
The 'stepping on toes' effect: research suffers from duplication.
CORRECT — $\lambda < 1$ means doubling $L_{At}$ less than doubles new ideas. Multiple researchers chase overlapping ideas; some effort is duplicated.
C
The 'fishing-out' effect: easy ideas exhausted before hard ones.
Wrong attribution. Fishing out is $\varphi < 0$, not $\lambda < 1$. Students who learned the three effects but didn't memorise which parameter each maps to pick here.
D
The rate at which existing ideas become obsolete.
Wrong — ideas don't depreciate in this model. Only physical capital does.

The two non-unity exponents in $\Delta A_t = \rho A_t^\varphi L_{At}^\lambda$ map to two distinct phenomena: $\varphi$ governs how prior knowledge feeds new research (standing on shoulders if $\varphi > 0$, fishing out if $\varphi < 0$), while $\lambda$ governs returns to research labour (stepping on toes if $\lambda < 1$). Don't conflate them — both are tested in the MTF that follows.

The R&D production function compresses three distinct ideas into two parameters: $\varphi$ governs the role of accumulated knowledge (shoulders / fishing out), $\lambda$ governs the role of contemporaneous research labour (toes). Try answering in your head first.
A
The basic Solow model with no technology growth.
Basic Solow has no $g_A$ at all, so $g_t$ isn't even defined. Picked by students conflating 'no growth' with 'constant zero growth'.
B
The general Solow model with exogenous $g$.
General Solow has $g_A$ constant BY ASSUMPTION, not by convergence. The stem says $g_t$ has 'stopped CHANGING' — implies dynamics, not exogenous fixity.
C

D

Q11Multi true-false

Compare the semi-endogenous Romer model ($\varphi < 1$, $n > 0$) and the strictly endogenous Romer model ($\varphi = 1$, $n = 0$). Evaluate each statement.

Think through the setup first.
ASemi-endogenous Romer requires $n > 0$ to deliver positive long-run growth.
$g_{se} = (1+n)^{\lambda/(1-\varphi)} - 1$ collapses to zero if $n=0$. Population growth is the scale-increasing channel that sustains endogenous growth despite diminishing returns in idea creation.
BStrictly endogenous Romer requires $n = 0$ to avoid accelerating growth.
With $\varphi = 1$ and $n > 0$ the model becomes explosive — growth rate of $g_t$ increases without bound. Imposing $n=0$ keeps the model tractable and matches Romer's original formulation.
CIn BOTH regimes, a permanent increase in $s_R$ raises the long-run growth rate.
FALSE — only in strictly endogenous ($g_e = \rho(s_R L)^\lambda$ rises with $s_R$). Semi-endogenous $g_{se} = (1+n)^{\lambda/(1-\varphi)}-1$ is independent of $s_R$. This level-vs-growth confusion is the single most exam-tested point.
DStrictly endogenous Romer predicts that larger countries (higher $L$) grow faster — the scale effect.
$g_e = \rho(s_R L)^\lambda$ rises with $L$. Empirically false (large countries don't grow faster) — this is why Module 5 introduces 'no scale effects' refinements.
Pick T or F for each statement.

The semi-vs-strictly endogenous distinction is structural: semi-endogenous uses population growth to feed endogenous tech growth (giving $g_{se}$ that depends only on $n, \lambda, \varphi$), while strictly endogenous uses constant population at $\varphi=1$ to break the diminishing-returns curse (giving $g_e$ that depends on $s_R$ and $L$). Statement C is the single highest-value Module 4 mistake — students reflexively assume more R&D effort always means more growth.

Q12Deep-short

In the AK model, doubling the capital stock $K_t$ while holding the labour force $L$ constant doubles output $Y_t$. This property is best described as:

Try answering in your head first.
A
Constant returns to capital and labour jointly.
Picked by students who don't distinguish 'returns to one factor alone' from 'returns to scale'. CRS to $(K,L)$ is the textbook Solow assumption; here only $K$ varies and $Y$ still doubles.
B
Constant returns to capital alone, holding $L$ fixed.
CORRECT — $Y = AK$ with $A$ held constant means $MPK = A$ is constant. Doubling $K$ alone doubles $Y$ — by definition CRS to capital alone.
C
Diminishing returns to capital plus aggregate IRS.
Describes the semi-endogenous regime ($\varphi<1$), not AK. The hallmark of AK is precisely constant — not diminishing — returns to capital alone.
D
Inada conditions for the marginal product of capital fail.
Partially true (Inada at $k\to\infty$ requires $MPK \to 0$; here $MPK = A$ everywhere), but this is a consequence of CRS-to-K-alone, not the direct description.

'Constant returns to capital alone' is the structural fingerprint of AK and what distinguishes it from all earlier Solow variants. The aggregate PF $Y = AK$ has output linear in $K$ at given $A$. This is what kills the Solow steady state: no diminishing returns to drag $k$ back, so $k$ grows at rate $g_e$ forever.

Q13Cross-topic

The general Solow model with exogenous technology (Module 2) exhibits conditional convergence to a balanced growth path. The AK model (Module 4, $\varphi=1$) does not exhibit convergence. What single structural feature accounts for this difference?

Try answering in your head first.
A
Returns to capital alone: diminishing in Solow, constant in AK.
CORRECT — diminishing $MPK$ in Solow ($\alpha < 1$) pulls $\tilde k$ back to its steady state. In AK, $MPK = A$ is constant; no force pulls $k_t$ to a fixed level.
B
Technology is exogenous in Solow but endogenous in AK.
Tempting but wrong: even endogenous-tech models can have convergence (semi-endogenous Romer does, on $\tilde k$). What matters is curvature in capital, not whether $A$ is endogenous.
C
General Solow has population growth, AK assumes $n = 0$.
Wrong: assumptions about $n$ don't explain the distinction. General Solow with $n=0$ still has convergence; AK with $n>0$ still would not.
D
Cobb-Douglas vs general functional form for production.
Both models use Cobb-Douglas. The qualitative difference is parameter values, not functional form.

The single structural feature is whether the marginal product of capital diminishes as capital accumulates. Solow has $MPK \to 0$ as $k \to \infty$, so investment eventually equals depreciation and a steady state forms. AK has $MPK = A$ constant — net investment never falls to zero, $k_t$ grows forever. This is also why $\varphi = 1$ is called the 'knife-edge' case: it's exactly the parameter value that flips the qualitative behaviour.

Connection: The Solow diagram works because $s f(k)$ is concave (diminishing returns), eventually flattening below $(n+\delta)k$. AK has $s f(k) = sAk$, a straight line — never flattens, never crosses depreciation linearly. The mechanism that makes Solow's steady state exist is exactly what AK removes.

Q15Derivation · justify step

The derivation below establishes that capital's share of national income equals $\alpha$ in the externality model (Ch 8). Which statement justifies the result in the highlighted line?

(1)y_t^d = (K_t^d)^\alpha (A_t L_t^d)^{1-\alpha} \quad \text{(firm-level PF, } A_t \text{ given)}
(2)r_t = \alpha \cdot y_t^d / K_t^d, \quad w_t = (1-\alpha) \cdot y_t^d / L_t^d
(3)K_t^d = K_t, \quad L_t^d = L_t, \quad A_t = K_t^\varphi \quad \text{(equilibrium)}
(4)Y_t = K_t^{\alpha + \varphi(1-\alpha)} L_t^{1-\alpha}
(5)r_t K_t / Y_t = \alpha
Try answering in your head first.
A
Factor prices use the firm-level MPK with $A_t$ taken as given; the externality is uncompensated.
CORRECT — line 2 derives $r_t$ from the FIRM-level PF, not the aggregate. Multiplying $r_t$ by $K_t$ and dividing by $Y_t$ gives $\alpha$, not $\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)$.
B
Zero profit forces factor shares to sum to 1, fixing them at $\alpha$ and $1-\alpha$.
Zero profit holds (factor payments exhaust output by Euler), but it doesn't determine the SPLIT between $K$ and $L$. Logically incomplete.
C
Cobb-Douglas form makes factor shares equal aggregate output elasticities directly.
Reflects the common mistake of differentiating the AGGREGATE PF: would give $\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)$, not $\alpha$. Confuses firm-level vs aggregate elasticities.
D
Replication argument plus Euler's theorem forces capital share to equal $\alpha$.
Replication delivers CRS at firm level; Euler's theorem says factor payments exhaust output. Together insufficient — they don't tell us the $\alpha$ split unless we already know factor prices are firm-level MPK.

The key insight: factor prices come from firm-level marginal products (line 2), with $A_t$ treated as exogenous by the firm. The externality enters only in line 3 (substituting equilibrium $A_t = K_t^\varphi$), and it shows up in the aggregate PF (line 4). But the CAPITAL SHARE is set by the firm's pricing decision — so $r_t K_t / Y_t = \alpha$, not $\alpha+\varphi(1-\alpha)$. This is the structural reason the externality model preserves perfect competition.

Q16Spot the flaw

A student argues: 'In the strictly endogenous Romer model ($\varphi=1$, $n=0$), the long-run growth rate is $g_e = \rho(s_R L)^\lambda$. Therefore countries with twice the population should grow $2^\lambda$ times faster — this is testable.' What's the principal flaw in using this as a real-world prediction?

Try answering in your head first.
A
The formula is wrong; the correct expression is $g_e = \rho s_R^\lambda$ with no $L$.
Wrong — the formula DOES contain $L$ (Lecture 4b p21). Students who half-remember the formula and want to dismiss the scale effect pick this.
B
Larger countries do not in fact grow faster — the predicted scale effect is empirically absent or reversed.
CORRECT — Lecture 4a p23 calls scale effect 'a serious and problematic' feature. Empirically, cross-country growth is not increasing in $L$ (China and India do not lead growth). This is the central critique that motivates Module 5.
C

Misreading — $\lambda < 1$ makes $2^\lambda > 1$ (just less than 2). Students unfamiliar with exponents pick here.
D

The scale effect ($g_e$ increasing in $L$) is the most prominent empirical failure of strictly endogenous Romer. Real-world growth rates are not positively correlated with country size — if anything, the 20th-century US grew at roughly constant rate while population quadrupled, contradicting the model's prediction of accelerating growth. This is the entire motivation for the 'no scale effects' literature in Module 5.

Q17Numeric

A country with population growth $n = 2\%$ per year is in semi-endogenous Romer steady state, growing at $g_{se} = 4\%$. The R&D production function has stepping-on-toes parameter $\lambda = 0.5$. Using the linearised approximation $g_{se} \approx \lambda n / (1 - \varphi)$, what is the implied $\varphi$?

Try answering in your head first.
A
$\varphi = 0.25$
Arithmetic-direction error: students compute $\lambda n / g = 0.01/0.04 = 0.25$ and mistake this ratio for $\varphi$ itself. But the formula gives $1 - \varphi = \lambda n / g$, so $\varphi = 1 - 0.25 = 0.75$.
B
$\varphi = 0.50$
Drop-a-factor error: students treat the formula as $g_{se} \approx n/(1-\varphi)$ (forgetting $\lambda$) and get $1-\varphi = 0.5$, so $\varphi = 0.5$. The presence of $\lambda = 0.5$ in the stem makes this trap especially tempting.
C
$\varphi = 0.75$
CORRECT — $0.04 = 0.5 \cdot 0.02 / (1-\varphi)$ gives $1-\varphi = 0.25$, so $\varphi = 0.75$. High $\varphi$ is consistent with the lecture's emphasis that 'standing on shoulders' is empirically substantial.
D
$\varphi = 0.85$
Educated-guess pick: students who recall '$\varphi$ close to 1 gives slow convergence' pick a higher value without computing. Plausible-looking but quantitatively wrong.

The exercise inverts the model: rather than predicting $g$ from $(\varphi, \lambda, n)$, it asks which $\varphi$ is consistent with observed $(g, \lambda, n)$. Setup: $g_{se} \approx \lambda n / (1-\varphi)$ ⇒ $1 - \varphi = \lambda n / g_{se} = 0.5 \cdot 0.02 / 0.04 = 0.25$ ⇒ $\varphi = 0.75$. The trap is treating $\lambda n / g$ as $\varphi$ rather than $1 - \varphi$ — the same arithmetic-direction error that bit M3 Q13 in the last quiz.

CORRECT — Lecture 4b p20: $g_{se}$ depends only on $\lambda, \varphi, n$, not on $s_R$ or $s$. $s_R$ shifts the level path but not its slope.

The level-vs-growth distinction is the single highest-value Module 4 mistake. Semi-endogenous Romer: $g_{se} = (1+n)^{\lambda/(1-\varphi)} - 1$ depends only on $\lambda, \varphi, n$. So $s_R$ shifts the BGP up (more $y$ at every $t$) but doesn't tilt it. Strictly endogenous: $g_e = \rho(s_R L)^\lambda$ — $s_R$ tilts the BGP. Same policy, qualitatively different outcomes because the regimes differ in whether ideas have diminishing or constant returns.
