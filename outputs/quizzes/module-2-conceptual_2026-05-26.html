Q1Multi true-false

A small open economy has aggregate saving $S_t$, gross domestic investment $I_t = K_{t+1} - K_t$ (with $\delta = 0$), and net foreign assets $F_t$. Evaluate each statement.

Think through the setup first.
A$F_{t+1} - F_t = S_t - I_t$ holds by an accounting identity, not a behavioral assumption.
Subtract the capital-accumulation equation from $V_{t+1} - V_t = S_t$, using $V = K + F$ — pure accounting. A doubter might think this only holds in equilibrium; in fact it's true period by period.
BA country with a current-account surplus ($CA_t > 0$) is exporting more goods than it imports.
$CA_t$ also includes net asset income $\bar r F_t$. A creditor country can run a goods deficit and still have $CA_t > 0$ via interest receipts. Picked by students who conflate $CA$ with the trade balance.
CIf domestic saving exceeds domestic investment, the country is accumulating net foreign assets.
Direct restatement of $F_{t+1} - F_t = S_t - I_t$. Looks too simple to be the right answer, but is.
DIn the closed-economy benchmark, $CA_t = 0$ is an assumption, not a result.
It's a result: closed economy means $F_t \equiv 0$, so $F_{t+1} - F_t = 0$, and the identity then forces $S_t = I_t$. Picked by students who think 'closed' is just shorthand for $CA = 0$ as an imposed condition.
Pick T or F for each statement.

The cluster targets four common misreadings of the open-economy accounting framework: (i) thinking the savings-investment gap requires equilibrium to bind, (ii) confusing $CA$ with the trade balance, (iii) trusting the simplest formulation, and (iv) misunderstanding what 'closed economy' assumes vs implies. All four trace back to losing track of what's definitional and what's behavioral.

Q2Multi true-false

A small open economy with perfect capital mobility, Cobb-Douglas production $Y = BK^{\alpha}L^{1-\alpha}$, $\delta = 0$, and constant world interest rate $\bar r$. The economy starts with $K_0$ below its open-economy level. Evaluate each statement.

Think through the setup first.
ACapital per worker $k_t$ jumps in period 0 to a level satisfying $\bar r = \alpha B k^{\alpha-1}$ and stays constant thereafter.
Capital is perfectly mobile, so domestic MPK must equal $\bar r$ in every period; this pins $k$ at a constant level independent of $s$ or $n$.
BWealth per worker $v_t$ jumps in period 0 to its steady-state value.
Wealth accumulates gradually through saving — the transition equation $v_{t+1} = \frac{1+s\bar r}{1+n}v_t + \frac{sw^*}{1+n}$ describes a SLOW adjustment. Conflated with the jumping behavior of $k$ by students who don't separate the two state-like variables.
CThe wage rate $w_t$ converges gradually toward $w^*$ alongside $v_t$.
Because $w = (1-\alpha)Bk^{\alpha}$ and $k$ has already jumped, $w$ has also already jumped to $w^*$ in period 0. Picked by students who forget that the wage depends on $k$ alone, not on $v$.
DThe savings rate $s$ affects long-run national wealth $v^*$ but does NOT affect $k^*$, $y^*$, or $w^*$.
$k^*$ is pinned by $\bar r$, not by saving. Saving accumulates as foreign assets (wealth) rather than as domestic capital. Counterintuitive but central.
Pick T or F for each statement.

The two-speed structure of the open-economy Solow model is its most distinctive feature: $k, y, w$ jump immediately (in one period) to their steady-state values, while $v$ (and hence $f = v - k$) crawls there gradually through saving. Misreading either timing wrecks the rest of the analysis.

Q3Multi true-false

A closed economy in the general Solow model with labour-augmenting technology $A_{t+1} = (1+g)A_t$, in balanced-growth steady state with $\tilde k_t = \tilde k^*$. Evaluate each statement.

Think through the setup first.
AOutput per worker $y_t$ grows at rate $g$, and capital per worker $k_t$ grows at rate $g$.
Both equal $A_t$ times a constant ($\tilde y^*$ and $\tilde k^*$ respectively), so both inherit $A_t$'s growth rate $g$.
BThe real interest rate $r_t$ grows at rate $g$.
$r_t$ is constant in balanced growth — it equals $\alpha (n+g+\delta+ng)/s$, which has no $t$ subscript. Picked by students who think 'everything grows at $g$' in balanced growth; in fact, factor prices split: $w$ grows at $g$, $r$ stays constant.
CThe wage rate $w_t$ grows at rate $g$.
$w_t = (1-\alpha) A_t \tilde y^*$, so it inherits $A_t$'s growth. The capital-labour ratio is fixed in efficiency units, so the real wage rises with the level of effective labour.
DThe capital-output ratio $K_t/Y_t$ is constant in balanced growth.
$K/Y = \tilde k^*/\tilde y^* = (\tilde k^*)^{1-\alpha}$, which is constant. This is one of Kaldor's stylized facts that the general Solow model successfully reproduces.
Pick T or F for each statement.

Balanced growth doesn't mean 'all variables grow at $g$' — it means per-worker quantities ($y, k, c, w$) all grow at the same rate $g$, factor PRICES split (wage tracks the growing technology, interest rate stays constant), and dimensionless RATIOS like $K/Y$ are constant. Statement B is the classic trap: thinking every variable inherits $g$.

Q4Multi true-false

The textbook proves $y^{n*} \geq y^*_c$, with equality only in the knife-edge case $\bar r = r^*_c$. Evaluate each statement about this result.

Think through the setup first.
AOpening the capital account weakly raises long-run national income per worker, regardless of whether the country is a strong or weak saver.
Direct statement of the theorem. The minimum of $x(\tilde r) = y^{n*}/y^*_c$ is 1, attained only at $\tilde r = 1$.
BOpening the capital account weakly raises the long-run wage, regardless of saving behavior.
Wages can FALL on opening: a strong-saver country has $r^*_c < \bar r$, so opening drives capital OUT, lowers domestic $k$, and lowers MPL and hence $w$. The welfare gain accrues to capital income, not labour income. Picked by students who conflate $y^{n*}$ with $w^*$.
COpening the capital account always makes the country a net creditor in the long run.
Direction of capital flows depends on $s/n$ vs $\bar s/\bar n$ (equivalently $r^*_c$ vs $\bar r$). Weak savers become DEBTORS on opening — they import capital. Picked by students who think 'opening = exporting saving'.
DIf the country's autarky rate $r^*_c$ exactly equals the world rate $\bar r$, opening the capital account leaves long-run national income unchanged.
The knife-edge case: $\tilde r = 1$ gives $x = 1$, and capital has no incentive to flow in either direction. Both $f^*$ and $y^{n*} - y^*_c$ are zero.
Pick T or F for each statement.

The opening theorem is about AVERAGE national income, not factor incomes or asset positions. Statements B and C are the high-frequency confusions: workers may lose, and the country may become a debtor — and both are still consistent with rising $y^{n*}$.

Q5Multi true-false

The Golden Rule savings rate $s^{**} = \alpha$ is derived in the basic Solow model. Evaluate each statement about how the Golden Rule transfers across the variants covered in Module 2.

Think through the setup first.
AIn the general Solow model with technological progress at rate $g$, the consumption-maximizing savings rate is still $s^{**} = \alpha$.
Technology multiplies $\tilde y^*$ by $A_t$ uniformly, so the $s$ that maximizes $(1-s)A_t \tilde y^*$ is the same $s$ that maximizes $(1-s) \tilde y^*$ — and the latter problem is structurally identical to basic Solow.
BIn the open-economy Solow model, there is a well-defined Golden Rule savings rate strictly between 0 and the stability bound $n/\bar r$.
There isn't one: $y^{n*}$ is strictly increasing in $s$ over the entire admissible range $s\bar r < n$, so $(1-s)y^{n*}$ never has an interior maximum. Picked by students who assume 'Golden Rule always exists when you can save'.
CThe Golden Rule condition can be expressed as MPK equals the dilution rate $n + g + \delta$ in the general Solow model.
At $s = \alpha$, the steady-state interest rate $r^* = \alpha(n+g+\delta+ng)/s$ becomes $r^* = n+g+\delta+ng \approx n+g+\delta$. This is the standard Modified Golden Rule statement.
DIf the actual savings rate exceeds $s^{**}$ in the general Solow model, raising $s$ further still raises steady-state consumption per worker.
Past the Golden Rule, raising $s$ LOWERS long-run consumption per worker — the capital-deepening cost exceeds the output gain. This is exactly Bolboaca Exercise 2.2's lesson: cutting $s$ from 0.45 to 0.35 raised $\tilde c^*$.
Pick T or F for each statement.

The Golden Rule survives the addition of exogenous technology unchanged (still $s^{**} = \alpha$), but the open-economy variant has no interior optimum because MPK is pinned by $\bar r$ rather than being driven down by saving. Statement D is the dynamic-inefficiency idea: economies CAN over-save, and Module 2's main numerical example demonstrates this.

Q6Multi true-false

Consider two specifications of technological progress with Cobb-Douglas production: (I) $Y_t = B_t K_t^{\alpha} L_t^{1-\alpha}$ with $B_t$ growing at rate $g_B$; (II) $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}(A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}$ with $A_t$ growing at rate $g_A$. Evaluate each statement.

Think through the setup first.
ASpecifications (I) and (II) generate the same time path of $Y_t$ if $B_t = A_t^{1-\alpha}$.
Substitute: $A_t^{1-\alpha} \cdot K^{\alpha} L^{1-\alpha} = K^{\alpha} (A_t L)^{1-\alpha}$. The equivalence is purely algebraic and depends on the Cobb-Douglas form.
BIf labour-augmenting technology grows at $g_A$, then under Cobb-Douglas the equivalent Hicks-neutral TFP grows at $g_B = (1-\alpha) g_A$.
Taking the log derivative of $B_t = A_t^{1-\alpha}$ gives $g_B = (1-\alpha) g_A$. For $\alpha = 1/3$, $g_B \approx 0.67 \cdot g_A$ — Hicks-neutral TFP grows slower than labour-augmenting tech.
CPer-worker output $y_t$ in steady state grows at $g_A$ under specification (II) but at $g_B/(1-\alpha)$ under specification (I).
Under (II), $y_t = A_t \tilde y^*$ grows at $g_A$. Under (I), per-worker output is $y_t = B_t^{1/(1-\alpha)} \cdot \text{const}$, growing at $g_B/(1-\alpha)$ — which equals $g_A$ once we equate them.
DFor a non-Cobb-Douglas production function (e.g. CES with elasticity of substitution $\neq 1$), Hicks-neutral and labour-augmenting technology are interchangeable.
The equivalence relies specifically on the Cobb-Douglas property that exponents factor through scaling. For CES or general homothetic forms, only labour-augmenting (Harrod-neutral) progress is consistent with a balanced-growth path. Picked by students who treat the Cobb-Douglas trick as general.
Pick T or F for each statement.

The Cobb-Douglas equivalence (Statement A) is a special case that makes the choice of specification a notational convenience. Statement D is the classical Uzawa theorem in disguise: away from Cobb-Douglas, balanced growth requires Harrod-neutrality. This is exactly the kind of 'when does the trick break' question Cozzi likes to probe.

Q7Multi true-false

The Module 2 lectures derive two distinct stability conditions: (I) $s\bar r < n$ for the open-economy wealth dynamics, and (II) $n + g + \delta + ng > 0$ for the general Solow $\tilde k$ dynamics. Evaluate each statement.

Think through the setup first.
ACondition (I) requires that the transition line $v_{t+1}(v_t)$ have slope strictly less than 1.
Slope $= (1+s\bar r)/(1+n)$; less than 1 iff $s\bar r < n$. The slope-less-than-1 logic is the same one used for any first-order linear difference equation.
BCondition (II) is essentially always satisfied in any economy with positive population, technology, or depreciation rates.
Each of $n, g, \delta$ being non-negative with at least one strictly positive makes $n+g+\delta+ng > 0$ automatic. The condition is more a sanity check than a binding constraint.
CCondition (I) translates, via the world's closure $\bar r = \alpha \bar n/\bar s$, into $\alpha \cdot s/n < \bar s/\bar n$, which is easily satisfied when the small economy resembles the world average.
Substitute $\bar r = \alpha \bar n/\bar s$ into $s\bar r < n$: $s\alpha \bar n/\bar s < n$, which rearranges to $\alpha (s/n) < \bar s/\bar n$. With $\alpha \approx 1/3$ and $s/n$ near the world average, the inequality reduces to $1/3 < 1$.
DIf condition (I) fails, $v_t$ explodes — and so does the world economy as a whole.
Even if a single small economy's condition fails, the world is closed and faces its own consistent steady state. Condition (I) is a per-country condition, not a global one. The world doesn't 'explode' because of one country's parameters.
Pick T or F for each statement.

The two stability conditions live at different levels: (I) is a small-economy condition that depends on the relation between $\bar r$ (world) and domestic $s/n$; (II) is a model-internal condition that's mechanically satisfied for any reasonable economy. Both are usually benign, but their LOGIC is different — (I) requires checking against world averages, (II) doesn't.

Q8Multi true-false

The general Solow model produces two empirical predictions tested in cross-country data: (i) the steady-state regression of $\ln y$ on $\ln s - \ln(n+g+\delta)$ should have slope $\alpha/(1-\alpha)$; (ii) the rate of convergence is $\lambda \approx (1-\alpha)(n+g+\delta)$. Evaluate each statement.

Think through the setup first.
AThe cross-country estimated slope of $\ln y$ on $\ln s - \ln(n+g+\delta)$ is about 1.5 — roughly three times larger than the model predicts for $\alpha = 1/3$.
Textbook OLS estimate: $\hat\beta_1 \approx 1.52$, vs predicted $\alpha/(1-\alpha) = 0.5$ for $\alpha = 1/3$. The model UNDERSTATES how much $s$ matters.
BThe estimated rate of convergence across countries is about 0.7%/year, slower than the model's prediction of about 5%/year.
Standard textbook number: $\hat\lambda \approx 0.007$ corresponds to a half-life of ~100 years; the model predicts ~14 years. Both empirical failures (slope and convergence) point in the same direction: the model is missing slow-moving accumulable factors.
CThe model passes the qualitative test of conditional convergence — countries further below their steady state grow faster, holding $s, n, g, \delta$ fixed.
The negative coefficient on $\ln y_0$ in the convergence regression is robust and significant. The model fails on MAGNITUDES, not on the direction of effects.
DBoth empirical failures are fixed by replacing $\alpha = 1/3$ with a higher $\alpha$ such as 0.6.
Raising $\alpha$ to 0.6 would match the OLS slope and slow predicted convergence — but $\alpha = 0.6$ is wildly inconsistent with capital's share of national income (~1/3). The MRW human-capital extension fixes both empirical failures with $\alpha$ unchanged by introducing a separate human-capital factor.
Pick T or F for each statement.

The Solow model's two main quantitative failures (slope ~3× too small, convergence ~7× too fast) both reflect a missing accumulable factor that responds to $s, n, g, \delta$ like physical capital. Brute-forcing $\alpha$ upward is mathematically equivalent but economically wrong — capital income share data refutes it. Module 3's MRW extension is the principled fix.

Q9Boundary

The textbook proves that liberalizing the capital account raises long-run national income per worker $y^{n*}$ in a small open economy. Which of the following is NOT implied by this result?

Try answering in your head first.
A
Domestic capital intensity $k$ may decrease as a result of opening if the country was a strong saver in autarky and capital flows out.
This IS implied — strong-saver capital flowing out is the mechanism behind the wage drop in such countries. Tempting to dismiss because the theorem is framed as 'good'.
B
Domestic real wages may decrease as a result of opening if the country was a strong saver in autarky and capital flows out.
Also implied — same mechanism: lower $k$ means lower MPL means lower $w$. Picked by students who confuse 'national income rises' with 'every income rises'.
C
The country's long-run consumption per worker $c^* = (1-s)y^{n*}$ rises, since the savings rate is held fixed under the policy shift.
Implied by the same theorem: since $y^{n*}$ rises and $s$ is held fixed, $c^*$ rises with it. Picked by students who think the theorem applies only to income and not to consumption.
D
Every domestic resident is at least as well off after opening as before, even without any compensating transfers across groups.
Correct — this is NOT implied. The theorem is about AVERAGE income, not individual welfare. Workers in a high-saving rich country can be made worse off by opening even though average income rises.

The textbook's welfare theorem is a statement about national income (an average), not individual welfare or any distributional concept. D is precisely the gap the textbook flags: 'redistributive policies may be needed to ensure all groups benefit'. A and B describe mechanisms COMPATIBLE with the theorem (and consistent with strong-saver countries); C follows trivially from the income result.

Q10Spot the flaw

A student argues: 'In the open-economy Solow model, since the world interest rate $\bar r$ is exogenous, the savings rate $s$ has no effect on the long-run economy. Increasing $s$ is therefore a useless policy in a small open economy.' What is wrong with this argument?

Try answering in your head first.
A
The argument is correct: in the open-economy long run, $s$ genuinely leaves $k^*$, $y^*$, $w^*$, and even $y^{n*}$ all pinned by the exogenous world rate $\bar r$.
Picked by students who notice $s$ doesn't appear in $k^*$, $y^*$, $w^*$ and over-generalize to $y^{n*}$ without checking the wealth channel through $v^*$.
B
The argument is wrong because in a small open economy, the world rate $\bar r$ adjusts endogenously to clear the domestic capital market once $s$ changes significantly.
False at the small-economy level: that's the entire point of 'small' — the country cannot move $\bar r$. Picked by students who confuse small-open-economy with closed-economy logic.
C
The argument is wrong because a higher savings rate accelerates the gradual convergence of $k_t$ toward its long-run level, generating transitional growth gains.
False: in the open-economy variant $k$ doesn't converge at all — it jumps immediately. The transition speed for $v$ does depend on $s$, but the question was about long-run effects, not transitional dynamics.
D
The savings rate still affects long-run net foreign assets $f^*$ and hence national income $y^{n*} = w^* + \bar r v^*$, even though it leaves productive capital $k^*$ unchanged.
Correct. $v^* = sw^*/(n-s\bar r)$ is strictly increasing in $s$, so a higher savings rate accumulates more national wealth — which earns $\bar r$ forever. The policy lever is wealth abroad, not capital at home.

The student's argument captures one true thing (that $k^*$ is pinned by $\bar r$) and over-generalizes it. The full picture (D): $s$ doesn't affect productive capital, but it builds NET FOREIGN ASSETS, and the return on those assets is part of national income. So $s$ remains a meaningful policy lever — it just acts on wealth abroad rather than capital at home.

Q11What it measures

In the general Solow production function $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}(A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}$, what does the symbol $A_t$ represent?

Try answering in your head first.
A
Hicks-neutral total factor productivity — the multiplier that appears in front of the entire Cobb-Douglas form $K^{\alpha}L^{1-\alpha}$.
False — that's $B_t$, related by $B_t = A_t^{1-\alpha}$ but not equal unless $\alpha = 0$. Picked by students who use $A$ and TFP interchangeably across modules.
B
Average labour productivity, computed as the data ratio of total output to total labour input $Y_t/L_t$ each period.
False — average labour productivity is $y_t$, which depends on $k_t$ as well as $A_t$. $A_t$ is a structural parameter, not the data ratio.
C
Labour-augmenting technology — the scaling factor that converts raw labour $L_t$ into effective labour $A_t L_t$ inside the production function.
Correct. $A_t$ multiplies $L$ inside the production function; it captures how much output a unit of raw labour can produce given current technology.
D
The growth rate of technological change in the economy, distinct from the level of technology that prevails at any given time.
False — that's $g$. $A_t$ is the LEVEL of technology in period $t$; $g$ is its growth rate. Picked by students who muddle stocks and flows in growth models.

The four choices reflect the standard confusions: A and C are different specifications related by the Cobb-Douglas trick; B confuses a parameter with a data ratio; D confuses a level with a growth rate. The distinction matters: under Cobb-Douglas $A$ and $B$ give identical predictions; under more general production functions they don't.

Q12Deep-short

A small open economy has $r^*_c = 0.06$ (autarky steady-state interest rate from the closed Solow model) and faces a world interest rate $\bar r = 0.04$. After opening the capital account, the country will...

Try answering in your head first.
A
...become a net debtor: its high autarky rate signals capital scarcity domestically, so opening lets foreign capital flow in to drive MPK down to $\bar r$.
Correct. $r^*_c > \bar r$ implies $\bar r < r^*_c$ implies $f^* < 0$. Capital flows IN to drive domestic MPK down to $\bar r$, making the country a net debtor.
B
...become a net creditor on the grounds that its high autarky rate implies it had been over-saving relative to the rest of the world.
Reverses the direction. $r^*_c > \bar r$ means the country was UNDER-investing relative to the world (high MPK because capital was scarce, not abundant). It becomes a debtor, not creditor.
C
...become a net debtor because the world capital market is willing to lend cheap, and the country opportunistically borrows whatever it can.
Right answer for the wrong reason. Cheap world borrowing is a CONSEQUENCE of $\bar r < r^*_c$, not the cause of becoming a debtor. The country becomes a debtor because domestic MPK exceeds $\bar r$, creating an arbitrage opportunity.
D
...remain at zero net foreign assets, because the 0.06 vs 0.04 difference is too small to generate meaningful capital flows in steady state.
False — even small rate differences produce nonzero long-run asset positions. The 0.06 vs 0.04 gap is substantial (50% relative) and produces meaningful capital inflows.

The key relationship is $f^* > 0 \iff \bar r > r^*_c$. Intuition: capital flows TO where returns are higher. If world rates are LOW relative to autarky, the country was capital-scarce in autarky (high MPK) — foreign capital pours in, equalizing returns, making the country a net debtor. C reaches the same conclusion via a confused mechanism (cheap borrowing as cause, not effect); a partial-understander could pick it.

Q13Cross-topic

The basic Solow model of Module 1 predicts a closed-economy steady-state interest rate $r^*_c = \alpha n/s$ (with $\delta = 0$). In the open-economy Solow model of Module 2, this exact same quantity appears again. What is its role in the open-economy model?

Try answering in your head first.
A
It continues to function as the steady-state real interest rate in the open economy as well, since the closed-economy formula does not change.
False — the open-economy steady-state rate is the exogenous $\bar r$, not $r^*_c$. Picked by students who think 'steady-state rate' is one number across models.
B
It determines, by comparison with $\bar r$, whether the country becomes a creditor or a debtor on the international capital market.
Correct. $f^* > 0 \iff \bar r > r^*_c \iff s/n > \bar s/\bar n$. The autarky rate is the threshold the world rate must exceed for the country to lend to the rest of the world.
C
It determines the stability of the wealth transition equation, with stability holding precisely when $\bar r$ falls below $r^*_c$.
Partially true but for the wrong reason. Stability requires $s\bar r < n$, equivalent to $\bar r < r^*_c/\alpha$ (not $r^*_c$ itself). A subtle distractor confusing related but distinct inequalities.
D
It is the interest rate that prevails domestically during the transition phase before national wealth $v$ has converged to its steady-state value $v^*$.
False — the domestic rate is $\bar r$ in EVERY period of the open-economy model, including the transition. Capital mobility is instantaneous, so the interest rate never deviates from the world rate.

The Module 1 autarky rate $r^*_c = \alpha n/s$ does not vanish when we open the economy — it's repurposed as a THRESHOLD. A country is on one side of the world capital market or the other depending on whether its autarky-Solow rate would have been below or above $\bar r$. This is exactly what links the Feldstein-Horioka logic ($s/n$ vs $\bar s/\bar n$) to the Module 1 formula.

Connection: Module 1 derives $r^*_c = \alpha n / s$ as the closed-economy long-run interest rate. Module 2 keeps this formula intact but reinterprets it: the same expression now tells us, before opening, whether the country's MPK would be above or below the world rate $\bar r$ — and hence the direction of capital flows once opening occurs. The algebra is identical; the economic meaning shifts from 'equilibrium rate' to 'threshold rate'.
Q14Numeric

A small open economy is observed in long-run steady state with $f^* > 0$ (net creditor). Its parameters are $\alpha = 0.3$, $n = 0.02$, $\bar r = 0.05$. What can we infer about the country's saving rate $s$?

Try answering in your head first.
A
$s > 0.12$. The creditor condition $\bar r > r^*_c = \alpha n/s$ gives this lower bound, and stability is automatic.
Drops the stability condition. Sets the right creditor lower bound but ignores that the model also requires $s\bar r < n$, which puts an upper bound on $s$.
B
$s < 0.40$. The stability condition $s\bar r < n$ gives this upper bound, and creditor status doesn't impose any lower bound.
Drops the creditor condition. Stability tells us the upper bound, but creditor status separately requires $s$ to be large enough that $r^*_c < \bar r$.
C
$s < 0.12$. The country is a creditor precisely when its savings rate stays low enough that domestic returns remain attractive.
Sign error: confuses $f^* > 0$ with $f^* < 0$. Debtor status would require $s < 0.12$; creditor requires $s > 0.12$ so that $r^*_c < \bar r$.
D
$0.12 < s < 0.40$. Creditor needs $s > \alpha n/\bar r = 0.12$, and stability needs $s < n/\bar r = 0.40$, simultaneously.
Correct. Creditor: $\bar r > r^*_c = \alpha n/s$, so $0.05 > 0.006/s$, giving $s > 0.12$. Stability: $s\bar r < n$, so $s < 0.40$. Combined: $0.12 < s < 0.40$.

This inverts the standard direction of the problem: instead of given $s$ ask whether $f^* > 0$, we observe $f^* > 0$ and back out the constraints on $s$. Two conditions bind: (i) creditor status requires $\bar r > r^*_c$, giving $s > \alpha n / \bar r = 0.12$; (ii) stability $s\bar r < n$ gives $s < n/\bar r = 0.40$. Both must hold simultaneously, so $0.12 < s < 0.40$.

Q15Graph

The diagram below shows a general Solow diagram in efficiency units, with the saving curve $s\tilde k^{\alpha}$ and the break-even line $(n+g+\delta)\tilde k$. Four points (A, B, C, D) are marked on or near the curves. Which point represents the steady-state value $\tilde k^*$?

Try answering in your head first.
A
Point A, located on the saving curve at low $\tilde k$ where the economy still has room to accumulate capital.
A is on the saving curve but below steady state (where $s \tilde k^{\alpha} > (n+g+\delta) \tilde k$, so $\tilde k$ is rising). The economy is moving toward $\tilde k^*$, not at it.
B
Point B, located on the saving curve at high $\tilde k$ where saving falls below break-even and capital declines.
B is above steady state (where the saving curve is below the break-even line, so $\tilde k$ is falling). The economy is shrinking toward $\tilde k^*$, not at it.
C
Point C, the unique positive intersection of the saving curve and the break-even line in the diagram.
Correct. Steady state is exactly where saving equals break-even, $s \tilde k^{\alpha} = (n+g+\delta) \tilde k$. Geometrically, this is the unique positive crossing of the two curves.
D
Point D, sitting above the break-even line at intermediate $\tilde k$ but on neither of the two curves.
D is off both curves. Picked by students who don't realize a steady state requires being ON the saving curve AND on the break-even line simultaneously.

In the general Solow diagram, the steady state is geometrically the intersection of the concave saving curve $s\tilde k^{\alpha}$ and the linear break-even line $(n+g+\delta)\tilde k$. To the LEFT of $\tilde k^*$, the saving curve is above the break-even line so $\tilde k$ rises; to the right, the opposite. Only at the intersection (point C) does $\tilde k$ stay put.

Q16Derivation · justify step

The derivation below converts the aggregate wealth recursion into a per-worker recursion. Which step justifies line (4)?

(1)V_{t+1} = V_t + S_t
(2)S_t = s Y^n_t = s (w_t L_t + \bar r V_t)
(3)V_{t+1} = V_t + s w_t L_t + s \bar r V_t
(4)v_{t+1} = \frac{(1 + s\bar r) v_t + s w_t}{1 + n}
Try answering in your head first.
A
Divide both sides of line (3) by $L_{t+1} = (1+n) L_t$, then use $v_t = V_t/L_t$ and $w_t L_t / L_t = w_t$ to simplify the result.
Correct. Dividing line (3) by $L_{t+1}$ converts the aggregate recursion into per-worker form; the algebra collapses to $((1+s\bar r)v_t + sw_t)/(1+n)$.
B
Apply the steady-state condition $v_{t+1} = v_t = v^*$ to the recursion above and solve algebraically for the implicit fixed point of the resulting difference equation.
Wrong: line (4) is still a recursion (time-subscripted on both sides), not a steady-state equation. Steady state would eliminate the $t+1$ subscript. Picked by students who confuse transition equations with their fixed points.
C
Substitute $w_t = w^*$, since the wage has already jumped to its steady-state value in the open-economy model with mobile capital.
Partial truth — the wage HAS jumped to $w^*$ already, but the move from line (3) to line (4) is the per-worker conversion, not the wage substitution. Picked by students who notice $w$ in the final answer.
D
Apply the labour-force growth equation $L_{t+1} = (1+n) L_t$ as a substitution into the numerator of the aggregate recursion to rewrite total population in next-period units.
Almost right but incomplete — that equation is USED in the division, but the step itself involves division by $L_{t+1}$, not substitution into the numerator.

The move from line (3) to line (4) is converting the aggregate recursion into per-worker form by dividing both sides by $L_{t+1}$. This requires (i) using $L_{t+1} = (1+n) L_t$ to express the denominator in terms of $L_t$, and (ii) recognizing $V_t/L_t = v_t$ and $w_t L_t/L_t = w_t$. After conversion, the further substitution $w_t = w^*$ gives the textbook form $v_{t+1} = (1+s\bar r)v_t/(1+n) + sw^*/(1+n)$.

Q17Derivation · source

The textbook identity $y^{n*} = w^* + \bar r v^*$ is a key intermediate result in the open-economy Solow analysis. Which derivation produces this identity?

Try answering in your head first.
A
Start from $Y^n_t = Y_t + \bar r F_t$, substitute $F_t = V_t - K_t$, apply $Y - \bar r K = wL$, and divide by $L_t$.
Correct path. $Y^n = Y + \bar r F = Y + \bar r(V - K) = (Y - \bar r K) + \bar r V = wL + \bar r V$. Per worker: $y^n = w + \bar r v$. In steady state, $w = w^*$ and $v = v^*$.
B
Set $v_{t+1} = v_t = v^*$ in the wealth transition equation derived earlier and solve algebraically for the steady-state wealth level $v^*$ as a function of parameters.
Produces $v^* = sw^*/(n - s\bar r)$, not $y^{n*} = w^* + \bar r v^*$. This is the steady-state of WEALTH, not of national income. Picked by students who confuse these distinct steady-state expressions.
C
Equate the marginal product of capital to the world rate, $\bar r = \alpha B k^{\alpha-1}$, then invert to obtain the immediate-adjustment value of $k$.
Produces $k^* = (\alpha B/\bar r)^{1/(1-\alpha)}$, the immediate-adjustment formula. Has nothing to do with the national-income identity. Picked by students who associate 'open economy' with the FOC step regardless of what's being derived.
D
Differentiate the welfare ratio $x(\tilde r) = y^{n*}/y^*_c$ with respect to $\tilde r$ and set the derivative to zero to characterize the minimum.
Produces the property that $\tilde r = 1$ is the minimum of $x$ — a result ABOUT the identity, not the identity itself. Picked by students who conflate proving an identity with analyzing its consequences.

The identity $y^{n*} = w^* + \bar r v^*$ decomposes national income into wage income (paid to labour from domestic production) plus interest income (earned on total national wealth). The decomposition emerges from rewriting $Y^n$ using the wealth definition $V = K + F$ and the production-function identity $Y = \bar r K + wL$. The other three derivations all live within the open-economy framework but produce DIFFERENT results.

Q18Derivation · why

In the open-economy Solow model, capital per worker $k_t$ satisfies $k_t = k^* = (\alpha B/\bar r)^{1/(1-\alpha)}$ in every period $t \geq 0$ — a constant. What mechanism makes this immediate-adjustment property hold?

Try answering in your head first.
A
The domestic savings rate is sufficiently high that domestic savings alone can fund the entire desired capital stock $K^*$ in a single period.
False — domestic savings alone cannot finance the entire capital stock in one period. Picked by students who think the jump is funded by domestic accumulation rather than capital imports.
B
Perfect international capital mobility: any deviation between domestic MPK and $\bar r$ triggers instantaneous capital flows that close the gap in zero time.
Correct. Mobile capital arbitrages any rate differential away within the period, pinning domestic MPK to $\bar r$ — which in turn pins $k$. This is the structural assumption doing the work.
C
The Cobb-Douglas functional form forces capital per worker $k$ to be constant whenever the factor prices $r$ and $w$ are constant over time.
Partial truth — Cobb-Douglas is convenient for solving the FOC, but the immediate-adjustment property doesn't depend on Cobb-Douglas specifically. With perfect mobility, $k$ adjusts instantly under any production function.
D
The stability condition $s\bar r < n$ ensures that the capital intensity $k$ converges very quickly to its steady-state value $k^*$.
Conflates two different convergence stories. $s\bar r < n$ is the stability condition for WEALTH ($v$), not capital ($k$). $k$ doesn't 'converge' in this model — it's pinned in period zero by the arbitrage condition.

The immediate-adjustment property is a CONSEQUENCE of the perfect capital mobility assumption, not of any functional form or saving behavior. With mobile capital, an MPK-$\bar r$ gap creates an arbitrage opportunity that gets exploited instantly: capital pours in (or out) until MPK equals $\bar r$. The Cobb-Douglas form just makes the resulting $k^*$ have a closed form; the qualitative property of jumping is universal under perfect mobility.

Scenario

Setup. A closed economy in the basic Solow model (no technology, $\delta = 0$) has $\alpha = 0.3$, $n = 0.03$, and saving rate $s = 0.15$. Initially the economy is in autarky steady state. At $t = 0$, two policy changes occur simultaneously: (i) the savings rate rises to $s' = 0.30$, AND (ii) the capital account opens to free international capital mobility at world rate $\bar r = 0.03$. Use this setup for Q19 and Q20.

Q19Deep-short

(Using the setup above.) Given the new parameters after the policy shift, will the country accumulate positive or negative net foreign assets in the long run?

Try answering in your head first.
A
$f^* > 0$ (net creditor): even though numbers are close to the knife-edge, a slight numerical advantage tilts the country toward exporting capital abroad.
Compelling because the numbers are close. But $r^*_c = 0.3 \cdot 0.03/0.30 = 0.03$ EXACTLY equals $\bar r$ — this is the knife-edge case, not a tilted creditor position. Picked by students who can't tolerate a 'no-flow' equilibrium.
B
$f^* < 0$ (net debtor): the country's increased savings push wealth above what's productive domestically, forcing it to borrow from abroad on net.
Reverses the logic completely. High savings would push wealth UP and the country toward creditor status, not debtor. Picked by students who confuse 'has lots of wealth' with 'borrows lots'.
C
$f^* = 0$ exactly: the new autarky rate $r^*_c = \alpha n/s' = 0.3 \cdot 0.03/0.30 = 0.03$ equals $\bar r$, putting the country at the knife-edge.
Correct. The combined policy shift puts the country precisely at $r^*_c = \bar r$, the knife-edge. Capital has no incentive to flow in either direction in steady state.
D
$f^* > 0$ (net creditor): the high savings rate $s' = 0.30$ gives $r^*_c < \bar r$, so opening lets the country invest abroad at the higher world rate.
Conceptually right direction but wrong calculation. $r^*_c = 0.03$ EQUALS $\bar r$, not less than it. The argument 'high savings → creditor' requires $r^*_c$ to actually fall below $\bar r$, which happens for $s' > 0.30$ but not at $s' = 0.30$ exactly.

This question forces the student to actually compute the new autarky rate after the savings-rate change, then compare it to $\bar r$. With $s' = 0.30$, $r^*_c = \alpha n/s' = 0.03$ — exactly the world rate. Equivalently, $s'/n = 10$ equals $\alpha/\bar r = 10$, so the strong-vs-weak-saver inequality is tight. The country is at the knife-edge: zero net foreign assets in steady state.

Q20Boundary

(Continuing the setup from Q19.) Compare the new long-run open-economy steady state to the original (pre-policy) closed-economy steady state. Which of the following is NOT a long-run consequence of the combined policy shift?

Try answering in your head first.
A
Capital per worker $k$ rises, because $k^* = (\alpha B/\bar r)^{1/(1-\alpha)}$ with $\bar r = 0.03$ exceeds the original autarky value at $s = 0.15$, $n = 0.03$.
This IS a consequence: $\alpha/\bar r = 10$ vs $s/n = 5$, so the new $k^*$ formula exceeds the old autarky $k^*_c$ formula. Picked as 'NOT a consequence' by students who don't run the comparison.
B
Long-run national income per worker $y^{n*}$ rises, even though net foreign assets remain at zero, simply because the new state has higher domestic output.
Subtle but IS a consequence. Even at the knife-edge ($f^* = 0$), $y^{n*}$ rises because the new $s' = 0.30$ pushes the country to a higher capital stock and hence higher output.
C
Long-run wages $w^*$ change as a result of the policy shift, since the wage depends on the capital intensity $k$ and $k$ moves to a new level.
IS a consequence: $w = (1-\alpha) B k^{\alpha}$ depends on $k$, and $k$ moves. Given A, $w$ rises along with $k$ in this particular scenario.
D
The country's long-run net foreign asset position $f^*$ rises from 0 in autarky to a positive value reflecting accumulated savings abroad.
Correct — this is NOT a consequence. From Q19, the new steady state has $f^* = 0$ (knife-edge), exactly the same as the autarky $f^* = 0$. So $f^*$ does NOT change. Picked by students who don't trust their Q19 answer.

The trick: the knife-edge property from Q19 ($f^* = 0$ in the new steady state) means net foreign assets DON'T change as a result of the combined policy. Both the original autarky and the new open-economy steady state have $f^* = 0$. Everything ELSE changes — $k$, $y$, $w$, $c$, $v$ — but the asset position stays at zero. This is the cleanest demonstration that 'opening the capital account' and 'becoming a creditor' are distinct events.
