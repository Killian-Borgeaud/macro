1. The Big Picture

Open any macroeconomics textbook and you'll see two almost-disjoint conversations. One is about why some countries are rich and others stay poor — why average income in Switzerland is roughly forty times what it is in Burundi, and what (if anything) Burundi could do to close the gap. The other is about why economies wobble from year to year — why a country can grow at 4% one year and shrink by 2% the next, and what policymakers can do about it. The first conversation is what 'macroeconomics for the long run' refers to. It is what this whole course is about. The second is left for another semester.

The two conversations use genuinely different models, because they ask different questions. Long-run models abstract from shocks, from sticky prices, from misforecasts — they treat the economy as if it always had time to fully adjust. Short-run models build on the opposite premise: shocks happen, prices stick, and people get caught off guard. So when you read about 'Solow' (long-run) and 'IS-LM' (short-run) in the same textbook, don't expect them to be variants of one master model. They are scaffolding for different questions, by design. Figure 1 shows how the same time series of GDP gets sliced two ways: the smooth trend (our subject) versus the zig-zag deviations around it (the other course).

Three sub-decisions get baked into how we measure prosperity. First: GDP per worker, not GDP per person. Dividing by the labour force rather than the population corrects for differences in participation rates — a country with a smaller official labour market (lots of subsistence farming, say) shouldn't be penalised for that when we're trying to measure productivity. Second: purchasing-power-parity adjustment. Pesos buy more if dinner costs less in pesos, so we can't just convert at market exchange rates. Third: real, not nominal. We're after changes in actual quantities of stuff people consume, not in the price tags they carry.

Why such fuss about growth rather than levels? Because levels come from sustained growth. A country growing at 6% a year doubles its income every twelve years (the 'rule of 70'). One growing at 0.5% doubles every hundred and forty. Robert Lucas (Nobel '95) put it bluntly: 'once you start to think about growth, it is difficult to think about anything else.' (Slides Session 1 p3 quotes this approvingly.)

The roadmap for this module is therefore three-step. First, look at the data: what regularities do we actually observe across countries and over time? We will lay these out as seven numbered 'stylized facts.' Second, build the simplest possible growth model that could plausibly account for them: the basic Solow model. Third, hold the model up against the facts — where does it fit and where does it break? That last comparison is exactly what mock-exam Question 1 asks for, so we will work it out carefully in §6.

2. Object Definitions — The Empirical and Theoretical Vocabulary

Before any model, we need vocabulary. Two clusters of objects to define: empirical (how we describe what we see in data) and theoretical (the gears of the model we are about to build).

2.1 The empirical vocabulary: Lorenz, Gini, and seven stylized facts

The Lorenz curve is the workhorse for talking about inequality. Imagine sorting all people in the world by income, lowest to highest. Walk through them from the poor end. After you have covered the poorest $x$% of the population, how much (cumulatively) of the world's total income have they earned? Call that $y$%. Plot $(x, y)$ for every $x$ from 0 to 100 and you get a curve from $(0,0)$ to $(100, 100)$. If everyone earned the same, you would get the $45°$ diagonal — 60% of the people would earn 60% of the income. If income were perfectly concentrated, the curve would hug the bottom-and-right boundary. Real income distributions sit somewhere in between, as Figure 2 shows.

= 2.0

Figure 2. The Lorenz Curve and Gini. Cumulative income share (y) plotted against cumulative population share (x), poor-to-rich. The 45° line is perfect equality. Any bulge below the diagonal is inequality; the Gini is twice the area between them. Drag p — higher values bend the curve away from the diagonal, raising inequality. At p=1 the curve coincides with the diagonal (Gini = 0); at p → ∞ all income goes to the richest, Gini → 1.

The Gini coefficient turns this picture into a single number. It is the area between the diagonal and the Lorenz curve, divided by the full area under the diagonal (which equals $1/2$). So Gini $= 0$ for perfect equality, Gini $= 1$ for perfect inequality. Computed across countries on GDP-per-worker data, the world Gini was about 0.61 in 1970 and 0.51 in 2003 — a meaningful fall, driven mostly by China and India growing fast (textbook ch2 p36).

Now the seven stylized facts. The word 'stylized' here means 'roughly true over sufficiently large periods' — these are the foundational empirical observations the rest of the course tries to explain. Cozzi numbers them; you should be able to recite the list (Lecture 1a, Slides Session 1 pp5-6).

#FactOne-line interpretation
1Some countries are rich, some are poor; differences are enormous and have stayed enormous over 40+ years (with some narrowing).Income levels persist; convergence at the bottom is weak.

Facts 5-7 are tightly bound together. If $g_y$ is roughly constant AND the labour share is roughly constant AND the rate of return on capital is roughly trendless, then several derived constancies follow automatically (real wages grow at $g$, $K/L$ grows at $g$, $K/Y$ is constant, etc.). The whole package gets a name:

Balanced growth (Lecture 1a p22; textbook ch2 p46). A growth process is on a balanced growth path if:
  1. GDP per worker, consumption per worker, the real wage, and capital intensity all grow at one common constant rate $g$.
  2. The labour force (population) grows at a constant rate $n$.
  3. GDP, consumption, and capital all grow at the common rate $g + n$.
  4. The capital-output ratio and the rate of return on capital are constant.
Why this matters: when we evaluate a growth model in later modules, the first question we ask is 'does it deliver balanced growth in the long run?' If it doesn't, it is inconsistent with stylized facts 5-7 and we either reject it or modify it. The 'as if' empirical consistency check.

2.2 The theoretical vocabulary: the Solow model setup

Now the model side. The basic Solow model (Lecture 1b, textbook ch3) is a closed economy with discrete periods $t = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$, three commodities (output, capital services, labour services), and three markets, all perfectly competitive. Output can be eaten or invested — a one-sector model — so there is one numéraire price (output, normalised to 1) and two relative prices: $r_t$ (real rental rate of capital) and $w_t$ (real wage).

All production is collapsed into one representative firm using a production function $F(K, L)$. The model assumes four properties for $F$. The table below states each one in plain words alongside what it formally implies and what would break if it failed.

PropertyPlain meaningFormal statementWhat breaks if violated
Constant returns to scale (CRS)Double both inputs, double output. 'Replication argument.'$F(\lambda K, \lambda L) = \lambda F(K, L)$ for $\lambda > 0$Either increasing returns (snowballing growth — too explosive) or decreasing (a single firm couldn't be representative).
Positive marginal productsMore of either input always raises output (at least a little).$F'_K > 0$, $F'_L > 0$You'd get satiation in inputs — implausible at aggregate level.
Diminishing marginal productsThe 100th machine adds less than the 10th, holding labour fixed.$F''_{KK} < 0$, $F''_{LL} < 0$Without this, capital accumulation never slows — no steady state, runaway growth.
Cross-partial positiveCapital and labour are complements: more capital makes labour more productive.$F''_{KL} > 0$Wages wouldn't rise with capital deepening — at odds with SF 6.

There is a particular $F$ that satisfies all four and has constant factor income shares — exactly what stylized facts 6-7 demand. It is the famous Cobb-Douglas:

Cobb-Douglas production function:$$Y_t = B \, K_t^{\alpha} \, L_t^{1-\alpha}, \qquad 0 < \alpha < 1, \quad B > 0$$Reading the symbols: $Y_t$ is output (real GDP), $K_t$ is the capital stock, $L_t$ is labour force, $B$ is total factor productivity (TFP — captures everything else: technology, institutions, the works), and $\alpha$ is the elasticity of output with respect to capital. Crucially, $\alpha$ will turn out to equal capital's share of national income.

Calibration aside: empirically labour's share is about $2/3$, so $\alpha$ (capital's share) is about $1/3$. Cozzi uses this in every numerical example, and so will we. The 'no subscript on $B$' point in Lecture 1b p12 is deliberate: the 'basic' Solow model treats technology as constant. Module 2 will let $B$ grow over time.

For per-worker analysis, divide both sides by $L_t$. With Cobb-Douglas this gives the per-worker production function:

$$y_t = \frac{Y_t}{L_t} = B \left(\frac{K_t}{L_t}\right)^{\alpha} = B \, k_t^{\alpha}$$

where $k_t = K_t/L_t$ is capital per worker. This is the curve we will plot constantly, and Figure 3 shows what it looks like. Notice the diminishing returns: doubling $k$ less than doubles $y$. The tangent line at any point is the marginal product of capital, $\partial y/\partial k = \alpha B \, k^{\alpha - 1}$, which flattens as $k$ rises. Drag $k_0$ in the figure and watch the tangent grow shallower.

Two more building blocks complete the model. First, capital accumulation: next period's capital equals today's plus net investment minus depreciation:

$$K_{t+1} - K_t = S_t - \delta K_t, \qquad 0 < \delta < 1$$

where $S_t$ is gross saving and $\delta$ is the depreciation rate (the fraction of the capital stock that wears out each year). In a closed economy national accounting gives $Y_t = C_t + I_t$ and saving equals investment $S_t = I_t$, so this is also the same as $K_{t+1} - K_t = I_t - \delta K_t$.

Second, savings behaviour — the model's biggest simplification: a constant fraction $s$ of income gets saved every period.

$$S_t = s \, Y_t, \qquad 0 < s < 1$$

Sørensen and Whitta-Jacobsen call this a 'short-cut': a more elaborate model would derive $s$ from explicit utility maximization. We accept the shortcut because empirically the consumption share of GDP is reasonably constant over decades (textbook ch3 Fig 3.2 — both private and public consumption shares move, but their sum stays roughly flat).

And labour grows mechanically — what Lecture 1b p11 calls 'biology':

$$L_{t+1} = (1 + n) L_t, \qquad n > -1$$

with $n$ given exogenously. That's the whole model — six equations (Cobb-Douglas, two factor-price equations, savings rule, capital accumulation, population growth) in six endogenous variables ($Y, K, L, r, w, S$). The state variables are $K_t$ and $L_t$, both predetermined at the start of each period.

Beyond exam scope, but worth flagging: the textbook briefly considers the CES production function (Exercise 1, ch. 3), which generalises Cobb-Douglas to arbitrary elasticity of substitution $\sigma \neq 1$. Cozzi sticks with $\sigma = 1$ (the Cobb-Douglas case) throughout, and the EXAM_PROFILE doesn't flag CES as a hotspot. Similarly, the distinction between Hicks-neutral and Harrod-neutral technical progress comes up in Module 2; for the basic model with $B$ constant the distinction is moot.

3. The Core Mechanics — Reducing the Model to One Equation

The basic Solow model has six equations in six unknowns. We will squeeze it down to a single one-dimensional difference equation — the celebrated Solow equation — and read its dynamics off a diagram. Three sub-steps: (a) profit maximisation pins down factor prices, (b) Euler's theorem links income shares to elasticities, (c) substitution collapses everything to the law of motion for capital per worker.

3.1 Factor prices from profit maximisation

Each period the firm chooses $K_t^d$ and $L_t^d$ to maximise pure profits $Y_t - r_t K_t^d - w_t L_t^d$ subject to $Y_t = F(K_t^d, L_t^d)$. The first-order conditions are the standard 'marginal product equals price' from microeconomics:

$$F'_K(K_t^d, L_t^d) = r_t, \qquad F'_L(K_t^d, L_t^d) = w_t$$

These two equations don't pin down the levels of $K^d$ and $L^d$ — only the ratio $K^d/L^d$, because of CRS. To close it, use competitive market clearing: capital demand equals the predetermined stock $K_t$, and labour demand equals the predetermined labour force $L_t$. Then plug Cobb-Douglas in. The factor prices become:

$$r_t = \alpha B \left(\frac{K_t}{L_t}\right)^{\alpha - 1}, \qquad w_t = (1 - \alpha) B \left(\frac{K_t}{L_t}\right)^{\alpha}$$

Pause and read these. The rental rate $r_t$ depends only on $K/L$ — the capital-labour ratio. The more capital per worker, the lower its marginal product (and thus its rental rate), because of diminishing returns. The wage $w_t$ also depends only on $K/L$, but in the opposite direction: more capital per worker raises labour's marginal product (capital and labour are complements) and so raises the wage. This is why long-run growth in $k$ implies long-run growth in $w$ — exactly stylized fact 6.

3.2 Euler's theorem: income shares are output elasticities

A consequence of CRS (Euler's theorem on homogeneous functions): $F(K, L) = F'_K \cdot K + F'_L \cdot L$. In words, paying each factor its marginal product exhausts the total product. There are no pure profits — every cent of output goes to either capital or labour. Each factor's share of total income is therefore:

$$\frac{r_t K_t}{Y_t} = \frac{F'_K \cdot K_t}{F(K_t, L_t)}, \qquad \frac{w_t L_t}{Y_t} = \frac{F'_L \cdot L_t}{F(K_t, L_t)}$$

That right-hand-side ratio is exactly the elasticity of $F$ with respect to that factor. With Cobb-Douglas, $F'_K \cdot K = \alpha \cdot B K^{\alpha} L^{1-\alpha} = \alpha Y$, so capital's share is exactly $\alpha$ — a constant, independent of the $K/L$ mix. Labour's share is exactly $1 - \alpha$. This is the magic of Cobb-Douglas: stylized facts 6-7 are matched automatically. Pick $\alpha = 1/3$ and you reproduce the empirical $2/3$ labour share, and you do so for any value of $K/L$ the economy might pass through.

3.3 Reducing the model to one equation

Now to collapse the six equations to a single law of motion for $k_t$. Figure 4 shows the flow of resources we are bookkeeping — output splits into consumption and investment, investment adds to the capital stock, depreciation subtracts, and population growth dilutes the per-worker capital each year.

Now the algebra. Plug $S_t = s Y_t$ into the capital accumulation equation:

$$K_{t+1} = s Y_t + (1 - \delta) K_t$$

Divide by $L_{t+1} = (1+n) L_t$ to get next period's capital per worker. The trick is that $K_{t+1}/L_{t+1} = k_{t+1}$ on the left, but on the right we have to write $L_{t+1} = (1+n) L_t$ to convert $K_{t+1}/L_{t+1}$ into something involving $K_{t+1}/L_t = k_{t+1}(1+n)$. After cancelling:

$$k_{t+1} = \frac{1}{1+n}\left[ s \, y_t + (1 - \delta) k_t \right]$$

This is the transition equation. The $(1+n)$ in the denominator is what Lecture 1b p16 calls the 'technical term appearing because of discrete time' — it captures the dilution of per-worker capital by population growth. Now substitute the per-worker production function $y_t = B k_t^{\alpha}$:

$$k_{t+1} = \frac{1}{1+n}\left[ s B k_t^{\alpha} + (1 - \delta) k_t \right]$$

Subtract $k_t$ from both sides, simplify the resulting expression carefully (a couple of algebraic lines you should verify), and you arrive at the famous form:

The Solow equation:$$\Delta k_t \equiv k_{t+1} - k_t = \frac{1}{1+n}\Big[ \underbrace{s B k_t^{\alpha}}_{\text{saving per worker}} - \underbrace{(n + \delta) k_t}_{\text{break-even investment}} \Big]$$Reading this: capital per worker grows when current saving per worker $s y_t = s B k_t^{\alpha}$ exceeds what is needed just to maintain the existing $k$ in the face of depreciation ($\delta k_t$) and population dilution ($n k_t$). The combined per-worker requirement $(n + \delta) k_t$ is called break-even investment. When saving exactly covers break-even, $k$ is constant — that is the steady state.

3.4 The Solow diagram and the transition diagram

Figure 5 plots the model both ways. The left panel — the Solow diagram — has $k$ on the horizontal axis and per-worker flows on the vertical: the concave blue curve $sBk^{\alpha}$ (saving per worker) and the straight red ray $(n+\delta) k$ (break-even investment). Where they cross is the steady state $k^*$. To the LEFT of $k^*$ the blue curve sits above the red ray — saving exceeds break-even, so $\Delta k > 0$ and capital is growing. To the RIGHT, the red ray is above the blue curve — depreciation and dilution outrun saving, so $\Delta k < 0$ and capital is shrinking. The economy is pulled toward $k^*$ from either side.

The right panel — the transition diagram — is the same story expressed differently. It plots $k_{t+1}$ on the vertical axis as a function of $k_t$ on the horizontal, along with the $45°$ line $k_{t+1} = k_t$. Where the transition curve crosses the $45°$ line is again $k^*$. The geometry guarantees a unique positive crossing: the curve starts at the origin, has infinite slope there (because $\alpha < 1$), and ends with slope $(1-\delta)/(1+n) < 1$ — so it must cross the $45°$ line exactly once away from zero. Drag $s$ in Figure 5 and watch $k^*$ migrate right in both panels simultaneously.

3.5 Why is convergence guaranteed?

Diminishing returns. As $k$ rises, $\partial y/\partial k = \alpha B k^{\alpha-1}$ falls (because $\alpha - 1 < 0$), so the extra saving generated by an extra unit of $k$ shrinks. Meanwhile break-even investment keeps growing linearly in $k$. Eventually the line catches the curve. Diminishing returns is not a quirk of Cobb-Douglas; it is a hardwired property of any neoclassical production function. The convergence story we just told is robust to the choice of $F$ as long as the basic properties hold.

One quick note on the public sector. The model has a clean government extension (Lecture 1c p3, textbook ch3 pp61-62). Reinterpret $K_t$ as private+public capital, $S_t$ as the sum of private and public saving, and $s$ as the implied national savings rate $S_t/Y_t$. As long as total consumption (private + public) is a constant fraction of GDP, the model goes through with no further changes. Public investment shows up in $s$ (raising it), public consumption shows up against $s$ (lowering it). That's enough to talk about fiscal policy in a long-run growth context.

4. Properties and Sharp Predictions

Now the model's predictions. We will derive the steady-state formulas, read off elasticity predictions from the log form, work through comparative statics, calibrate the famous 'natural interest rate' (3.3%!), establish conditional convergence as a model property, and characterise the optimal savings rate.

4.1 Solving for the steady state

In steady state $k_{t+1} = k_t = k^*$, so $\Delta k = 0$. From the Solow equation that requires saving per worker to exactly equal break-even investment:

$$s B (k^*)^{\alpha} = (n + \delta) k^*$$

Divide both sides by $(k^*)^{\alpha}$ and isolate $k^*$:

$$k^* = B^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}} \left(\frac{s}{n+\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}$$

That's the steady-state capital intensity. Plug into $y_t = B k_t^{\alpha}$ to get steady-state output per worker:

$$y^* = B^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}} \left(\frac{s}{n+\delta}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}$$

Same trick for the other variables. The full steady-state table:

VariableFormulaWhat it tells you
$k^*$$B^{1/(1-\alpha)} \left(s/(n+\delta)\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)}$Long-run capital per worker
$y^*$$B^{1/(1-\alpha)} \left(s/(n+\delta)\right)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$Long-run income per worker
$c^*$$(1 - s) y^*$Long-run consumption per worker
$w^*$$(1 - \alpha) y^*$Long-run real wage
$r^*$$\alpha \left(s/(n+\delta)\right)^{-1}$Long-run rental rate of capital
$\rho^*$$r^* - \delta$Long-run real interest rate ('natural rate')

4.2 Sharp elasticity predictions

Take logs of $y^*$:

$$\ln y^* = \frac{1}{1-\alpha} \ln B + \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} \big[\ln s - \ln(n + \delta)\big]$$

Now read off elasticities directly from the coefficients. With $\alpha \approx 1/3$:

VariableElasticity of $y^*$ wrt. itNumerical value (α=1/3)Reading
$s$$\alpha / (1-\alpha)$$\approx 1/2$10% rise in $s$ → 5% rise in $y^*$
$n + \delta$$-\alpha / (1-\alpha)$$\approx -1/2$Symmetric on the downside
$B$$1 / (1-\alpha)$$\approx 3/2$Stronger than one-to-one!

The last one deserves an explanation. Why is the $B$-elasticity stronger than one? Because higher $B$ raises the level of production directly AND it triggers additional capital accumulation (more output → more saving at given $s$ → more capital → more output again). The two effects compound, giving an elasticity of $1/(1-\alpha) > 1$. This is the model's strongest steady-state prediction — and one of the reasons technology is the deepest lever for prosperity.

4.3 Comparative statics: a permanent increase in s

Imagine the economy sitting at its old steady state when, in some year zero, the savings rate jumps permanently from $s$ to $s' > s$. Figure 6 shows what happens. The Solow diagram shifts: the saving curve $sBk^{\alpha}$ rotates upward to $s'Bk^{\alpha}$ — the same concave shape, just scaled higher. The old $k^*$ is no longer a steady state: at $k^*$ the new curve $s'Bk^{\alpha}$ lies above the break-even ray $(n+\delta)k$, so $\Delta k > 0$ and capital starts accumulating. It keeps accumulating until reaching the new (higher) steady state $k^{**}$. Output per worker follows: $y^{**} > y^*$.

A different comparative-statics scenario: an exogenous event (war, earthquake) wipes out half the capital stock overnight. Now nothing about the curves changes — only the position of $k_t$ moves. At $k^*/2$ we sit far below steady state, so $\Delta k > 0$ (large). The economy recovers, with high growth at first, slowing as it approaches $k^*$ from below. This 'postwar boom' is part of how the Solow model rationalises the strong recoveries of Japan and West Germany after WWII (textbook ch3 Exercise 10 walks the Japan case out numerically).

4.4 The 'natural' interest rate: productivity and thrift

Plug Cozzi's reference parameters ($\alpha = 1/3$, $s = 0.22$, $n = 0.005$, $\delta = 0.05$) into $\rho^* = \alpha(s/(n+\delta))^{-1} - \delta$:

$$\rho^* = \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{n + \delta}{s} - \delta = \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{0.055}{0.22} - 0.05 = 0.0833 - 0.05 = 0.0333$$

So the model predicts a long-run real interest rate of about 3.3% per year. That number is strikingly close to the long-run average real return on government bonds in advanced economies. For a model this simple, getting the natural rate within a fraction of a percentage point of empirical estimates is a genuine empirical win — and is one of the reasons Lecture 1c p12 lingers on this calculation.

Knut Wicksell (early 20th century) called this the 'natural rate of interest' and described it as set by productivity (which raises capital's marginal product and thus the demand price) and thrift (which raises capital supply and so lowers the price). Both forces fall out of the Solow steady-state formula automatically. Higher $\alpha$ (capital is more productive) → higher $\rho^*$. Higher $s/(n+\delta)$ (more thrift relative to dilution) → lower $\rho^*$.

4.5 Modified Solow equation and conditional convergence

Divide the Solow equation through by $k_t$:

$$\frac{\Delta k_t}{k_t} = \frac{1}{1+n}\left[ s B k_t^{\alpha - 1} - (n + \delta) \right]$$

This is the modified Solow equation. It says the growth rate of capital per worker is proportional to the gap between $s B k_t^{\alpha - 1}$ (which is decreasing in $k$ because $\alpha - 1 < 0$) and the constant $n + \delta$. The further below $k^*$ the economy sits, the larger that gap, and the higher the growth rate. This is conditional convergence appearing as a model property: among economies with the same parameters $(s, n, \delta, B, \alpha)$, the one further from $k^*$ grows faster.

It is conditional because if two economies have different parameters they are converging to different $k^*$'s — there's no reason they should reach the same level. Cross-country, the model predicts that economies will not all converge to a common income level (absolute convergence) but should converge to their own structural steady states. We will see in §6 how this plays out in the data.

4.6 The golden rule of saving

How much should the country save, from a long-run welfare standpoint? Higher $s$ gives higher $y^*$, but it also takes a larger bite for investment: $c^* = (1 - s) y^*$. So there's a hill — too little saving means low income, too much means low consumption out of high income. Figure 7 shows the hill explicitly.

Golden rule of saving:$$s^{**} = \alpha$$The consumption-maximising savings rate equals the capital share. Derivation (textbook ch3 p72, also Exercise 7): substitute $c^* = (1-s) B^{1/(1-\alpha)} (s/(n+\delta))^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$, take $\partial c^* / \partial s = 0$, solve. The 'hill' is geometrically the vertical gap between $Bk^{\alpha}$ and $(n+\delta)k$ in a Solow diagram, maximised at the $k^{**}$ where the slope of the production curve equals the slope of the break-even ray.

With $\alpha \approx 1/3$, the golden rule is about 33%. Most developed countries save around 22% — below the golden rule. The basic Solow model thus suggests policy could raise long-run welfare by encouraging more saving (institutions, tax incentives, pension design — Lecture 1c p15 catalogues them). Bear in mind: pushing $s$ higher costs current consumption during the transition (the early years of the simulation in §5 show this clearly), and the basic model has no utility function to evaluate that intertemporal trade-off. So 'go to $s^{**}$' is suggestive, not prescriptive. But the direction of the intuition is robust.

5.2 Cross-country evidence: do the predicted scatter patterns show up?

The Solow model makes two specific cross-country predictions: $\ln y^*$ should rise with $\ln s$ at slope $\alpha/(1-\alpha) \approx 1/2$, and fall with $\ln(n+\delta)$ at slope $-\alpha/(1-\alpha) \approx -1/2$. Both directions show up clearly in the data.

Figure 9 plots GDP per worker against the average investment rate for the representative 65-country sample used in the textbook (ch3 Fig 3.7 — and reproduced in Lecture 1b p22). There's a clear positive relationship: countries that have saved more, on average, have higher long-run income per worker — exactly as Solow predicts. The same exercise with $n$ on the x-axis (textbook Fig 3.8) gives a clean negative slope. Both qualitative tests pass.

There is a more demanding version of the test, walked through in textbook ch3 Exercise 9 (and one of the most important exercises in the chapter). Plot $\ln y$ against $\ln s - \ln(n + \delta)$ and fit a line. The slope should be $\alpha/(1-\alpha) \approx 0.5$. The actual fitted slope, across this sample, is closer to $1.0$ — meaning the basic Solow model under-predicts the strength of investment-rate effects on income. The empirical slope says you need $\alpha \approx 0.5$ (a capital share of 50%) to fit the data, but income-share data say $\alpha \approx 1/3$. This is the famous Mankiw-Romer-Weil (MRW 1992) puzzle that Module 3 tries to fix by adding human capital — bumping the effective elasticity from $\alpha/(1-\alpha)$ to $(\alpha+\varphi)/(1 - \alpha - \varphi)$.

Drag the $\alpha$ slider in Figure 9 to see how the predicted slope of the fit line varies. With $\alpha = 0.5$ the line tilts up steeply (matching the data slope); with $\alpha = 0.33$ the slope is gentler than the cloud of points suggests. That mismatch is the MRW puzzle made visual.

A side example worth a mention: crowding out. Suppose the government permanently raises its consumption share, reducing the national savings rate $s$ to a new lower level. On impact, GDP is unaffected (capital and labour are predetermined), but savings drop — so private investment falls one-for-one with the new public consumption. Full crowding out in the short run. In the long run things are worse: lower $s$ means lower $k^*$ and lower $y^*$, so output gradually falls below where it would otherwise have been. More-than-full crowding out. The government cannot raise long-run GDP per worker by consuming more (Lecture 1c p13; textbook ch3 pp70-71). The crowding-out story is the basic Solow's main fiscal-policy lesson and one of the things Cozzi expects you to be able to narrate cleanly.

6.4 Common traps

Trap 1: Confusing transitory and steady-state growth. The basic Solow model gives zero steady-state growth in $y$. It does produce transitory growth during convergence — that transition lasts decades (§5.1) — but indefinite growth in $y$ is OUT of basic Solow. Don't say 'Solow explains the 2% per year' on the exam; it explains transient catch-up, not permanent $g_y$.
Trap 2: Galton's fallacy. If you plot two random variables with the same range, you'll get a negative correlation between initial value and change just from regression-to-mean. So a negative growth/initial-$y$ correlation is suggestive but not by itself proof of convergence. Textbook ch2 Exercise 7 walks through a simulation where 26 imaginary countries get random initial and final $y$'s — the negative slope appears even though nothing is converging. Be careful citing 'convergence' as the explanation for negative correlation.
Trap 3: 'Balanced growth' in the Solow steady state vs. in the real world. In real Western economies, balanced growth holds with $y$, $c$, $w$, $K/L$ all rising at common rate $g \approx 2\%$ — see stylized facts 5-7. In a basic Solow steady state, balanced growth also holds, but with $g = 0$. Same definition, different value of $g$. The basic Solow model's steady state is a degenerate case of balanced growth. The conflict resolves only once we add technological progress (Module 2), which lets steady-state $g$ be positive.

7. Summary — Levers, Formulas, and the Big Takeaway

The basic Solow model in one sentence: a country's long-run income per worker is determined by its saving rate, population growth, depreciation rate, and technology, with diminishing returns to capital ensuring convergence to a unique steady state. Everything else — the diagrams, the formulas, the comparative statics, the policy implications — is just consequence of that sentence plus the Cobb-Douglas form.

7.3 The big takeaway

The basic Solow model is a remarkably economical machine. From a handful of behavioural assumptions and one production function, it generates testable predictions about cross-country income differences, the natural interest rate, transitional dynamics, conditional convergence, and the optimal savings rate. The match with data is partial: signs correct in nearly every case, magnitudes too weak in several (notably the MRW investment-rate elasticity), and the deepest problem (zero long-run growth in $y$ contradicting stylized fact 5) just unfixable inside the basic model.

The rest of this course is essentially a sequence of fixes — each module patches one of those gaps. Module 2 patches zero growth, Module 3 patches the magnitude puzzles with human capital, Modules 4-5 endogenise technological progress, Module 6 questions the underlying full-employment assumption. So when Cozzi asks 'describe the main empirical evidence supporting and contradicting the basic Solow model' on the exam, what he really wants is for you to set up the gaps that the rest of the course has been filling. That's why this lecture is the foundation of everything that follows.

One last practical pointer: when you sit down to answer Mock Q1, the structure that scores best is (a) lay out the model in 2-3 lines (production function, savings rule, capital accumulation, key implication that $k$ converges to a finite $k^*$); (b) give 3-4 supporting empirical points with the specific evidence (scatter directions, calibrated $\rho^*$, conditional convergence regression); (c) give 3-4 contradicting empirical points with the specific puzzles ($g_y = 0$ in SS, MRW elasticity, slow convergence rate); (d) close with one sentence on what later modules do to patch the gaps.
