1. The Big Picture: Two Patches to Basic Solow

Two stories, one module. The basic Solow model from Module 1 gave us two answers that don't quite match the world. First, it said the long-run growth rate of GDP per worker is zero — capital piles up, hits diminishing returns, and the per-worker stock stops growing forever. But Western economies have grown roughly 2% per worker per year for over a century. Something is missing. Second, it said the only source of investment is domestic saving — a perfectly natural assumption in 1956 when capital controls were strict, but visibly false today. By 2007 the world stock of cross-border portfolio investment had grown nine times faster than world GDP. The closed-economy premise is itself an artefact of the postwar era.

Module 2 patches both leaks. Two extensions, taken in this lecture in order:

  1. Open the capital account. Drop the assumption that domestic investment equals domestic saving. Let capital flow freely across borders. The world interest rate $\bar{r}$ becomes exogenous to the small economy; domestic capital intensity adjusts instantly to equate the marginal product of capital with $\bar{r}$. (Lecture 2a / Chapter 4.)
  2. Let technology improve over time. Replace the constant TFP $B$ with a sequence $B_t$ (or equivalently a labour-augmenting productivity $A_t$) growing at some exogenous rate $g$. The model then predicts a balanced growth path — a long-run trajectory along which all per-worker quantities ($k_t$, $y_t$, $c_t$, $w_t$) grow at one common constant positive rate, the capital-output ratio $K/Y$ is constant, and the real interest rate is constant. In this model that common rate equals $g$, the rate of technical progress. (Lecture 2b / Chapter 5.)

These two extensions are independent: you can do either without the other. We treat them in one module because they are the natural pair of one-step refinements to basic Solow. Both keep the Solow architecture; both reset what 'steady state' means; both leave one thing exogenous that later modules try to endogenize. In Module 3 we'll add human capital and natural resources. In Modules 4-5 we'll endogenize the technical progress itself.

The exam-relevant question to keep in mind for each extension: what changes, what stays the same?

For open economy, the headline is unambiguous and Cozzi states it explicitly in the lecture summary: opening the capital account always raises long-run national income per capita. The wage rate goes the other way in a savings-strong country — it falls. So the model has a clear policy verdict on capital mobility, plus a clear distributional warning that explains a lot of the politics of globalization.

For technical progress, the headline is more subtle. Cobb-Douglas production with labour-augmenting technology delivers exactly the balanced growth path the data demands; the model passes its empirical test on per-worker growth. But it overestimates how fast countries converge to their growth paths (predicts 5% per year, data say 0.7%) and underestimates how strongly the structural parameters shape steady-state income (predicts elasticity 0.5, data say 1.5). These quantitative shortcomings are what Module 3's human-capital extension tries to fix.

The plan: define the new objects (§2), derive the laws of motion (§3), characterize the steady states and prove the key theorems (§4), run a concrete numerical example for each (§5), check what the data says and flag the common traps (§6), then a summary with a formula sheet (§7).

2. Object Definitions: What Each New Symbol Means

Two unrelated extensions mean two unrelated sets of new objects. Take them in order.

2.1 Open economy: three quantities, not one

In a closed economy, the only stock that matters is the domestic capital stock $K_t$. In an open economy, three stocks coexist, and confusing them is the single most common error on Cozzi's exams.

National wealth $V_t$ is the total stock of assets owned by the country's residents. It can be invested at home (in domestic capital) or abroad (in foreign assets).

Domestic capital $K_t$ is the stock of physical capital located inside the country, regardless of who owns it. Foreigners may own part of it; domestic residents may own part of foreign capital.

Net foreign assets $F_t$ is the difference: assets domestic residents own abroad minus the part of domestic capital owned by foreigners. By definition:

$$V_t = K_t + F_t$$

Symbol reading: $V_t$ is wealth-by-ownership, $K_t$ is capital-by-location, $F_t$ is the net foreign claim — positive if 'we own more of them than they own of us,' negative otherwise. A country with $F_t > 0$ is a creditor; a country with $F_t < 0$ is a debtor. These terms describe the sign of $F_t$ and nothing more — they're not moral judgements.

There's a parallel split on income. Income earned inside the borders is gross domestic product (GDP), denoted $Y_t$. Income earned by the country's residents — wherever those residents have invested — is gross national product (GNP), denoted $Y^n_t$. Since net foreign assets earn the world interest rate $\bar{r}$:

$$Y^n_t = Y_t + \bar{r} F_t$$

A creditor has $Y^n_t > Y_t$ (collects interest from abroad); a debtor has $Y^n_t < Y_t$ (pays interest abroad). The fact that we now have two measures of national income — produced vs received — is the single most exam-trap-prone consequence of opening the capital account.

For the saving rule, the natural assumption in the open economy is that national savings is a constant fraction $s$ of national income (not domestic product, because the residents save out of what they actually earn):

$$S_t = s Y^n_t$$

Figure 1 below makes the comparison between closed and open accounting visible at a glance.

Three assumptions define what Cozzi calls a small open economy:

  • Small: the country is too tiny to affect the world interest rate $\bar{r}$ — it takes $\bar{r}$ as given.
  • Open: capital can move freely across borders, and so can goods (which are all assumed identical to a single 'world good', so we don't have a theory of trade patterns here).
  • Labour immobile: no migration. Why? Empirically labour really does move much less than capital and goods, and the assumption lets the model isolate the effects of capital mobility cleanly.

Throughout Lecture 2a we also set $\delta = 0$ as a simplifying assumption. Failure mode if $\delta = 0$ fails to hold: the analysis goes through almost identically, but the rental rate $r_t$ no longer equals the real interest rate exactly ($r_t = \bar{r} + \delta$ instead). The qualitative results are unchanged; the algebra just gets one more term.

Under perfect capital mobility the domestic real interest rate must equal $\bar{r}$. Any wedge would create an arbitrage: investors would dump the lower-yielding asset until prices equalized. Combined with Cobb-Douglas production $Y_t = B K_t^{\alpha} L_t^{1-\alpha}$ and competitive markets, the rental rate equation pins down domestic capital intensity in every single period. We'll see in §3 that $k_t$ adjusts instantly, while in the closed economy it crept along — that's the central change of behaviour caused by opening up.

2.2 Technological progress: effective workers

Now to the second extension. In Module 1's basic Solow model, total factor productivity was a constant $B$. Now let it depend on time:

$$Y_t = K_t^{\alpha} (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}, \quad A_t = (1+g)^t A_0$$

Symbol reading: $A_t$ is the labour-augmenting productivity variable, growing exogenously at the constant rate $g$ per period from an initial level $A_0$. The product $A_t L_t$ is the country's input of effective labour (sometimes called 'efficiency units of labour'): the headcount $L_t$, scaled up by how productive each headcount unit is.

Why call it labour-augmenting? Because $A_t$ multiplies $L$, not $K$. Technology shows up as if each worker were doing more. There are other equivalent ways to model neutral technical progress, and you should be able to identify them:

FormProduction functionName
TFP-multiplier$B_t \cdot F(K_t, L_t)$Hicks-neutral
Capital-augmenting$F(D_t K_t, L_t)$Solow-neutral
Labour-augmenting$F(K_t, A_t L_t)$Harrod-neutral

For Cobb-Douglas (and only for Cobb-Douglas) these three forms are mathematically equivalent: $A_t = B_t^{1/(1-\alpha)}$, so a sequence $(B_t)$ for TFP and a sequence $(A_t)$ for labour-augmenting productivity carry exactly the same information. The labour-augmenting form turns out to be the most convenient for steady-state analysis, so we'll use it.

Beyond exam scope, but useful: outside the Cobb-Douglas case, the three forms generate genuinely different model predictions, and there's a classic result (Uzawa's theorem) saying only Harrod-neutral progress is compatible with balanced growth. For our purposes the equivalence under Cobb-Douglas is what matters.

The natural per-worker quantities now have to adjust. Define:

$$\tilde{k}_t \equiv \frac{K_t}{A_t L_t}, \quad \tilde{y}_t \equiv \frac{Y_t}{A_t L_t}$$

These are capital per effective worker and output per effective worker. The tilde distinguishes them from the per-headcount versions $k_t = K_t / L_t$ and $y_t = Y_t / L_t$ we used in Module 1. The notation matters: $k_t$ grows forever in steady state, but $\tilde{k}_t$ is constant.

Why bother defining new variables? Because the analytical strategy is to find quantities that are constant in the long run, even though the underlying per-worker quantities are growing forever. If $\tilde{k}_t$ and $\tilde{y}_t$ are constant in steady state, then we can analyse the model exactly like the basic Solow — same diagrams, same stability arguments — substituting 'effective' for 'headcount.' That hope pans out, as §3 will show.

3. The Core Mechanics: Deriving the Laws of Motion

Now the laws of motion. We'll do the open economy first (Lecture 2a), then technological progress (Lecture 2b).

3.1 Open economy: what jumps and what crawls

Start with the firm's problem. Cobb-Douglas production $Y_t = B K_t^{\alpha} L_t^{1-\alpha}$; the firm picks $(K_t, L_t)$ to maximize profit at given factor prices. The first-order conditions equate marginal products with real factor prices:

$$r_t = \frac{\partial Y_t}{\partial K_t} = \alpha B \left(\frac{K_t}{L_t}\right)^{\alpha-1}, \quad w_t = (1-\alpha) B \left(\frac{K_t}{L_t}\right)^{\alpha}$$

Symbol reading: $r_t$ is the rental rate (marginal product) of capital, $w_t$ the wage (marginal product of labour). Both depend only on the capital-labour ratio $K_t/L_t \equiv k_t$, not on the levels of $K$ and $L$ separately — a familiar Cobb-Douglas property.

Here's the move that gives the open economy its distinctive flavour. The domestic real interest rate equals the world rate $\bar{r}$ (perfect mobility, $\delta = 0$ so rental rate = interest rate). Set $r_t = \bar{r}$ in the FOC above and solve for $k_t$. Notice — what we're doing is reading off the capital-labour ratio that makes the marginal product hit a target. So set:

$$\bar{r} = \alpha B k_t^{\alpha-1}$$

Now isolate $k_t$. Divide both sides by $\alpha B$ to get $k_t^{\alpha-1} = \bar{r}/(\alpha B)$. Raise both sides to the power $1/(\alpha - 1)$, which is the same as raising to $-1/(1-\alpha)$:

$$\boxed{k_t = k^{*} = B^{1/(1-\alpha)} \left(\frac{\alpha}{\bar{r}}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)}}$$

Notice what this is and what it isn't. It is not a steady-state condition reached only after gradual adjustment. It is a condition that must hold in every period — at any moment the marginal product of domestic capital must equal $\bar{r}$, otherwise capital would flow in or out until it did. So $k_t$ jumps to $k^*$ in period one and stays there forever, independent of how much wealth the country has accumulated. This is called immediate adjustment.

Because $k_t$ is pinned, so are $y_t$ and $w_t$. They jump immediately to:

$$y^{*} = B \cdot (k^*)^{\alpha} = B^{1/(1-\alpha)} \left(\frac{\alpha}{\bar{r}}\right)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}, \quad w^{*} = (1-\alpha) y^{*}$$

So domestic capital per worker, GDP per worker, and the real wage all hit their steady-state values from period one. Nothing crawls. This is the big contrast with closed-economy Solow, where the capital stock built up gradually toward its steady-state level. Under perfect capital mobility, the world capital market absorbs or supplies whatever domestic firms need, instantly.

But wealth per worker $v_t$ is different. The capital that firms use sits at $k^*$ from day one, but the wealth the country owns depends on its saving history. So $v_t$ crawls. To derive its law of motion, we need first a clean expression for national income.

3.1.1 Decomposing national income

Start from the per-worker version of the GNP identity, $y^n_t = y_t + \bar{r} f_t$ where $f_t = F_t / L_t$. Now use $f_t = v_t - k_t$ (per-worker form of $V = K + F$):

$$y^n_t = y_t + \bar{r}(v_t - k_t) = (y_t - \bar{r} k_t) + \bar{r} v_t$$

The trick is to recognize what $y_t - \bar{r} k_t$ is. From the firm's FOCs, total income paid to factors equals output (Cobb-Douglas + perfect competition, no pure profits): $y_t = r_t k_t + w_t$. With $r_t = \bar{r}$ (open economy), this rearranges to $y_t - \bar{r} k_t = w_t$. Substitute:

National income decomposition.

$$y^n_t = w_t + \bar{r} v_t$$

National income per capita has two parts: the wage you get for working ($w_t = w^*$ always, since $k_t = k^*$ always), and the interest income on whatever wealth you've accumulated ($\bar{r} v_t$).

This decomposition will keep showing up. Memorize it — Cozzi uses it three times in the steady-state derivation and twice more in the welfare theorem.

3.1.2 The wealth transition equation

The intertemporal budget constraint for the country says wealth changes by saving: $V_{t+1} - V_t = S_t$ (with $\delta = 0$, all wealth that isn't consumed accumulates). Re-state $S_t = s Y^n_t$ from the saving rule. Combine:

$$V_{t+1} = V_t + s Y^n_t$$

Divide both sides by $L_{t+1} = (1+n) L_t$ to convert to per-worker terms. Recall the population growth equation $L_{t+1} = (1+n) L_t$ from Module 1:

$$v_{t+1} = \frac{V_{t+1}}{L_{t+1}} = \frac{V_t + s Y^n_t}{(1+n) L_t} = \frac{1}{1+n}(v_t + s y^n_t)$$

Now substitute the national income decomposition $y^n_t = w^* + \bar{r} v_t$ (using $w_t = w^*$):

$$v_{t+1} = \frac{1}{1+n}\bigl(v_t + s(w^* + \bar{r} v_t)\bigr) = \frac{1}{1+n}\bigl((1 + s\bar{r}) v_t + s w^*\bigr)$$

Pull everything together:

Open-economy wealth transition equation.

$$v_{t+1} = \frac{1+s\bar{r}}{1+n} v_t + \frac{s w^*}{1+n}$$

A first-order linear difference equation with slope $(1+s\bar{r})/(1+n)$ and intercept $sw^*/(1+n)$.

Figure 2 plots it. Notice that the transition curve is a straight line, not the concave curve of the basic Solow model. Why? Because every extra unit of wealth earns the same return $\bar{r}$ regardless of how much wealth has already piled up. There are no diminishing returns to wealth in the open economy, only to domestic capital — and domestic capital is no longer the binding constraint, since whatever isn't justified by $r = \bar{r}$ flows abroad.

The slope $(1+s\bar{r})/(1+n)$ tells you how much next period's wealth scales with this period's. As long as that slope is less than 1, the transition curve eventually crosses the 45° line, and the economy converges to a steady-state wealth level $v^*$. That condition — $s\bar{r} < n$ — is the open-economy stability condition we'll prove reasonable in §4. 3.2 General Solow: derive the same things in (effective) per-worker terms

The plan: write the system in terms of $\tilde{k}$ and $\tilde{y}$, exactly mimicking the Module 1 analysis but with $A_t L_t$ replacing $L_t$. We need three pieces: production, accumulation, transition.

3.2.1 Per-effective-worker production

Start from the production function $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha} (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}$. Divide both sides by $A_t L_t$:

$$\frac{Y_t}{A_t L_t} = \frac{K_t^{\alpha} (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}}{A_t L_t} = \frac{K_t^{\alpha}}{(A_t L_t)^{\alpha}} = \left(\frac{K_t}{A_t L_t}\right)^{\alpha}$$

That is:

$$\boxed{\tilde{y}_t = \tilde{k}_t^{\alpha}}$$

Exactly the same per-worker production function as basic Solow — just decorated with tildes. The diminishing-returns geometry is preserved.

3.2.2 Capital accumulation per effective worker

The fundamental accumulation equation is the same as in Module 1: $K_{t+1} = sY_t + (1-\delta)K_t$. To convert to per-effective-worker, divide both sides by $A_{t+1} L_{t+1}$. The left side becomes $\tilde{k}_{t+1}$:

$$\tilde{k}_{t+1} = \frac{K_{t+1}}{A_{t+1} L_{t+1}} = \frac{sY_t + (1-\delta) K_t}{A_{t+1} L_{t+1}}$$

Now manipulate the right side. The denominator $A_{t+1} L_{t+1}$ equals $(1+g) A_t \cdot (1+n) L_t = (1+n)(1+g) A_t L_t$, using the laws of motion for $A$ and $L$. In the numerator, $sY_t / (A_t L_t) = s \tilde{y}_t$ and $(1-\delta) K_t / (A_t L_t) = (1-\delta) \tilde{k}_t$. So:

$$\tilde{k}_{t+1} = \frac{1}{(1+n)(1+g)} \bigl(s \tilde{y}_t + (1-\delta) \tilde{k}_t\bigr)$$

Re-state $\tilde{y}_t = \tilde{k}_t^{\alpha}$ to substitute:

General Solow transition equation.

$$\tilde{k}_{t+1} = \frac{1}{(1+n)(1+g)} \bigl(s \tilde{k}_t^{\alpha} + (1-\delta) \tilde{k}_t\bigr)$$

Subtract $\tilde{k}_t$ from both sides to get the Solow equation:

$$\tilde{k}_{t+1} - \tilde{k}_t = \frac{1}{(1+n)(1+g)} \bigl(s \tilde{k}_t^{\alpha} - (n + g + \delta + ng) \tilde{k}_t\bigr)$$

The combination $(n + g + \delta + ng)$ is the effective break-even rate: the share of $\tilde{k}$ that has to be replaced each period to keep $\tilde{k}$ constant. Population growth $n$ dilutes per-effective-worker capital (more headcount); technical progress $g$ does too (more effectiveness per headcount); depreciation $\delta$ wears it out; the small cross-term $ng$ comes from the discrete-time interaction (typically negligible — about $0.0002$ for plausible values, and often dropped in the textbook to write $n + g + \delta$).

Compare to the basic Solow break-even $(n + \delta)$ — adding technical progress just adds $g$ (plus the negligible cross-term) to what the saving curve has to compensate for. That's the only structural change.

4. Properties and Proofs: Stability, Steady States, and the Welfare Theorem

From the laws of motion to their consequences.

4.1 Open economy: stability

The transition curve in Figure 2 must be flatter than the 45° line for the economy to converge — otherwise iterations diverge. Algebraically:

$$\frac{1 + s\bar{r}}{1 + n} < 1 \;\Leftrightarrow\; s\bar{r} < n$$

Stability condition (open economy): $s\bar{r} < n$.

Is this reasonable? Here's Cozzi's argument. If the whole world were closed, the world interest rate would be set by world savings and population growth — and the world is closed in aggregate, since one country's foreign assets are another country's foreign liabilities. So $\bar{r} = \alpha \bar{n}/\bar{s}$, where bars denote world averages (this is the closed-economy Solow steady-state interest rate from Module 1, applied at the world level). The stability condition becomes:

$$s\bar{r} < n \;\Leftrightarrow\; s \cdot \alpha \frac{\bar{n}}{\bar{s}} < n \;\Leftrightarrow\; \alpha \cdot \frac{s}{n} < \frac{\bar{s}}{\bar{n}}$$

Since $\alpha \approx 1/3$ — much smaller than 1 — this holds whenever the small economy isn't radically more saving-prone than the world. Stability is essentially automatic for any country whose savings rate is within a factor of 3 of the world average. Reasonable.

4.2 Open economy: steady-state values

Under stability, set $v_{t+1} = v_t = v^*$ in the transition equation. Re-state the transition equation: $v_{t+1} = ((1+s\bar{r})/(1+n)) v_t + (sw^*/(1+n))$. Substitute the steady-state condition:

$$v^* = \frac{1+s\bar{r}}{1+n} v^* + \frac{s w^*}{1+n}$$

Collect $v^*$ on the left:

$$v^* \left(1 - \frac{1+s\bar{r}}{1+n}\right) = \frac{s w^*}{1+n} \;\Leftrightarrow\; v^* \cdot \frac{n - s\bar{r}}{1+n} = \frac{s w^*}{1+n}$$

The $(1+n)$ factors cancel. Solve for $v^*$:

$$\boxed{v^* = \frac{s}{n - s\bar{r}} w^* = \frac{s/n}{1 - s\bar{r}/n} w^*}$$

Symbol reading: $v^*$ is steady-state wealth per worker; $w^*$ is the (immediately-jumped-to) wage. The denominator $(1 - s\bar{r}/n)$ is positive under stability, and shrinks toward zero as $s\bar{r}$ approaches $n$ — a country that is just barely stable accumulates enormous wealth, like a runaway saving spiral.

National income per capita in steady state: substitute $v^*$ into $y^{n*} = w^* + \bar{r} v^*$. Re-state $v^*$:

$$y^{n*} = w^* + \bar{r} \cdot \frac{s}{n - s\bar{r}} w^* = w^* \left(1 + \frac{s\bar{r}}{n - s\bar{r}}\right) = w^* \cdot \frac{n}{n - s\bar{r}} = \frac{w^*}{1 - s\bar{r}/n}$$

4.2.1 Creditor vs debtor: deriving the cutoff

Net foreign assets in steady state are $f^* = v^* - k^*$. We have $v^*$ in terms of $w^*$; we need $k^*$ in terms of $w^*$. Recall the factor-price decomposition: $w^* = (1-\alpha) B (k^*)^{\alpha} = (1-\alpha) y^*$, and $\bar{r} k^* = \alpha y^*$ (the Cobb-Douglas Euler identity, where $\bar{r}$ plays the role of $r^*$). Divide one by the other:

$$\frac{w^*}{\bar{r} k^*} = \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha} \;\Leftrightarrow\; k^* = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} \cdot \frac{w^*}{\bar{r}}$$

Now form $f^* = v^* - k^*$ using both expressions:

$$f^* = \frac{s w^*}{n - s\bar{r}} - \frac{\alpha w^*}{(1-\alpha) \bar{r}}$$

Factor out $w^*$ and rearrange. After some algebra (Cozzi p20 shows the steps; the punchline is what matters):

$$f^* > 0 \;\Leftrightarrow\; \bar{r} > \frac{\alpha n}{s} \equiv r_c^*$$

where $r_c^* = \alpha n / s$ is the steady-state real interest rate the country would have generated under autarky (from Module 1's closed-economy Solow with $\delta = 0$).

Creditor/debtor cutoff. A country becomes a net creditor ($f^* > 0$) exactly when the world rate $\bar{r}$ exceeds the rate $r_c^*$ it would have generated under autarky. Equivalently — using $r_c^* = \alpha n / s$ and $\bar{r} = \alpha \bar{n}/\bar{s}$ — when $s/n > \bar{s}/\bar{n}$.

Intuition: a savings-strong country (high $s/n$) would, in autarky, accumulate so much capital that the marginal product would fall below the world rate. So when it opens, capital flows out to where it earns more — the country becomes a creditor. The reverse for a savings-weak country: its autarky rate is high, so capital flows in when it opens, making it a debtor.

4.3 The welfare theorem: opening always (weakly) helps

The claim: $y^{n*} \geq y_c^*$, with equality only when $\bar{r} = r_c^*$. Free capital mobility never lowers long-run national income.

The proof works by writing the ratio $x \equiv y^{n*}/y_c^*$ as a function of $\tilde{r} \equiv \bar{r}/r_c^*$ (the world rate normalized by the autarky rate) and showing $x(\tilde{r}) \geq 1$ everywhere. Start by recalling the closed-economy result from Module 1 (with $\delta = 0$):

$$y_c^* = B^{1/(1-\alpha)} \left(\frac{s}{n}\right)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$$

And from §4.2 we had:

$$y^{n*} = (1-\alpha) B^{1/(1-\alpha)} \left(\frac{\alpha}{\bar{r}}\right)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)} \frac{1}{1 - s\bar{r}/n}$$

(using $w^* = (1-\alpha) y^*$ and substituting $y^*$ from §3.) Form the ratio:

$$x = \frac{y^{n*}}{y_c^*} = (1-\alpha) \left(\frac{1}{\bar{r}} \cdot \frac{\alpha n}{s}\right)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)} \cdot \frac{1}{1 - s\bar{r}/n}$$

Now use Cozzi's trick: $\alpha n / s = r_c^*$, so $1/\bar{r} \cdot \alpha n/s = r_c^*/\bar{r} = 1/\tilde{r}$. And $s\bar{r}/n = \alpha \bar{r}/r_c^* = \alpha \tilde{r}$. Substitute both:

$$\boxed{x(\tilde{r}) = (1-\alpha) \left(\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}\right)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)} \cdot \frac{1}{1 - \alpha \tilde{r}}}$$

This is the welfare ratio as a function of one variable. Three properties prove the theorem:

  1. Equality at autarky-equivalent point. Plug $\tilde{r} = 1$: $x = (1-\alpha) \cdot 1 \cdot \frac{1}{1-\alpha} = 1$. When the world rate happens to equal the autarky rate, opening up makes no difference.
  2. Derivative is zero at $\tilde{r} = 1$. Take logs: $\ln x = \ln(1-\alpha) - \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} \ln \tilde{r} - \ln(1 - \alpha \tilde{r})$. Differentiate: $\frac{d\ln x}{d\tilde{r}} = -\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} \cdot \frac{1}{\tilde{r}} + \frac{\alpha}{1 - \alpha \tilde{r}}$. At $\tilde{r} = 1$: $-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} + \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} = 0$.
  3. Second derivative is positive at $\tilde{r} = 1$ (and elsewhere). Differentiate the first-derivative expression — both terms produce positive contributions. So $\tilde{r} = 1$ is a strict minimum of $x(\tilde{r})$.

Conclusion: $x(\tilde{r}) \geq 1$ always, with equality only at $\tilde{r} = 1$.

4.4 Distributional caveat: wages don't always rise

National income going up doesn't mean everyone gains. In a savings-strong country opening up, wages fall. Here's why. The wage depends on $k^* = B^{1/(1-\alpha)} (\alpha/\bar{r})^{1/(1-\alpha)}$, and what determines whether $k^*$ moves up or down on opening is whether $\bar{r}$ is above or below $r_c^*$. Recall the autarky $k$ for a closed economy (Module 1): $k_c^* = (sB/n)^{1/(1-\alpha)}$. Open versus closed:

$$\frac{w^*}{w_c^*} = \left(\frac{k^*}{k_c^*}\right)^{\alpha} = \left(\frac{\alpha n / s}{\bar{r}}\right)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)} = \left(\frac{r_c^*}{\bar{r}}\right)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$$

This is less than 1 exactly when $\bar{r} > r_c^*$ — that is, exactly when the country is a creditor (savings-strong). So savings-strong countries gain in aggregate (Figure 4 says so) but lose at the wage rate. Capital owners gain (they collect a higher return abroad); workers lose. In a debtor country, the reverse: capital owners take a lower return but workers gain via higher wages.

The exam point: 'free capital mobility raises long-run national income' is true and Cozzi makes you derive it. But 'free capital mobility benefits everyone' is false without redistribution — and this distributional caveat is the natural critique of globalization that the textbook explicitly flags.

4.5 General Solow: stability and steady state

The transition curve in Figure 3 must approach a slope less than 1 as $\tilde{k} \to \infty$ for convergence. Differentiate the transition equation (re-stated: $\tilde{k}_{t+1} = [(s\tilde{k}^{\alpha} + (1-\delta)\tilde{k})] / [(1+n)(1+g)]$) and take the limit:

$$\lim_{\tilde{k} \to \infty} \frac{d \tilde{k}_{t+1}}{d \tilde{k}_t} = \frac{1-\delta}{(1+n)(1+g)} < 1 \;\Leftrightarrow\; n + g + \delta + ng > 0$$

This holds for any sensible parameter values — everything in $n+g+\delta+ng$ is non-negative and at least one is strictly positive. So stability is automatic in the general Solow (unlike in the open economy, where it had to be argued for).

Set $\tilde{k}_{t+1} = \tilde{k}_t = \tilde{k}^*$ in the Solow equation (re-state: $\tilde{k}_{t+1} - \tilde{k}_t = [(s\tilde{k}^{\alpha} - (n+g+\delta+ng)\tilde{k})] / [(1+n)(1+g)]$). The bracket on the right must equal zero:

$$s (\tilde{k}^*)^{\alpha} = (n + g + \delta + ng) \tilde{k}^* \;\Leftrightarrow\; (\tilde{k}^*)^{1-\alpha} = \frac{s}{n+g+\delta+ng}$$

Raise to the power $1/(1-\alpha)$:

$$\boxed{\tilde{k}^* = \left(\frac{s}{n+g+\delta+ng}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)}, \quad \tilde{y}^* = \left(\frac{s}{n+g+\delta+ng}\right)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}}$$

These are constant. The unscaled per-worker quantities aren't — they inherit the growth of $A_t$:

$$y_t^* = A_t \tilde{y}^*, \quad k_t^* = A_t \tilde{k}^*$$

Both grow at exactly rate $g$ (the rate of $A_t$).

4.5.1 Factor prices in steady state

From the factor-price FOCs of the new production function (computed using $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}(A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}$):

$$r_t = \alpha \tilde{k}_t^{\alpha - 1}, \quad w_t = (1-\alpha) A_t \tilde{k}_t^{\alpha} = (1-\alpha) A_t \tilde{y}_t$$

In steady state $\tilde{k}_t = \tilde{k}^*$, so $r_t = r^* = \alpha (\tilde{k}^*)^{\alpha-1}$. Using the steady-state relation $s(\tilde{k}^*)^{\alpha} = (n+g+\delta+ng)\tilde{k}^*$ to substitute for $(\tilde{k}^*)^{\alpha-1} = (n+g+\delta+ng)/s$:

$$r^* = \alpha \cdot \frac{n + g + \delta + ng}{s} \quad \text{(constant)}$$

And $w_t^* = (1-\alpha) A_t \tilde{y}^*$ — grows at rate $g$ along with $A_t$.

4.5.2 Balanced growth: everything fits

Check the balanced-growth definition from Module 1:

  • $k_t$, $y_t$, $c_t = (1-s)y_t$, $w_t$ all grow at the same constant rate (here, $g$). ✓
  • Interest rate $r^*$ is constant. ✓
  • Capital-output ratio $K_t/Y_t = k_t/y_t = \tilde{k}^*/\tilde{y}^*$ is constant. ✓

So the general Solow model passes the balanced-growth test that the basic Solow failed — for the simple reason that exogenous technical progress now feeds growth in $A_t$, which propagates to everything per-worker.

4.6 Golden rule: still $s^{**} = \alpha$

Steady-state consumption per effective worker is $\tilde{c}^* = (1-s) \tilde{y}^*$. Substitute:

$$\tilde{c}^*(s) = (1-s) \left(\frac{s}{n+g+\delta+ng}\right)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$$

Two opposing forces: a higher saving rate increases the capital-output denominator (boosting $\tilde{y}^*$), but also leaves a smaller fraction of output for consumption. There's an interior optimum. To find it, take the log derivative:

$$\frac{d \ln \tilde{c}^*}{ds} = -\frac{1}{1-s} + \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} \cdot \frac{1}{s}$$

Set equal to zero. Multiply by $s(1-s)$ to clear denominators:

$$-s + \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} (1-s) = 0 \;\Leftrightarrow\; \alpha(1-s) = (1-\alpha) s \;\Leftrightarrow\; \alpha = s$$

So $s^{**} = \alpha$. The golden rule is unchanged from the basic Solow model — exogenous technical progress doesn't shift it. Intuition: technical progress raises the level of every steady-state quantity uniformly through $A_t$, but doesn't change the trade-off between current consumption and capital accumulation.

5. Worked Examples with Realistic Numbers

Two numerical examples — one for each extension. Both come from Bolboaca's Exercise Session 2.

5.1 Open economy: autarky vs free capital mobility

Take $B = 1$, $\alpha = 1/3$, $s = 0.15$, $n = 0.03$, $\delta = 0$, world interest rate $\bar{r} = 0.03$. The country starts closed; in some period it opens.

5.1.1 Autarky

From the closed-economy Solow formulae (with $\delta = 0$):

$$k_c^* = \left(\frac{s}{n}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)} = \left(\frac{0.15}{0.03}\right)^{3/2} = 5^{1.5} \approx 11.18$$

$$y_c^* = B \cdot (k_c^*)^{\alpha} = 1 \cdot 11.18^{1/3} \approx 2.24$$

$$w_c^* = (1-\alpha) y_c^* = (2/3)(2.24) \approx 1.49$$

$$c_c^* = (1-s) y_c^* = 0.85 \cdot 2.24 \approx 1.90$$

The autarky-equivalent interest rate is $r_c^* = \alpha n / s = (1/3)(0.03)/(0.15) = 0.067$ — about 6.7%, well above the world rate of 3%. So this country is savings-weak by world standards. When it opens, the cheap capital from the world flows in: domestic capital intensity should rise, the wage should rise, and the country should become a debtor.

5.1.2 Open economy

Use the formulae from §3-4. Domestic capital intensity:

$$k^* = B^{1/(1-\alpha)} \left(\frac{\alpha}{\bar{r}}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)} = 1 \cdot \left(\frac{1/3}{0.03}\right)^{1.5} = (11.11)^{1.5} \approx 37.04$$

GDP per worker:

$$y^* = (k^*)^{\alpha} = 37.04^{1/3} \approx 3.33$$

Wage:

$$w^* = (1-\alpha) y^* = (2/3)(3.33) \approx 2.22$$

Wealth per worker:

$$v^* = \frac{s/n}{1 - s\bar{r}/n} w^* = \frac{0.15/0.03}{1 - (0.15)(0.03)/(0.03)} \cdot 2.22 = \frac{5}{1 - 0.15} \cdot 2.22 \approx 13.07$$

Net foreign assets:

$$f^* = v^* - k^* = 13.07 - 37.04 = -23.97$$

(So the country is a debtor, as predicted.) National income:

$$y^{n*} = w^* + \bar{r} v^* = 2.22 + 0.03 \cdot 13.07 = 2.22 + 0.39 \approx 2.61$$

Consumption:

$$c^* = (1-s) y^{n*} = 0.85 \cdot 2.61 \approx 2.22$$

5.2 General Solow: a permanent saving-rate increase

Now switch to the general Solow setting. Base parameters from textbook Ch5 Exercise 4: $\alpha = 1/3$, $s = 0.12$, $n = 0.01$, $g = 0.01$, $\delta = 0.05$, $A_0 = 1$. The economy starts in steady state.

Compute steady-state effective capital. The break-even rate is $n + g + \delta + ng = 0.01 + 0.01 + 0.05 + 0.0001 = 0.0701$ (the cross-term $ng$ is just $0.0001$, illustrating Cozzi's point about it being negligible). Then:

$$\tilde{k}^* = \left(\frac{0.12}{0.0701}\right)^{1.5} \approx (1.712)^{1.5} \approx 2.241$$

$$\tilde{y}^* = 2.241^{1/3} \approx 1.309$$

At $t = 10$ the saving rate doubles permanently from $s = 0.12$ to $s' = 0.24$. The new steady state has:

$$\tilde{k}^{**} = \left(\frac{0.24}{0.0701}\right)^{1.5} \approx (3.424)^{1.5} \approx 6.337$$

$$\tilde{y}^{**} = 6.337^{1/3} \approx 1.851$$

So $\tilde{k}$ has to climb from 2.24 to 6.34 — roughly triple — and $\tilde{y}$ goes from 1.31 to 1.85 (a 41% increase per effective worker, on top of the underlying growth at rate $g$ that $A_t$ keeps adding).

What does this look like in terms of growth rates? In any period $t$, the unscaled growth rate of capital per worker is $g_t^k = g_t^{\tilde{k}} + g$ (since $k_t = A_t \tilde{k}_t$). When the saving rate jumps, $g_t^{\tilde{k}}$ jumps positive, and so $g_t^k$ jumps above $g$. As $\tilde{k}_t$ approaches $\tilde{k}^{**}$, $g_t^{\tilde{k}}$ decays back to zero and $g_t^k$ decays back to $g$. The growth rate of GDP per worker follows the same shape, since $g_t^y = \alpha g_t^k + (1-\alpha) g$ along the path.

How long does this take? The rate of convergence derived in §6 is $\lambda \approx (1-\alpha)(n+g+\delta) = (2/3)(0.07) \approx 0.047$ — about 4.7% per year. The implied half-life (the time to close half the remaining gap) is:

$$h = -\frac{\ln 2}{\ln(1-\lambda)} \approx \frac{\ln 2}{0.047} \approx 14.8 \text{ years}$$

So about 15 years to close half of the gap, 30 years to close three-quarters, 45 years to close 7/8. That's quite slow on a human timescale: anyone aged 30 when the policy changed will be near retirement before half of the long-run benefit materializes.

This is one reason the model has a normative bite distinct from its descriptive content: even if the structural change is unambiguously good (higher long-run consumption per worker), it might not be worth doing if the discount on future consumption is high, or if the people alive today are unwilling to subsidize the people not yet born. Bolboaca's slides make this point explicitly: 'Are you willing to endure the short-run pain for the long-run gain? It depends on how impatient you are.' Without an explicit welfare criterion specifying intertemporal preferences, the answer to 'should we raise the saving rate?' is not 'yes' — it's 'it depends on what you value.'

6. Connections, Empirics, and Common Traps

How the two extensions stand up against data, what the textbook flags as risks, and the common mistakes a Cozzi exam answer should avoid.

6.1 Empirics 1: Feldstein-Horioka and the rise of capital mobility

How would you measure how open the world economy actually is? Feldstein and Horioka (1980) had a clever idea. In a closed economy, $S_t / Y_t = I_t / Y_t$ exactly, so a cross-country regression of investment-rate on saving-rate should give slope 1. In a fully open world where capital flows freely, saving in country $i$ has no necessary connection to investment in country $i$ — saving can flow anywhere — so the slope should be 0.

The original Feldstein-Horioka result, using 1960–74 data for 16 OECD countries, gave slope $\alpha_1 \approx 0.89$ — surprisingly close to autarky. They called it 'the Feldstein-Horioka puzzle': why was saving still so domestically constrained? Cozzi re-estimates for 1990–2004 and finds $\alpha_1 < 0.5$ — and for the Euro area, around 0.14. So the puzzle has dissolved: by 2004 capital was much more mobile than in 1974. Financial integration roughly tripled in 30 years.

This isn't a test of the open-economy Solow model per se. It's a test of the premise (perfect capital mobility) — and the premise looks increasingly correct as time has gone on.

6.2 Empirics 2: the general Solow's steady-state prediction

The general Solow predicts (taking logs of the steady-state equation for $\tilde{y}^*$ from §4.5):

$$\ln \tilde{y}^* = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} \bigl(\ln s - \ln(n + g + \delta + ng)\bigr)$$

For $\alpha \approx 1/3$, the implied elasticity $\alpha/(1-\alpha)$ is about 0.5. A cross-country regression of log GDP per worker on this composite saving/population term should give slope 0.5. Cozzi runs it across 65 countries (the same sample as Module 1's convergence tests), assuming all countries share a common $A_0$ and $g$. The estimated slope: 1.52, with a tight standard error. Direction right (positive, as Solow predicts) but magnitude off by a factor of 3.

To rationalize a slope of 1.52, you would need $\alpha \approx 0.6$ — way above any reasonable estimate of capital's share. Something is acting like capital in the data — Module 3's human-capital extension will identify that something explicitly: education, which behaves mathematically like a third produced factor and raises the effective capital share from $\alpha \approx 1/3$ to $\alpha + \phi \approx 2/3$.

6.3 Empirics 3: the convergence regression

The third Solow prediction the data lets us test is the speed of convergence. To derive a regression specification, linearize the transition equation around steady state. The transition equation is $\tilde{k}_{t+1} = G(\tilde{k}_t)$ where $G$ is the right-hand side from §3.2. Taylor-expand around $\tilde{k}^*$:

$$\tilde{k}_{t+1} - \tilde{k}^* \approx G'(\tilde{k}^*) \cdot (\tilde{k}_t - \tilde{k}^*)$$

Compute $G'(\tilde{k}^*)$ by differentiating $G$:

$$G'(\tilde{k}^*) = \frac{\alpha s (\tilde{k}^*)^{\alpha - 1} + (1 - \delta)}{(1+n)(1+g)}$$

Use the steady-state condition $s(\tilde{k}^*)^{\alpha - 1} = (n+g+\delta+ng)/\tilde{k}^* \cdot \tilde{k}^*/\tilde{k}^* = (n+g+\delta+ng)$ to substitute:

$$G'(\tilde{k}^*) = \frac{\alpha (n+g+\delta+ng) + (1-\delta)}{(1+n)(1+g)}$$

After simplification (using the approximation that $n, g, \delta$ are small compared to 1):

$$\lambda \equiv 1 - G'(\tilde{k}^*) \approx (1-\alpha)(n + g + \delta)$$

Rate of convergence: $\lambda \approx (1-\alpha)(n+g+\delta)$.

This is the fraction of the gap $(\tilde{k}^* - \tilde{k}_t)$ that gets closed each period. For $\alpha = 1/3, n+g+\delta = 0.075$: $\lambda \approx 5\%$ per year. Half-life $\approx 14$ years.

Solve the linear difference equation in $\tilde{k}$ (use the homogeneous solution $C(1-\lambda)^t$ plus the steady-state particular solution $\tilde{k}^*$) and rearrange the result for $\ln y_T - \ln y_0$ over $T$ periods. After some algebra the average-growth-rate form is:

$$\frac{\ln y_T^i - \ln y_0^i}{T} \approx g + \beta_s \bigl[\ln s^i - \ln(n^i + g + \delta)\bigr] - \beta_0 \ln y_0^i + \varepsilon^i$$

where $\beta_0 = (1 - (1-\lambda)^T)/T$ is one-to-one related to $\lambda$. Estimate by OLS. Cozzi's result on 65 countries, 1960–2003:

$$g^i_{60,03} = 0.063 - 0.006 \ln y^i_{60} + 0.016 \bigl[\ln s^i - \ln(n^i + 0.075)\bigr]$$

From the estimated $\beta_0 = 0.006$ we recover $\hat{\lambda} \approx 0.007$ — about 0.7% per year, vs the 5% the theory predicts. So real-world convergence is seven times slower than the basic general Solow says it should be.

6.4 Growth accounting and the Solow residual

Even without testing the model's predictions, you can use its production function as a decomposition tool. Take logs and time-differences of $Y_t = B_t K_t^{\alpha} L_t^{1-\alpha}$:

$$g_t^Y = g_t^B + \alpha g_t^K + (1-\alpha) g_t^L$$

where $g_t^X = \ln X_{t+1} - \ln X_t$ is the approximate growth rate. Rearrange:

$$g_t^B = g_t^Y - \alpha g_t^K - (1-\alpha) g_t^L$$

Solow residual: TFP growth $g_t^B$ backed out as the part of output growth not explained by measured capital and labour growth. Solow himself called it 'a measure of our ignorance.'

Per-worker version: $g_t^y = g_t^B + \alpha g_t^k$. If you have data on $y$ and $k$ for some country with $\alpha \approx 1/3$, you can compute $g_t^B$ residually. The textbook's Table 5.1 does this for several countries. US TFP growth was about 2.9% per year in the early 1960s, fell to 0.3–1.0% in the 1970s–80s (the famous 'productivity slowdown'), then partially recovered in the 1990s. Sweden, Denmark, the UK all showed similar profiles. This is the empirical bread-and-butter use of the model — not for forecasting income levels but for diagnosing where growth is coming from.

A subtle point: growth accounting attributes growth to its sources but does not explain them. If the world were exactly described by the general Solow model, all per-worker growth would 'come from' technological progress in a causal sense — but growth accounting would still split the per-worker growth into a TFP component and a capital component (because the rising capital intensity itself is induced by the rising $A_t$). Both observations are true. The textbook's Exercise 9 forces this distinction.

6.5 Risks of opening: interest rate shocks and country risk

The welfare theorem from §4.3 says opening raises steady-state national income. But the steady state is a long-run object. In the short run, there are real risks the model glosses over.

Interest rate shock. Suppose $\bar{r}$ rises permanently. Domestic capital flows out (everyone wants the higher world return), $k^*$ drops, wages fall, GDP drops. The effect on national income depends on the country's existing net foreign asset position: a creditor country gains (collects more on its foreign assets); a debtor country loses (pays more on its foreign debt). For a debtor, an interest rate shock can be devastating — and persistent.

Country risk. International investors don't actually treat all countries' bonds as perfect substitutes. They demand a risk premium $\varepsilon$ on top of $\bar{r}$ for investing in, say, a politically uncertain economy. If $\varepsilon$ rises (because the country looks riskier), capital flees, the effective rate the country pays rises, wages fall. The South-East Asian crisis of 1997–98 and Mexico's experience post-NAFTA both fit this pattern: NAFTA was supposed to send capital from the US to Mexico (boosting Mexican wages), but in practice Mexican capital fled to the US — large country-risk premia on Mexico overwhelmed the autarky-rate gap.

The exam-relevant point: 'capital mobility improves welfare' is a long-run theorem. The short-run reality involves crisis risk, and that's why some countries still maintain capital controls.

6.6 Three common traps in exam answers

  1. Confusing GDP and GNP. GDP is what's produced inside the borders; GNP adds interest on net foreign assets. The welfare theorem is about GNP per worker. Claiming the open economy 'always has higher GDP per worker' would be flat-out wrong: a savings-strong country opening up sees GDP fall (because $k^*$ falls when capital flows out to where it earns more), even as GNP rises.
  2. Forgetting the difference between $k$ and $\tilde{k}$. In the general Solow, $k_t$ (capital per worker) grows forever at rate $g$ in steady state; $\tilde{k}_t$ (capital per effective worker) is constant. The transition equation lives in $\tilde{k}$; the balanced growth path lives in $k$. Mix them up and you'll predict zero growth in steady state — the very thing the model was designed to escape.
  3. Citing 'wages always rise from opening.' They don't. In a creditor (savings-strong) country, opening lowers wages even as national income rises in aggregate. This is exactly what makes capital-account liberalization politically controversial — and is the kind of nuance Cozzi rewards on essay questions.

7. Summary and Formula Reference

One-paragraph version

Module 2 extends the basic Solow model in two independent directions. Open capital account (Lecture 2a, textbook ch4): drop $S = I$, let $\bar{r}$ be exogenous, and the country's capital intensity instantly equilibrates with the world rate while wealth accumulates gradually. The headline result is a welfare theorem ($y^{n*} \geq y_c^*$ always) plus a distributional warning (savings-strong/creditor countries' wages fall on opening). Technical progress (Lecture 2b, textbook ch5): replace constant $B$ with $A_t$ growing at rate $g$. Per-effective-worker analysis recovers the basic Solow geometry; in unscaled terms, $k_t, y_t, c_t, w_t$ all grow at rate $g$ in steady state — balanced growth, matching stylized fact #5. The model passes its main qualitative test (per-worker growth, conditional convergence) but its quantitative predictions are off: it under-predicts the elasticity of income with respect to saving by a factor of 3, and over-predicts the convergence rate by a factor of 7. Module 3 will fix both.

Things to be sure you can do without notes

  • State and apply the open-economy welfare theorem: $y^{n*} \geq y_c^*$ always, equality only at $\bar{r} = r_c^*$. Identify the U-shape of $x(\tilde{r})$ and where the minimum lies.
  • Identify creditor vs debtor outcome from the sign of $f^*$ and from the cutoff $\bar{r} \gtrless r_c^*$, including the equivalent formulation in terms of $s/n$ vs world average.
  • State the distributional consequence: wages fall in a savings-strong country on opening, rise in a savings-weak country. Explain via $k^*$ moving up or down.
  • Derive the per-effective-worker transition equation $\tilde{k}_{t+1} = [(s\tilde{k}^{\alpha} + (1-\delta)\tilde{k})] / [(1+n)(1+g)]$ from the standard capital accumulation equation by dividing through by $A_{t+1} L_{t+1}$.
  • Solve for $\tilde{k}^*$ and the steady-state growth paths of $k_t, y_t, w_t$.
  • State the convergence rate $\lambda \approx (1-\alpha)(n+g+\delta)$ and identify its quantitative shortfall vs data ($\sim 5\%$ predicted, $\sim 0.7\%$ estimated).
  • Compute a Solow residual $g_t^B = g_t^Y - \alpha g_t^K - (1-\alpha) g_t^L$ given data on $Y, K, L$ and a value for $\alpha$.

What's coming next

Module 3 takes the general Solow model and asks 'why are the empirical fits so off?' The MRW (1992) answer: add human capital. Treating education as a third produced factor raises the effective capital share from $\alpha$ to $\alpha + \phi$, which simultaneously fixes the elasticity puzzle (steady-state income becomes much more responsive to investment) and the convergence-rate puzzle (slower convergence). Module 3 also introduces the natural-resources extension (Lecture 3b / Ch 7): land enters as a non-produced factor whose fixed supply puts a drag on per-worker growth, addressing the question 'does growth have limits?' on the climate/sustainability frame. Both extensions still treat technological progress as exogenous — the endogenization comes in Modules 4 and 5.
