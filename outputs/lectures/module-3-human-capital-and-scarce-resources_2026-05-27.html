1. The Big Picture

Picture yourself looking at the basic Solow model — the workhorse from earlier in the course. It worked surprisingly well in many respects, but two specific empirical puzzles refused to go away. Module 3 exists to fix them. So let's start by stating those puzzles concretely, because the rest of the lecture is shaped around solving them.

The first puzzle is about how fast economies converge. The basic Solow model's predicted rate of convergence — the rate at which a country closes the gap between its current income and its long-run path — is approximately $\lambda \approx (1-\alpha)(n+g+\delta)$, where $\alpha$ is the capital share. Plug in realistic numbers — $\alpha\approx 1/3$, $n+g+\delta\approx 0.075$ — and the model predicts $\lambda \approx (2/3)(0.075) \approx 0.05$. A country half-way below its long-run income would close half the remaining gap in about $\ln(2)/0.05\approx 14$ years. The data tell a different story: when economists actually run convergence regressions on cross-country data, they find $\lambda$ closer to 1–2% per year. Half-life is 35–70 years, not 14. The model is off by a factor of three or more.

The second puzzle is about how strongly the saving rate and population growth rate affect long-run income per worker. The basic Solow steady-state formula gives

$$y^* \propto \left(\tfrac{s}{n+g+\delta}\right)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)},$$

so the elasticity of $y^*$ with respect to $s/(n+g+\delta)$ is $\alpha/(1-\alpha)$. At $\alpha\approx 1/3$ this is about $0.5$. When economists actually estimate this elasticity from cross-country regressions, they get a slope closer to $1.6$. To make the basic Solow model match the data you would need $\alpha\approx 0.62$ — far too large for physical capital, which can claim at most about a third of GDP because that is roughly the non-labour share we actually observe in national accounts.

Notice that both puzzles point in the same direction: they BOTH say that 'capital' — whatever capital means — plays a larger role than the basic Solow model gives it credit for. Convergence is slower than predicted (suggesting MORE capital that needs to be accumulated before reaching the steady state), and saving has a stronger long-run effect (suggesting MORE capital that gets built up by saving). What if there's another kind of accumulated capital we've been ignoring?

That is the Mankiw–Romer–Weil insight (1992, Quarterly Journal of Economics). Add human capital — the accumulated stock of education, training, on-the-job skills, even health — to the production function alongside physical capital. Human capital, in their formulation, accumulates each period as a constant fraction $s_H$ of GDP, just like physical capital does at rate $s_K$; it depreciates at the same rate $\delta$; and it has its own Cobb-Douglas exponent $\phi$ in the production function. So adding $H$ adds 'more capital to build up' (slows convergence — fix #1) AND amplifies the chain $s \to y \to$ more $h$ investment $\to$ more $y$ still (boosts the $s$-effect — fix #2). One change, two fixes. Sections 3 through 5 will make the algebra precise and verify that the empirical fit improves dramatically.

But Module 3 has a second half, and it goes in nearly the opposite direction. Long before Solow, three classical economists — Adam Smith, David Ricardo, Thomas Malthus — looked at essentially the same question we are asking ('can income per capita grow forever?') and reached a much grimmer answer. Their argument: land is in fixed supply, so as population grows and capital piles up, more and more workers and machines are pressed onto the same stock of land. Diminishing returns to capital-plus-labour set in, income per worker falls, eventually real wages reach a subsistence floor, mortality rises until population stops growing, and the economy settles into a grim stagnation. Malthus's phrase for this end-state was 'misery and vice'. That's where the label 'dismal science' for economics comes from.

So which view is correct — the optimistic story of sustained per-capita growth, or the classical pessimism? Empirically, Western economies have averaged something like 2% income growth per capita for two centuries, which seems to vindicate the optimists. But the classics weren't being silly: their logic was sound. They just couldn't foresee two factors — sustained technological progress, and modern fertility control — that have so far counterbalanced the diminishing returns to land. Lecture 3b makes the trade-off explicit: it adds LAND and OIL to a Solow framework and asks quantitatively, under what conditions does technology beat scarcity? The conclusions are nuanced: yes for rich countries today; not for developing countries with high $n$; and the whole result hinges on a substitutability assumption we'll have to look in the eye in §6.

One module, two extensions of Solow that go in opposite directions. The MRW extension makes MORE things accumulate ($h$ on top of $k$) to plug empirical gaps. The land/oil extension makes some things STOP accumulating ($X$ fixed, $R$ depleting) to test classical pessimism. We handle both in turn, then close (§6) with what is STILL missing: the rate of technological progress $g$ is still exogenous in everything we do here. That gap is the agenda of Module 4.

Before we manipulate anything, let's pin down the objects. The notation table below is canonical for this entire lecture — every symbol used in sections 3 through 7 is listed here.

Two conventions to flag up-front. Stars always mean steady state — they never mean anything else. Tildes always mean "per effective worker", i.e. divided by $A_t L_t$. We use $A_t$ for labour-augmenting technology throughout (never the Hicks-neutral $B$ used elsewhere). The depreciation rate $\delta$ is the same for $K$ and $H$ — this is a simplifying assumption that keeps the algebra tractable; relaxing it just clutters formulas without changing qualitative conclusions.

Now the new objects. Start with human capital. Think of $H_t$ as the accumulated stock of everything that makes labour more productive: years of schooling, on-the-job training, learning by doing, health investments. It is NOT the same thing as raw labour. A worker with a degree and an unschooled worker both supply one person-year of raw labour, but very different amounts of human capital.

A structural fact about human capital that will matter for the algebra: human capital is inseparable from workers. You can detach a worker from her laptop (physical capital) and sell the laptop on its own. You cannot detach her from her education and sell that separately. So there is no separate market for human-capital services — it is bundled with labour in the labour market. When a firm hires one additional worker, it gets both raw labour AND that worker's human capital, in a fixed combination. The wage compensates both components in one number.

That structural fact has a precise consequence for marginal-product calculations: when we differentiate $Y$ with respect to $L$ to find the wage, we must hold human capital per worker $h_t = H_t/L_t$ fixed (because each additional worker comes embodying the same average $h_t$). Holding TOTAL $H_t$ fixed would be wrong — you can't hire an additional worker and leave $H_t$ unchanged. We'll use this explicitly in §3.

Now we can write down the production functions. The MRW production function is

$$Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}\, H_t^{\phi}\, (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha-\phi}.$$

Reading the symbols: $K_t$ is the aggregate physical capital stock; $H_t = h_t L_t$ is total human capital; $L_t$ is the labour force; $A_t$ is labour-augmenting technology; $\alpha,\phi\in (0,1)$ are the exponents on $K$ and $H$; the remaining $1-\alpha-\phi$ exponent falls on effective labour $A_t L_t$. We assume $\alpha+\phi < 1$ — this guarantees DIMINISHING RETURNS jointly to the two accumulated factors.

Why does $\alpha+\phi<1$ matter so much? There are constant returns to all THREE inputs $(K, H, AL)$ — that is the replication argument: doubling capital, human capital AND effective labour will double output, since $2^{\alpha+\phi+(1-\alpha-\phi)} = 2^1 = 2$. But if you doubled only $K$ and $H$ while $AL$ stayed fixed, output would scale by $2^{\alpha+\phi}$, which is LESS than 2 because $\alpha+\phi<1$. That diminishing return on $(K,H)$ jointly is what eventually pulls the economy to a steady state — you can't accumulate $K$ and $H$ without bound and keep multiplying $Y$ proportionally. If $\alpha+\phi=1$ were allowed, doubling $(K,H)$ would double $Y$ even without any $AL$ growth, and there would be no pull toward a steady state at all (that would push us into endogenous-growth territory — Module 4).

Empirically, $\alpha\approx 1/3$ — capital income is about one third of GDP. For $\phi$ the evidence is less direct: MRW argue that in US manufacturing the average wage is about twice the minimum wage, suggesting that of workers' roughly two-thirds income share, half is compensation for raw labour and half is the return to embedded human capital. That puts $\phi \approx 1/3$ as well. A useful baseline calibration throughout this lecture is $\alpha = \phi = 1/3$, with all three factors (K, H, AL) equally weighted.

Per-worker form. Divide both sides by $L_t$. With $H_t = h_t L_t$ and $K_t = k_t L_t$:

$$\frac{Y_t}{L_t} = \frac{K_t^{\alpha}\,(h_t L_t)^{\phi}\,(A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha-\phi}}{L_t}.$$

Let's track the $L_t$ powers carefully — this is the kind of step that's easy to gloss over but worth showing line by line. Write $K_t = k_t L_t$, so $K_t^{\alpha} = k_t^{\alpha}\,L_t^{\alpha}$. The numerator becomes

$$k_t^{\alpha}L_t^{\alpha}\;\cdot\;h_t^{\phi}L_t^{\phi}\;\cdot\;A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi}L_t^{1-\alpha-\phi} = k_t^{\alpha}\,h_t^{\phi}\,A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi}\;\cdot\;L_t^{\alpha+\phi+(1-\alpha-\phi)} = k_t^{\alpha}\,h_t^{\phi}\,A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi}\;\cdot\;L_t.$$

The $L_t$ in the numerator cancels the $1/L_t$ outside, and we are left with

Per-worker MRW production function: $$y_t = k_t^{\alpha}\,h_t^{\phi}\,A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi}.$$ Output per worker is jointly determined by physical capital per worker, human capital per worker, and the level of labour-augmenting technology. The exponents weight their contributions, and they sum to 1 once $A_t$ is included.

Now the land model. Three classical inputs, all Cobb-Douglas:

$$Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}\,(A_t L_t)^{\beta}\,X^{\kappa},\qquad \alpha+\beta+\kappa = 1.$$

The new symbol $X$ is the FIXED stock of land — no time subscript, because land doesn't grow or accumulate. The constraint $\alpha+\beta+\kappa=1$ enforces constant returns to scale jointly across the three inputs (replication argument once more: doubling capital, effective labour AND land doubles output). With CRS to all three, the implication is DIMINISHING returns to capital and labour alone, which is exactly what we want to capture: the fixed stock of land creates a bottleneck. The classics knew this intuitively two centuries before Cobb-Douglas existed.

The income share of land's owners — call them landlords — is $\kappa$, which we will leave between roughly $0.1$ and $0.2$ for plausibility. (In Nordhaus's calibration of the combined Land+Oil model, $\kappa = \varepsilon = 0.1$.)

And finally the land + oil model:

$$Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}\,(A_t L_t)^{\beta}\,X^{\kappa}\,E_t^{\varepsilon},\qquad \alpha+\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon = 1.$$

Here $E_t$ is the FLOW of oil used in production in period $t$. The new state variable is $R_t$, the remaining stock of oil at the start of period $t$. The resource-depletion equation is mass balance:

$$R_{t+1} = R_t - E_t.$$

How much of the stock gets extracted each period? A proper micro-foundation would have oil-suppliers solving an intertemporal optimisation (sell now vs. save for later at higher future prices). To avoid that complexity, we postulate a constant extraction rate $s_E$:

$$E_t = s_E\,R_t\quad\Longrightarrow\quad R_{t+1} = (1-s_E)\,R_t\quad\Longrightarrow\quad R_t = (1-s_E)^t R_0.$$

The stock declines geometrically — like depreciation, but applied to a non-renewable resource. Nordhaus suggests $s_E \approx 0.005$ (half a percent of the remaining stock used per year), which is well below the typical $\delta \approx 0.05$ depreciation rate of physical capital. We will assume $s_E < \delta$ throughout; this guarantees that the resource is being used "slowly enough" relative to capital depreciation that the dynamic system has a sensible balanced-growth path.

That's four production functions. Figure 1 above places them side by side. The remainder of the lecture works with these objects: §3 derives the mechanics for MRW and for the land model, §4 works out their steady-state implications and growth drags, §5 puts in numbers, and §6 confronts the models with data and with the assumptions they hide.

3. How Things Move: The Mechanics

Now we have the objects. The next job is to see how they MOVE — how $K$ and $H$ accumulate, how factor prices get determined, and how the whole system rolls forward in time. We work the MRW model first (subsections 3.1 to 3.5), then switch to the Solow-with-land model (3.6 to 3.7).

3.1 Factor prices in MRW

The firm rents capital, hires labour, and produces output. Profit maximisation in perfect competition gives the standard condition: pay each factor its marginal product. Two prices to find — the rental rate of capital $r_t$ and the wage $w_t$ — by differentiating the production function appropriately.

Start from the aggregate production function we wrote in §2:

$$Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}\,H_t^{\phi}\,(A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha-\phi} = K_t^{\alpha}\,h_t^{\phi}\,A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi}\,L_t^{1-\alpha}.$$

(In the second form I used $H_t = h_t L_t$ so that $H_t^{\phi} = h_t^{\phi} L_t^{\phi}$, and then collected the $L_t$ exponents: $\phi + (1-\alpha-\phi) = 1-\alpha$. We need this form for the wage derivation, where the "held fixed" quantity is going to be $h_t$ not $H_t$.)

The rental rate is the marginal product of capital, computed at given $L_t$, $A_t$, and total $H_t$ (or equivalently given $L_t$, $A_t$, and $h_t$ — same thing):

$$r_t = \frac{\partial Y_t}{\partial K_t} = \alpha\,K_t^{\alpha-1}\,h_t^{\phi}\,A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi}\,L_t^{1-\alpha}.$$

That's the answer in aggregate variables. Pull it into per-effective-worker form by dividing the inside terms by $A_t L_t$ where they appear. Write $K_t = (K_t/(A_t L_t))\cdot A_t L_t$. After regrouping (this is a useful arithmetic exercise — take a moment to verify it on paper if you want):

$$r_t = \alpha\,\left(\tfrac{K_t}{A_t L_t}\right)^{\alpha-1}\,\left(\tfrac{H_t}{A_t L_t}\right)^{\phi}.$$

So $r_t$ depends only on the two per-effective-worker capital intensities. Notice in particular that $r_t$ does not depend on $A_t$ alone — only on the RATIOS $K_t/(A_t L_t)$ and $H_t/(A_t L_t)$. This is the same diminishing-returns logic as in the basic Solow model: capital and labour interact symmetrically through their ratios.

The wage is more delicate. We are computing the marginal product of LABOUR, which means we hold $K_t$, $A_t$, and $h_t$ (NOT $H_t$) fixed and differentiate with respect to $L_t$. Why $h_t$ rather than $H_t$? Because of the inseparability fact from §2: when one extra worker is hired, she brings the same average human capital $h_t$ as everyone else. The firm cannot hire a worker without her embedded education. Holding $H_t$ fixed would mean "hire one more worker but reduce everyone else's education to compensate" — that's not how labour markets actually work.

So we use the form $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}\,h_t^{\phi}\,A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi}\,L_t^{1-\alpha}$ and differentiate:

$$w_t = \frac{\partial Y_t}{\partial L_t}\bigg|_{K_t,A_t,h_t} = (1-\alpha)\,K_t^{\alpha}\,h_t^{\phi}\,A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi}\,L_t^{-\alpha}.$$

Rearranging in per-effective-worker form:

$$w_t = (1-\alpha)\,\left(\tfrac{K_t}{A_t L_t}\right)^{\alpha}\,\left(\tfrac{H_t}{A_t L_t}\right)^{\phi}\,A_t.$$

Notice what's striking about this expression: the exponent on $L_t$-related quantities is $1-\alpha$, NOT $1-\alpha-\phi$. That extra $\phi$ has been absorbed into the wage. Why? Because $h_t$ was held fixed, the wage paid per worker compensates BOTH for raw labour (the $1-\alpha-\phi$ part) AND for the human capital that comes attached to the worker (the $\phi$ part). Total labour income $w_t L_t$ therefore captures both the raw-labour share and the human-capital share.

3.2 Income shares

Now we can compute income shares directly. Starting from the rental rate $r_t = \alpha K_t^{\alpha-1} h_t^{\phi} A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi} L_t^{1-\alpha}$:

$$r_t K_t = \alpha\,K_t^{\alpha}\,h_t^{\phi}\,A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi}\,L_t^{1-\alpha} = \alpha\,Y_t.$$

So $r_t K_t / Y_t = \alpha$. The owners of physical capital take the share $\alpha$ of total output — same result as in basic Solow.

Similarly $w_t L_t = (1-\alpha) K_t^{\alpha} h_t^{\phi} A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi} L_t^{1-\alpha} = (1-\alpha)\,Y_t$, so $w_t L_t / Y_t = 1-\alpha$. The WORKERS take the share $1-\alpha$.

Income shares in MRW: $$\frac{r_t K_t}{Y_t} = \alpha,\qquad \frac{w_t L_t}{Y_t} = 1-\alpha.$$ The capital-owners' share is unchanged from basic Solow ($\alpha$). The workers' share is also $1-\alpha$ — but it now includes both the compensation for raw labour AND the return to embedded human capital. There is NO separate market price for $h$; the return to human capital is paid through the wage.

That last point is conceptually important and easy to miss. You might have expected $w_t L_t / Y_t = 1-\alpha-\phi$ — "workers take only the raw-labour share". But because human capital is bundled with labour, the $\phi$ share of human-capital income also flows through the wage to workers. The labour share you observe in national accounts is the SUM of these two flows.

3.3 Capital accumulation and the per-effective-worker reduction

Now to dynamics. The two accumulation equations are direct generalisations of the basic Solow setup:

$$K_{t+1} = s_K\,Y_t + (1-\delta)\,K_t,\qquad H_{t+1} = s_H\,Y_t + (1-\delta)\,H_t.$$

Reading: tomorrow's physical capital equals today's $K$, less depreciation, plus the fraction $s_K$ of today's output invested in $K$. Same logic for human capital with rate $s_H$. The two rates satisfy $s_K + s_H < 1$ so that consumption $c_t = (1-s_K-s_H)y_t$ is positive. Both stocks depreciate at the same rate $\delta$ — a simplifying assumption.

Following the standard Solow technique we move to per-effective-worker variables $\tilde k_t = K_t/(A_t L_t)$ and $\tilde h_t = H_t/(A_t L_t)$. Why this transformation? Because in steady state these per-effective-worker quantities will be CONSTANT — that's the whole point of dividing by $A_t L_t$, which is the "denominator that grows at the trend rate $g+n$".

Divide the first accumulation equation by $A_{t+1} L_{t+1} = (1+g)(1+n) A_t L_t$:

$$\frac{K_{t+1}}{A_{t+1} L_{t+1}} = \frac{s_K Y_t + (1-\delta) K_t}{(1+n)(1+g) A_t L_t}.$$

The left-hand side is $\tilde k_{t+1}$ by definition. On the right, divide each term inside by $A_t L_t$:

$$\tilde k_{t+1} = \frac{s_K\,\tilde y_t + (1-\delta)\,\tilde k_t}{(1+n)(1+g)}.$$

Identical algebra for human capital:

$$\tilde h_{t+1} = \frac{s_H\,\tilde y_t + (1-\delta)\,\tilde h_t}{(1+n)(1+g)}.$$

What is $\tilde y_t$ in terms of $\tilde k_t$ and $\tilde h_t$? Divide the per-worker production $y_t = k_t^{\alpha} h_t^{\phi} A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi}$ by $A_t$:

$$\tilde y_t = \tilde k_t^{\alpha}\,\tilde h_t^{\phi}.$$

(Check the exponents: $k_t/A_t = (K_t/L_t)/A_t = \tilde k_t$, raised to $\alpha$; similarly $h_t/A_t = \tilde h_t$ to the $\phi$; and the $A_t^{1-\alpha-\phi}$ on top divided by $A_t^{(1)} = A_t$ leaves $A_t^{-\alpha-\phi}$ which cancels with the $A_t^{\alpha+\phi}$ inside the tildes.) Note that the technology level $A_t$ has vanished entirely — that's why the tilde-variables have a chance of being constant in steady state.

MRW transition equations (per effective worker): $$\tilde k_{t+1} = \frac{s_K\,\tilde k_t^{\alpha}\,\tilde h_t^{\phi} + (1-\delta)\,\tilde k_t}{(1+n)(1+g)},\qquad \tilde h_{t+1} = \frac{s_H\,\tilde k_t^{\alpha}\,\tilde h_t^{\phi} + (1-\delta)\,\tilde h_t}{(1+n)(1+g)}.$$ A pair of coupled first-order difference equations. Given $(\tilde k_0, \tilde h_0)$, this system determines the entire sequence.

What's new compared to basic Solow? The transition for $\tilde k$ now depends on $\tilde h$ (and vice versa) — they are coupled. In a single-variable system you could draw the standard Solow diagram and read off convergence by eye. Here there's no simple 1D diagram — instead we'll use a 2D phase diagram.

3.4 The Solow equations and the phase diagram

Subtract $\tilde k_t$ from both sides of the $\tilde k$ transition equation, multiplying through by $(1+n)(1+g)$:

$$(1+n)(1+g)\,\Delta \tilde k_t = s_K\,\tilde k_t^{\alpha}\,\tilde h_t^{\phi} - [(1+n)(1+g) - (1-\delta)]\,\tilde k_t.$$

And the bracketed term simplifies: $(1+n)(1+g) - (1-\delta) = 1 + n + g + ng - 1 + \delta = n + g + \delta + ng$. So:

$$(1+n)(1+g)\,\Delta\tilde k_t = s_K\,\tilde k_t^{\alpha}\,\tilde h_t^{\phi} - (n+g+\delta+ng)\,\tilde k_t.$$

Same for $\tilde h$:

$$(1+n)(1+g)\,\Delta\tilde h_t = s_H\,\tilde k_t^{\alpha}\,\tilde h_t^{\phi} - (n+g+\delta+ng)\,\tilde h_t.$$

These are the MRW Solow equations. Each says: the change in tilde-capital equals (gross investment from the savings rate) minus (the dilution by depreciation, population growth, and tech growth). When the two terms balance, that capital stock is in steady state.

Set $\Delta\tilde k_t = 0$ to find the LOCUS in $(\tilde k, \tilde h)$-space along which $\tilde k$ is constant:

$$s_K\,\tilde k^{\alpha}\,\tilde h^{\phi} = (n+g+\delta+ng)\,\tilde k.$$

Solving for $\tilde h$:

$$\tilde h = \left(\tfrac{n+g+\delta+ng}{s_K}\right)^{1/\phi}\,\tilde k^{(1-\alpha)/\phi}\qquad [\Delta\tilde k = 0].$$

Same for the $\Delta\tilde h = 0$ locus:

$$s_H\,\tilde k^{\alpha}\,\tilde h^{\phi} = (n+g+\delta+ng)\,\tilde h\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\tilde h = \left(\tfrac{s_H}{n+g+\delta+ng}\right)^{1/(1-\phi)}\,\tilde k^{\alpha/(1-\phi)}\qquad [\Delta\tilde h = 0].$$

Both curves pass through the origin and are increasing in $\tilde k$. They intersect at a unique positive steady state $E = (\tilde k^*, \tilde h^*)$. Above the $\Delta\tilde k=0$ curve, $s_K\tilde k^{\alpha}\tilde h^{\phi} > (n+g+\delta+ng)\tilde k$ (because $\tilde h$ is too large for the given $\tilde k$), so $\Delta\tilde k > 0$ — the economy moves to the right; below, it moves left. Similarly to the right of the $\Delta\tilde h=0$ curve, $\Delta\tilde h > 0$ — the economy moves up; to the left, it moves down. From any starting point the economy gets pulled toward $E$ (this is rigorous in the linearised system; in the nonlinear system it holds in numerical simulations for sensible parameters, but a full global stability proof is non-trivial).

3.5 Switching to the Solow-with-land model

The MRW analysis is done. Now we switch to a different model — the Solow-with-land model from Lecture 3b. This is a deliberate model-variant switch, so let me state the three things that change:

Switch from MRW to Solow-with-land.
(a) We now move from the MRW model (Lecture 3a, Chapter 6) to the Solow model with land (Lecture 3b, Chapter 7).
(b) What changes: we DROP human capital entirely ($\phi=0$, no $H_t$). We ADD a fixed factor land $X$ with exponent $\kappa$, so the production function is $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}(A_t L_t)^{\beta} X^{\kappa}$ with $\alpha+\beta+\kappa=1$. The key conceptual difference: instead of having MORE things accumulate (helping convergence), we have a factor that does NOT accumulate at all, creating a growth drag.
(c) New assumptions: $X$ is constant over time (this is the whole point); $\alpha+\beta+\kappa=1$ enforces constant returns to all three; we still assume the standard Solow savings behaviour $S_t = s Y_t$, with a single saving rate $s$ in physical capital.

For analysing this new model the cleanest state variable turns out to be the capital-output ratio $z_t = K_t/Y_t$. Why $z$ rather than per-effective-worker $\tilde k$ as in MRW? Because the natural balanced-growth condition for this model is that $K/Y$ becomes constant in steady state — the rate of capital growth equals the rate of output growth — and reducing the model to a single equation in $z$ makes the analysis tractable.

3.6 Deriving the transition equation for $z$ in Solow-with-land

From the per-worker production function (derived analogously to what we did in §2 for MRW, but now with $\beta$ instead of $1-\alpha-\phi$ as the labour exponent and $X^{\kappa}/L_t^{\kappa}$ pulled out):

$$y_t = k_t^{\alpha}\,A_t^{\beta}\,\left(\tfrac{X}{L_t}\right)^{\kappa}.$$

Let me confirm this expression by tracing the $L_t$ exponents one more time. From $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}(A_t L_t)^{\beta} X^{\kappa}$, divide by $L_t$:

$$\frac{Y_t}{L_t} = \frac{K_t^{\alpha} A_t^{\beta} L_t^{\beta} X^{\kappa}}{L_t} = K_t^{\alpha} A_t^{\beta} L_t^{\beta-1} X^{\kappa}.$$

Now substitute $K_t = k_t L_t$, so $K_t^{\alpha} = k_t^{\alpha} L_t^{\alpha}$, and use $\alpha+\beta-1 = -\kappa$ (because $\alpha+\beta+\kappa=1$):

$$y_t = k_t^{\alpha} L_t^{\alpha+\beta-1} A_t^{\beta} X^{\kappa} = k_t^{\alpha} L_t^{-\kappa} A_t^{\beta} X^{\kappa} = k_t^{\alpha} A_t^{\beta} (X/L_t)^{\kappa}.$$

That gives us a closed expression for $y_t$ in terms of capital per worker, technology, and land per worker. Now to the capital-output ratio. From $z_t = k_t/y_t$ and the expression above:

$$z_t = \frac{k_t}{k_t^{\alpha} A_t^{\beta} (X/L_t)^{\kappa}} = k_t^{1-\alpha}\,A_t^{-\beta}\,(X/L_t)^{-\kappa}.$$

Tomorrow's value $z_{t+1}$ comes from leading this expression one period and using the capital accumulation rule. From $k_{t+1} L_{t+1} = K_{t+1} = s Y_t + (1-\delta) K_t = s y_t L_t + (1-\delta) k_t L_t$, we get $k_{t+1}(1+n) = s y_t + (1-\delta) k_t$, hence $k_{t+1} = (s y_t + (1-\delta) k_t)/(1+n)$. Then

$$z_{t+1} = \frac{k_{t+1}}{y_{t+1}}.$$

After doing the substitutions and using the fact that $A_{t+1} = (1+g)A_t$, $L_{t+1} = (1+n)L_t$ (a small algebra exercise; the textbook does this in detail), one arrives at a clean expression of the form:

$$z_{t+1} = c_t\,z_t^{p} \;+\; (\text{additive term from depreciation}),$$

where the curvature exponent $p<1$ comes from the $1-\alpha$ power on $k$ combined with the per-capita technology and land terms, and $c_t$ is a slowly-evolving scale factor depending on $A_t$ and $L_t$. Rather than writing out the full unwieldy formula, the point is that the qualitative shape of $z_{t+1}(z_t)$ is what matters for stability — and that shape satisfies four properties that we can verify directly from the equation. They are:

  1. The transition curve passes through the origin. If $z_t=0$ (so $K_t=0$), then $K_{t+1} = sY_t = 0$ as well (no capital, no output, no investment), so $z_{t+1}=0$.
  2. It is everywhere strictly increasing. Larger $z_t$ today (more capital relative to output) translates monotonically into larger $z_{t+1}$ tomorrow, because the depreciation term $(1-\delta)z_t$ is monotone in $z_t$ and the gross-investment term grows with $z_t$ too.
  3. It has exactly one strictly positive intersection with the 45° line. Set $z_{t+1} = z_t = z^*$ and solve. The textbook does this carefully; the answer (using $\alpha+\beta+\kappa=1$) takes the form

    $$z^* = \left(\tfrac{s}{n+g+\delta+ng}\right)^{1/(\beta+\kappa)} \cdot (\text{terms involving }A,L,X),$$

    and is a single positive number. In the special case $\kappa = 0$ (no land) this reduces to $z^* = s/(n+g+\delta+ng)$ — exactly the basic Solow result.

  4. The slope at the origin is strictly greater than 1. Differentiating $z_{t+1}$ with respect to $z_t$ at $z_t=0$ gives $\infty$ (because the $z_t^p$ term with $p<1$ has infinite slope at zero). So just to the right of the origin the transition curve is above the 45° line — it pushes upward — and only later crosses down through it at $z^*$.

These four properties together guarantee the geometric picture in Figure 3: the transition curve starts above the 45° line near zero, rises (but at a decreasing rate, because $p<1$), crosses the 45° line ONCE at $z^*$, and from then on lies below the 45° line. Convergence to $z^*$ from any positive starting point follows by the standard staircase argument — pick any $z_0$, read $z_1$ off the transition curve, drop down to the 45° line to find your new horizontal coordinate, read $z_2$, and so on. The sequence creeps toward $z^*$ from either side.

4. Properties: Steady State, Golden Rule, Growth Drag

Now that we have the equations of motion, we can ask what they imply. Four big results in this section: the MRW steady state (and the elasticities that turn out to match the data); the Golden Rule for $s_K, s_H$; the MRW convergence rate; and the balanced-growth growth rate in the land and land+oil models.

4.1 The MRW steady state

A steady state of the MRW system is a pair $(\tilde k^*, \tilde h^*)$ at which both $\Delta\tilde k = 0$ and $\Delta\tilde h = 0$. From the Solow equations in §3.4, setting both deltas to zero gives:

$$s_K\,\tilde k^{*\alpha}\,\tilde h^{*\phi} = (n+g+\delta+ng)\,\tilde k^*,$$

$$s_H\,\tilde k^{*\alpha}\,\tilde h^{*\phi} = (n+g+\delta+ng)\,\tilde h^*.$$

Two equations, two unknowns. Rearrange the first by dividing both sides by $\tilde k^*$ and then by $\tilde k^{*\alpha-1}$ on the left:

$$\tilde k^{*1-\alpha} = \tfrac{s_K}{n+g+\delta+ng}\;\tilde h^{*\phi}.\quad (A)$$

The second equation, similarly:

$$\tilde h^{*1-\phi} = \tfrac{s_H}{n+g+\delta+ng}\;\tilde k^{*\alpha}.\quad (B)$$

From (B), $\tilde h^* = (s_H/(n+g+\delta+ng))^{1/(1-\phi)}\;\tilde k^{*\alpha/(1-\phi)}$. Substitute into (A):

$$\tilde k^{*1-\alpha} = \tfrac{s_K}{n+g+\delta+ng}\;\Big[\tfrac{s_H}{n+g+\delta+ng}\Big]^{\phi/(1-\phi)}\,\tilde k^{*\alpha\phi/(1-\phi)}.$$

Collect the $\tilde k^*$ powers. The exponent on $\tilde k^*$ on the left is $1-\alpha$, on the right is $\alpha\phi/(1-\phi)$, so moving everything to the left:

$$\tilde k^{*(1-\alpha)-\alpha\phi/(1-\phi)} = \tfrac{s_K}{n+g+\delta+ng}\;\Big[\tfrac{s_H}{n+g+\delta+ng}\Big]^{\phi/(1-\phi)}.$$

The exponent on $\tilde k^*$ simplifies:

$$(1-\alpha) - \tfrac{\alpha\phi}{1-\phi} = \tfrac{(1-\alpha)(1-\phi) - \alpha\phi}{1-\phi} = \tfrac{1 - \alpha - \phi + \alpha\phi - \alpha\phi}{1-\phi} = \tfrac{1-\alpha-\phi}{1-\phi}.$$

So $\tilde k^{*(1-\alpha-\phi)/(1-\phi)} = (s_K/(n+g+\delta+ng))\cdot(s_H/(n+g+\delta+ng))^{\phi/(1-\phi)}$. Raise both sides to the power $(1-\phi)/(1-\alpha-\phi)$ and collect terms:

MRW steady-state capital intensities (per effective worker): $$\tilde k^* = \left(\tfrac{s_K^{1-\phi}\,s_H^{\phi}}{n+g+\delta+ng}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha-\phi)},\quad \tilde h^* = \left(\tfrac{s_K^{\alpha}\,s_H^{1-\alpha}}{n+g+\delta+ng}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha-\phi)}.$$ Both depend on BOTH saving rates — that's because physical and human capital reinforce each other in production. Plugging into $\tilde y^* = \tilde k^{*\alpha}\,\tilde h^{*\phi}$ gives $$\tilde y^* = \left(\tfrac{s_K}{n+g+\delta+ng}\right)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha-\phi)}\,\left(\tfrac{s_H}{n+g+\delta+ng}\right)^{\phi/(1-\alpha-\phi)}.$$

That last formula is the punchline. Compare it to the basic Solow steady state $\tilde y^* = (s/(n+g+\delta))^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$. Two things have changed: the elasticity has gone from $\alpha/(1-\alpha)$ to $\alpha/(1-\alpha-\phi)$, and there is now a SECOND term involving $s_H$.

4.2 Elasticities and why they fit the data

Take logs of $\tilde y^*$:

$$\ln \tilde y^* = \tfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha-\phi}[\ln s_K - \ln(n+g+\delta+ng)] + \tfrac{\phi}{1-\alpha-\phi}[\ln s_H - \ln(n+g+\delta+ng)].$$

So the elasticities of $\tilde y^*$ are:

  • with respect to $s_K$: $\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha-\phi}$
  • with respect to $s_H$: $\frac{\phi}{1-\alpha-\phi}$
  • with respect to $n+g+\delta$ (combining both $\ln(\cdot)$ terms): $-\frac{\alpha+\phi}{1-\alpha-\phi}$

Plug in $\alpha = \phi = 1/3$. Each elasticity with respect to $s_K$ or $s_H$ becomes $(1/3)/(1/3) = 1$. The elasticity with respect to $n+g+\delta$ becomes $-(2/3)/(1/3) = -2$. Compare to basic Solow where the s-elasticity was $\alpha/(1-\alpha) \approx 0.5$ and the $n$-elasticity was about $-0.5$.

These are exactly the magnitudes that the cross-country data point to (we'll see the regression in §6). The MRW model didn't just fix the sign of the elasticities — it produced quantitative predictions about MAGNITUDE that the basic Solow model was unable to produce. That's what makes the empirical fit so satisfying.

4.3 The Golden Rule

What saving rates $s_K, s_H$ would MAXIMISE long-run consumption per worker? In steady state, consumption per worker is $c_t^* = (1 - s_K - s_H)\,y_t^*$, where $y_t^* = A_t\,\tilde y^*$. Since the only choice variables are $s_K$ and $s_H$, and $\ln$ is monotone, we maximise $\ln c_t^* = \ln(1 - s_K - s_H) + \ln A_t + \ln \tilde y^*$. The $\ln A_t$ piece is exogenous and irrelevant for the choice. Using the elasticity formula for $\ln \tilde y^*$ from §4.2:

$$\ln c_t^* = \ln(1 - s_K - s_H) + \tfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha-\phi}\ln s_K + \tfrac{\phi}{1-\alpha-\phi}\ln s_H + (\text{terms not involving }s_K, s_H).$$

Differentiate with respect to $s_K$ and set to zero:

$$-\tfrac{1}{1-s_K-s_H} + \tfrac{\alpha}{(1-\alpha-\phi)\,s_K} = 0\;\Longrightarrow\;\tfrac{\alpha}{(1-\alpha-\phi)\,s_K} = \tfrac{1}{1-s_K-s_H}\;\Longrightarrow\;\alpha(1-s_K-s_H) = (1-\alpha-\phi)\,s_K.\quad (I)$$

Same for $s_H$:

$$\phi(1-s_K-s_H) = (1-\alpha-\phi)\,s_H.\quad (II)$$

Divide (I) by $\alpha$ and (II) by $\phi$; both right-hand sides become $(1-\alpha-\phi)\cdot$(saving rate)/(corresponding exponent). Setting the left-hand sides equal (both equal $1-s_K-s_H$): $(1-\alpha-\phi)s_K/\alpha = (1-\alpha-\phi)s_H/\phi$, hence $s_K/\alpha = s_H/\phi$. Now solve (I) using this proportionality. From $s_H = (\phi/\alpha)s_K$:

$$\alpha(1 - s_K - (\phi/\alpha)s_K) = (1-\alpha-\phi)s_K\;\Longrightarrow\;\alpha - (\alpha + \phi)s_K = (1-\alpha-\phi)s_K\;\Longrightarrow\;\alpha = s_K.$$

And then $s_H = (\phi/\alpha)\cdot\alpha = \phi$.

MRW Golden Rule: $s_K^{**} = \alpha$ and $s_H^{**} = \phi$. The saving rates that maximise long-run consumption per worker equal the corresponding Cobb-Douglas exponents — the "invest exactly in proportion to factor importance" rule. At $\alpha=\phi=1/3$, the Golden Rule prescribes $s_K = s_H = 1/3$, with $1/3$ left over for consumption.

4.4 The MRW convergence rate

How fast does the economy approach steady state from a typical starting point? The formal derivation requires linearising the 2D Solow system around $(\tilde k^*, \tilde h^*)$, which is more algebra than is illuminating to reproduce in full. Instead I'll sketch the basic-Solow analog (which has the same structure) and then state the MRW result.

For BASIC Solow (no $H$), the transition equation is $\tilde k_{t+1} = (s\tilde k_t^{\alpha} + (1-\delta)\tilde k_t)/((1+n)(1+g))$. Call the right-hand side $\Phi(\tilde k)$. Linearise around $\tilde k^*$:

$$\tilde k_{t+1} - \tilde k^* \approx \Phi'(\tilde k^*)\cdot(\tilde k_t - \tilde k^*).$$

Computing $\Phi'(\tilde k)$ and evaluating at $\tilde k^*$ using the steady-state condition $s\tilde k^{*\alpha-1} = n+g+\delta+ng$:

$$\Phi'(\tilde k^*) = \tfrac{\alpha\,(n+g+\delta+ng) + (1-\delta)}{(1+n)(1+g)}.$$

Define $1-\lambda = \Phi'(\tilde k^*)$. After some algebra (using $(1+n)(1+g)-(1-\delta) = n+g+\delta+ng$), this simplifies to:

$$\lambda = \tfrac{(1-\alpha)(n+g+\delta+ng)}{(1+n)(1+g)} \approx (1-\alpha)(n+g+\delta)\quad\text{for small }n,g.$$

The MRW analog applies the same technique to the 2D system. Multiply the linearised $\tilde k$-equation by $\alpha$ and the linearised $\tilde h$-equation by $\phi$ and add them; the trick is that on the left-hand side $\alpha\Delta(\ln\tilde k) + \phi\Delta(\ln\tilde h)$ equals $\Delta(\ln\tilde y)$ (since $\tilde y = \tilde k^{\alpha}\tilde h^{\phi}$, so $\ln\tilde y = \alpha\ln\tilde k + \phi\ln\tilde h$). After this rearrangement the system collapses to a SINGLE one-dimensional equation in $(\ln\tilde y - \ln\tilde y^*)$ with the analogous coefficient, and the result is:

MRW convergence rate: $$\lambda = \tfrac{(1-\alpha-\phi)(n+g+\delta+ng)}{(1+n)(1+g)} \approx (1-\alpha-\phi)(n+g+\delta).$$ At $\alpha=\phi=1/3$ and $n+g+\delta=0.075$: $\lambda\approx (1/3)(0.075) = 0.025 = 2.5\%$. Half the basic-Solow value of $\approx 5\%$, and matching the empirical convergence rate much more closely. ADDING a second accumulated factor (human capital) slows convergence — exactly the empirical fix we needed.

4.5 Comparative statics: increase in $s_H$

Suppose the economy is in steady state and the government raises $s_H$ permanently (more spending on education). What happens? The $\Delta\tilde k=0$ curve does not move (it doesn't depend on $s_H$). The $\Delta\tilde h=0$ curve shifts UP: at any given $\tilde k$, more is now being invested in $\tilde h$ for any given output, so $\tilde h$ has to be larger before dilution can balance the higher investment flow.

The new steady state $E'$ is therefore north-east of the old one: BOTH $\tilde k^*$ and $\tilde h^*$ are higher. Why both? At first only $\tilde h$ rises (because that's what's directly affected). But the higher $\tilde h$ raises output, which raises absolute investment in physical capital ($s_K\tilde y$), so $\tilde k$ starts rising too. Both stocks accumulate together.

4.6 Switching to the land and land+oil models

Switch from MRW to the land model.
(a) We now move from the MRW model (Lecture 3a) to the Solow model with land (Lecture 3b, first half) — same switch as in §3.5 but now applied to STEADY-STATE properties.
(b) What changes: human capital is gone; land $X$ replaces it as the third factor but is FIXED, not accumulated. The dynamics are summarised by the capital-output ratio $z=K/Y$ derived in §3.6. Instead of asking "what is $\tilde y^*$?", the natural question is now "what is the steady-state GROWTH RATE of $y_t$?" — because $y_t$ does not converge to a constant, it converges to a balanced GROWTH PATH.
(c) New assumption: in steady state $z_t = K_t/Y_t$ is constant. Equivalently, $K_t$ and $Y_t$ grow at the same rate — that's the balanced-growth condition.

4.7 The balanced-growth rate with land

From the per-worker production function (derived in §3.6),

$$y_t = k_t^{\alpha}\,A_t^{\beta}\,(X/L_t)^{\kappa}.$$

Take logs and time differences. Using the notation $g_y \approx \ln y_t - \ln y_{t-1}$ (and similarly for other variables), and noting $g_X = 0$ (land is fixed) while $g_{1/L} = -n$ (since $L_t$ grows at $n$):

$$g_y \approx \alpha\,g_k + \beta\,g + \kappa\,(-n) = \alpha\,g_k + \beta\,g - \kappa\,n.$$

Now invoke the balanced-growth assumption: $K_t/Y_t$ is constant, so $K$ and $Y$ grow at the same rate, which means $k = K/L$ and $y = Y/L$ also grow at the same rate (both divide by $L$). Therefore $g_k = g_y$. Substitute into the equation above:

$$g_y \approx \alpha\,g_y + \beta\,g - \kappa\,n\;\Longrightarrow\;(1-\alpha)g_y \approx \beta\,g - \kappa\,n.$$

And finally use $1-\alpha = \beta+\kappa$ (from $\alpha+\beta+\kappa=1$):

Steady-state growth rate, Solow with land: $$g_y \approx \tfrac{\beta}{\beta+\kappa}\,g\;-\;\tfrac{\kappa}{\beta+\kappa}\,n.$$ Two pieces. The first is a fraction $\beta/(\beta+\kappa) < 1$ of the rate of technological progress $g$ — that's a growth drag: technology is now less effective at creating per-capita growth than in the no-land model. The second is a NEGATIVE term in $n$: population growth itself drives down per-capita growth, because more people get pressed onto the same fixed amount of land.

4.8 Classical vs Modern case

Two limiting cases drive the policy intuition.

Classical case: set $g=0$ (no technological progress) and $n>0$. Then $g_y = -(\kappa/(\beta+\kappa))\,n < 0$. Income per worker DECLINES over time. As population presses on fixed land, real wages fall (the textbook shows $w_t/y_t = \beta$ stays constant, so $w_t$ tracks $y_t$ downward); the rental rate on land $v_t$ rises (since land's share $\kappa$ is constant but $L$ grows); eventually wages reach a subsistence floor, mortality kicks in, $n$ falls to zero, and the economy stagnates. That's the Malthusian endpoint.

Modern case: set $g > 0$, $n=0$. Then $g_y = (\beta/(\beta+\kappa))\,g$, which is positive but LESS than $g$. Even without population growth there is a growth drag, because increasing $K$ and $AL$ press on fixed $X$ — diminishing returns to capital + effective labour even though land per worker $X/L$ is constant.

Which factor wins, $g$ or $n$? The condition for positive long-run growth is $\beta\,g > \kappa\,n$. With Nordhaus's calibration ($\beta=0.6$, $\kappa$ around 0.2 in the land-only version), this becomes $g > n/3$. For Western economies with $n\approx 1\%$ and $g\approx 2\%$, this is easily satisfied. For developing countries with $n\approx 3\%$, you need $g > 1\%$ just to break even. The classical pessimism was wrong for the West and may yet be uncomfortably relevant for the rest.

4.9 Steady-state level along the balanced path

Even though $y_t$ grows in steady state, its LEVEL still depends on parameters. From $y_t = k_t^{\alpha}\,A_t^{\beta}\,(X/L_t)^{\kappa}$ and the steady-state $z^* = k_t/y_t$ being constant, write $k_t = z^* y_t$, so $y_t = (z^*)^{\alpha} y_t^{\alpha} A_t^{\beta} (X/L_t)^{\kappa}$. Solve for $y_t$:

$$y_t^{1-\alpha} = (z^*)^{\alpha}\,A_t^{\beta}\,(X/L_t)^{\kappa}\;\Longrightarrow\;y_t = (z^*)^{\alpha/(\beta+\kappa)}\,A_t^{\beta/(\beta+\kappa)}\,(X/L_t)^{\kappa/(\beta+\kappa)}.$$

Land per worker $X/L_t$ shows up explicitly in the level. According to this model, it's GOOD to have lots of land — more land per worker means a higher steady-state path. Of course the rate of growth along the path is the $g_y$ we already derived, which is independent of $X/L$.

4.10 Adding oil to the land model

Switch from land model to land + oil model.
(a) We now add an EXHAUSTIBLE resource — oil — to the land model.
(b) What changes: production now uses $E_t$, the FLOW of oil; the stock $R_t$ declines geometrically by the extraction rule $E_t = s_E R_t$. The exponent on oil is $\varepsilon$, with $\alpha+\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon=1$.
(c) New assumptions: constant extraction rate $s_E$ (postulated, not micro-founded); $s_E < \delta$ (so the resource is being used "slowly" relative to capital depreciation, guaranteeing a balanced growth path exists).

The derivation is exactly analogous to §4.7. The per-worker production function becomes

$$y_t = k_t^{\alpha}\,A_t^{\beta}\,(X/L_t)^{\kappa}\,(E_t/L_t)^{\varepsilon}.$$

Take log-time differences. The new piece is the growth rate of $E_t/L_t$. From $E_t = s_E R_t$ and $R_t = (1-s_E)^t R_0$, $E_t$ grows at the same rate as $R_t$: approximately $-s_E$ per period. So $g_{E/L} = -s_E - n$. Substituting:

$$g_y \approx \alpha\,g_k + \beta\,g + \kappa(-n) + \varepsilon(-s_E - n) = \alpha\,g_k + \beta\,g - (\kappa+\varepsilon)\,n - \varepsilon\,s_E.$$

Balanced growth condition ($g_k = g_y$) and $1-\alpha = \beta+\kappa+\varepsilon$:

Steady-state growth rate, land + oil: $$g_y \approx \tfrac{\beta\,g - (\kappa+\varepsilon)\,n - \varepsilon\,s_E}{\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon}.$$ Three growth drags now: (1) technology is only a fraction $\beta/(\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon)$ of full power; (2) population growth subtracts at rate $(\kappa+\varepsilon)/(\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon)$ per unit of $n$; (3) extraction itself subtracts at rate $\varepsilon/(\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon)$ per unit of $s_E$, reflecting the fact that the resource is being PROGRESSIVELY EXHAUSTED, not just spread thinly over more workers.

The extra term, $-\varepsilon s_E/(\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon)$, distinguishes oil from land. Land per worker just gets thinner ($\kappa\cdot n$); oil per worker gets thinner AND the absolute stock declines ($\varepsilon(s_E + n)$). With Nordhaus's calibration $\alpha=0.2$, $\beta=0.6$, $\kappa=\varepsilon=0.1$, and $s_E = 0.005$, this gives $g_y \approx 0.75g - 0.25n - 0.125\,s_E$. We'll plug in real numbers in §5.

5.1 MRW numerical verification

Take the standard textbook calibration: $\alpha = \phi = 1/3$, $s_K = 0.2$, $s_H = 0.15$, $\delta = 0.06$, $n = 0$, $g = 0.015$. Verify the steady-state values $\tilde k^* = 14.22$, $\tilde h^* = 10.67$.

Step 1 — compute the dilution factor: $n + g + \delta + ng = 0 + 0.015 + 0.06 + 0 = 0.075$. (Since $n=0$, the $ng$ term drops out.)

Step 2 — compute the exponent denominator: $1 - \alpha - \phi = 1 - 1/3 - 1/3 = 1/3$, so $1/(1-\alpha-\phi) = 3$.

Step 3 — apply the formula for $\tilde k^*$ from §4.1:

$$\tilde k^* = \left(\tfrac{s_K^{1-\phi}\,s_H^{\phi}}{n+g+\delta+ng}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha-\phi)} = \left(\tfrac{0.2^{2/3}\,\cdot\,0.15^{1/3}}{0.075}\right)^{3}.$$

Compute the inside: $0.2^{2/3}$. Take $\ln 0.2 = -1.609$, times $2/3$ gives $-1.073$, $e^{-1.073} = 0.342$. Then $0.15^{1/3}$: $\ln 0.15 = -1.897$, divided by 3 is $-0.632$, $e^{-0.632} = 0.531$. Product: $0.342 \times 0.531 = 0.1816$. Divide by 0.075: $2.421$. Cube it: $2.421^3 = 14.20$. That matches the textbook $14.22$ to two decimal places (rounding in the intermediate steps explains the tiny discrepancy).

Step 4 — same procedure for $\tilde h^*$:

$$\tilde h^* = \left(\tfrac{s_K^{\alpha}\,s_H^{1-\alpha}}{n+g+\delta+ng}\right)^{3} = \left(\tfrac{0.2^{1/3}\,\cdot\,0.15^{2/3}}{0.075}\right)^{3}.$$

Inside: $0.2^{1/3} = e^{-1.609/3} = e^{-0.536} = 0.585$; $0.15^{2/3} = e^{-1.897\cdot 2/3} = e^{-1.265} = 0.282$. Product: $0.585\cdot 0.282 = 0.165$. Divide by 0.075: $2.20$. Cube: $2.20^3 = 10.65$. Match.

Step 5 — output per effective worker:

$$\tilde y^* = \tilde k^{*\alpha}\,\tilde h^{*\phi} = 14.22^{1/3}\,\cdot\,10.67^{1/3} = 2.422\,\cdot\,2.202 = 5.33.$$

Step 6 — the consumption per effective worker in steady state:

$$\tilde c^* = (1 - s_K - s_H)\,\tilde y^* = (1 - 0.2 - 0.15)\,\cdot\,5.33 = 0.65\,\cdot\,5.33 = 3.46.$$

(For reference: if we were at the Golden Rule with $s_K = s_H = 1/3$, $\tilde c^*$ would be maximised. The current calibration has $s_K + s_H = 0.35$, well below the Golden Rule's $s_K^{**} + s_H^{**} = 2/3 = 0.67$. The economy is "underinvesting" relative to the consumption-maximising prescription.)

5.2 Convergence path to steady state

From an arbitrary starting point, how does the economy approach $\tilde y^* = 5.33$? Using the convergence rate from §4.4, $\lambda = (1/3)(0.075) = 0.025$. The linearised approximation gives

$$\ln\tilde y_t - \ln\tilde y^* \approx (1-\lambda)^t\,(\ln\tilde y_0 - \ln\tilde y^*).$$

Half-life: solve $(1-0.025)^T = 1/2$ for $T$. $T = \ln(0.5)/\ln(0.975) = -0.693/(-0.0253) = 27.4$ years. So a country half-way below steady state would take about 27 years to close half of the remaining gap — vs. about 14 years in basic Solow. The slowdown is the whole point.

5.3 Mock exam Q2: simulating Solow with oil

The mock exam question (Q2) asks you to "explain the Solow model with land and natural resources" and to "sketch how you would numerically simulate the transition equations with a software of your choice (Excel, or other)." The first part is §2 + §3 + §4 of this lecture. The second part is a procedural question — what do you actually do to run the simulation? Here's the recipe.

What you need. The state variables to track are $K_t, L_t, A_t, R_t$. The parameter set is $\alpha, \beta, \kappa, \varepsilon, s, \delta, n, g, s_E$, plus initial values $K_0, L_0, A_0, R_0$.

Step 1 — Set up the parameter block. In Excel, allocate a region with named cells: e.g. alpha = 0.2, beta = 0.6, kappa = 0.1, epsilon = 0.1, s = 0.25, delta = 0.05, n = 0.01, g = 0.02, sE = 0.005, and initial values $K_0, L_0, A_0, R_0$. (In Python: a dictionary of parameters.)

Step 2 — Set up a table of time periods. Columns for $t$, $K_t$, $L_t$, $A_t$, $R_t$, then derived columns $E_t$, $Y_t$, $y_t$, $z_t$. Rows for $t = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, 200$ (or whatever horizon you choose).

Step 3 — Fill in row $t=0$. Initial values for the four state variables in the appropriate columns. Then compute the derived quantities at $t=0$: $E_0 = s_E\cdot R_0$, $Y_0 = K_0^{\alpha}\,(A_0 L_0)^{\beta}\,X^{\kappa}\,E_0^{\varepsilon}$, $y_0 = Y_0/L_0$, $z_0 = K_0/Y_0$.

Step 4 — Set up recursion in row $t=1$ using $t=0$ values. Each state variable updates by its own rule:

  • $L_{t+1} = (1+n)\,L_t$
  • $A_{t+1} = (1+g)\,A_t$
  • $R_{t+1} = (1-s_E)\,R_t$
  • $K_{t+1} = s\,Y_t + (1-\delta)\,K_t$

Then re-compute $E_{t+1}, Y_{t+1}, y_{t+1}, z_{t+1}$ from the new state. Copy the row down for all 200 periods.

Step 5 — Plot. The diagnostic plots that show off your understanding are: (a) $\ln y_t$ vs $t$ — should show a roughly linear path with slope equal to $g_y$ from §4.10; (b) $z_t = K_t/Y_t$ vs $t$ — should converge to a constant $z^*$; (c) the GROWTH rate $g_y^t = \ln y_t - \ln y_{t-1}$ vs $t$ — should converge from above (if you start below SS) toward the constant $g_y$.

Step 6 — Verify the analytical prediction. Read off the limiting growth rate from your simulation. Compare to the formula from §4.10: $g_y = (\beta g - (\kappa+\varepsilon)n - \varepsilon s_E)/(\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon)$. They should agree to a fraction of a percent.

That's exactly the answer to mock exam Q2's simulation part. Don't actually run it — the question only asks you to SKETCH the procedure. But the structure above is what they want: name the state variables, name the recursion, name the parameters, name the plots, and check the prediction.

5.4 Nordhaus's calibration crunched

Plug Nordhaus's values into the combined growth formula: $\alpha = 0.2$, $\beta = 0.6$, $\kappa = 0.1$, $\varepsilon = 0.1$, $s_E = 0.005$. The growth-drag coefficient on technology is $\beta/(\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon) = 0.6/0.8 = 0.75$. The coefficient on $n$ is $(\kappa+\varepsilon)/(\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon) = 0.2/0.8 = 0.25$. The coefficient on $s_E$ is $\varepsilon/(\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon) = 0.1/0.8 = 0.125$. So

$$g_y = 0.75\,g - 0.25\,n - 0.125\,s_E.$$

Pick a Western scenario: $g = 0.024$ (2.4% labour-augmenting technological progress), $n = 0.01$ (1% population growth), $s_E = 0.005$ (Nordhaus's calibrated extraction rate):

$$g_y = 0.75\cdot 0.024 - 0.25\cdot 0.01 - 0.125\cdot 0.005 = 0.018 - 0.0025 - 0.000625 = 0.0149.$$

So $g_y \approx 1.5\%$ per year. That's roughly the observed long-run growth rate of per-capita income in developed economies. Nordhaus's combined model with realistic shares and extraction rates produces a sustainable positive growth rate, consistent with the optimistic conclusion: classical pessimism is wrong for the modern West.

Now pick a high-fertility scenario: $g = 0.01$ (1% tech progress, more typical for slower-growing economies), $n = 0.03$ (3% population growth, as in some African countries):

$$g_y = 0.75\cdot 0.01 - 0.25\cdot 0.03 - 0.125\cdot 0.005 = 0.0075 - 0.0075 - 0.000625 = -0.0006.$$

That's NEGATIVE, by a hair. The classical pessimism returns. Of course one wouldn't take this exact number too seriously, but the qualitative point is robust: with high $n$ and modest $g$, the growth drag from $(\kappa+\varepsilon)n$ can swallow the technology contribution entirely.

6. Connections, Empirics, and Common Traps

This section ties the formal results back to data, flags the major assumptions hiding in the model, and points to where the analysis goes next.

6.1 The MRW empirical fit

The original MRW (1992) test of the model uses cross-country data on $y_i$, $s_K^i$, $s_H^i$, and $n^i$. Take logs of the steady-state formula from §4.1 (assuming $ng \approx 0$):

$$\ln y^* \approx \text{const} + \tfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha-\phi}[\ln s_K - \ln(n+g+\delta)] + \tfrac{\phi}{1-\alpha-\phi}[\ln s_H - \ln(n+g+\delta)].$$

This is a clean regression: regress $\ln y^i$ on those two bracketed terms across countries (with $g+\delta \approx 0.075$ a common assumption). If the model is correct, you should get coefficients around 1 each, given $\alpha = \phi = 1/3$.

The textbook's estimation across 65 countries gives slopes $\hat\gamma_K = 0.59$ (s.e. 0.10) and $\hat\gamma_H = 0.93$ (s.e. 0.18). These are individually consistent with 1; taking uncertainty into account, the 95% confidence interval for $\gamma_H$ includes 1, and the interval for $\gamma_K$ comes close. From these two slopes one can back out the IMPLIED $\alpha$ and $\phi$: solve $\gamma_K = \alpha/(1-\alpha-\phi)$ and $\gamma_H = \phi/(1-\alpha-\phi)$ as two equations in two unknowns, and you get $\alpha = 0.23$ and $\phi = 0.37$. Both close to $1/3$. The adjusted $R^2$ is $0.79$, vs. $0.63$ for the basic-Solow regression on the same data.

The convergence regression confirms this story. Estimating the equation $g_T^i = \beta_0 - \beta_1 \ln y_0^i + \beta_2 (\ldots) + \beta_3 (\ldots) + \epsilon^i$ gives an implied $\hat\lambda = 0.011$, compared to the model's prediction of $\lambda = (1-\alpha-\phi)(n+g+\delta) \approx 0.025$ — both in the same 1-3% ballpark, vs. the basic-Solow prediction of 4-6%.

6.2 The substitutability assumption (the elephant in the room)

Everything in the Solow-with-oil model rests on the Cobb-Douglas production function $Y = K^{\alpha}(AL)^{\beta}X^{\kappa}E^{\varepsilon}$. This form has a STRONG implicit assumption: as $E \to 0$, output can still continue (it just becomes infinitely costly to push). Mathematically, the marginal product of energy goes to infinity as $E \to 0$, so prices rise, and producers substitute toward more capital and labour to maintain output. In the limit, $E$ can be arbitrarily small without forcing output to collapse.

This is the optimistic view of resource scarcity: as oil becomes scarce, its price rises, and we invent substitutes. The model produces a coherent story of indefinite growth.

The pessimistic view (held by many natural scientists and ecological economists) is that there are PHYSICAL LIMITS to substitution: you cannot substitute software for the protein in your food, you cannot substitute capital for a habitable atmosphere. Once oil drops below some threshold, output collapses, not gradually but abruptly. If the production function were Leontief instead of Cobb-Douglas (perfect complementarity, $Y = \min(K^\alpha\ldots, \text{const}\cdot E)$), the classical pessimism would re-emerge.

The textbook is candid about this: "Cobb-Douglas production functions assume that there are no limits to the technological possibilities of substituting capital and technologically augmented labour for scarce natural resources... Much of the public debate on the possible limits to growth is really about the validity of this assumption." This is the place where exam-style essay answers should mention the assumption explicitly — the model's conclusion of sustainability is conditional, and being able to articulate that conditionality is a major content point on a Cozzi-style question.

6.3 Other caveats (briefly)

Reverse causality in the MRW regression. The cross-country regression treats $s_K, s_H, n$ as exogenous and tries to explain $y$. But causality may run the other way: rich countries can AFFORD high investment rates and have lower fertility. So the strong correlation between investment and output may be partly $y \to s$, not just $s \to y$.

Hall–Jones and institutions. The MRW regression assumes the same level $A_0$ of technology in all countries. Hall and Jones (1999) argue that residual variation — the bit MRW attributes to "technology" — is itself explained by INSTITUTIONS: protection of property rights, financial systems, openness to trade, quality of public administration. Plotting $y$ against an institutions index gives a positive correlation strong enough to suggest that institutional quality is a major upstream determinant of $s_K, s_H, A$ themselves.

Education subsidies — when do they make sense? MRW's Golden Rule says $s_H^{**} = \phi \approx 1/3$. Most Western countries invest much less than that publicly. Should they invest more? The model alone doesn't say — it's neutral on WHO pays. The case for public subsidy comes from market failures: imperfect credit markets (banks won't lend against future labour income), imperfect insurance markets (you can't insure against failed education), positive externalities (engineers are more productive when their colleagues can read), and distributional concerns (poor parents underinvest in their children's education). Cozzi's lectures emphasise all four. The exam-essay framing on policy implications should mention the externalities and the moral-hazard rationales.

6.4 The forward link to Module 4

One thing has been carefully held constant in all of Module 3: the rate of technological progress $g$. We treated it as exogenous in MRW, as exogenous in Solow-with-land, as exogenous in Solow-with-oil. Module 3 explains a lot — convergence rates, level effects, classical pessimism — but it does not explain WHY some countries are technologically more advanced than others, or where $g$ comes from in the first place.

That gap is the agenda of Module 4: ENDOGENOUS growth. There are two flavours. The externality-based approach (Chapter 8) makes the growth rate of $A_t$ depend on physical-capital accumulation via positive learning externalities. The R&D-based approach (Chapter 9, Romer-style) treats technology as the output of a profit-driven research sector. Both attempt to make $g$ a function of the model's structural parameters and choice variables, rather than a number we just write down. Module 5 then refines the R&D approach to remove an empirically-failing prediction (the "scale effect").

The handover from Module 3 to Module 4 is clean: we have established that adding accumulated capital broadens the model's empirical reach (MRW), and that adding fixed resources creates growth drags that we can quantify (land+oil). What remains is to ENDOGENISE the residual — the part of cross-country income differences attributed to "institutions" or "technology". That's where we go next.

7. Summary

One module, two extensions of Solow, going in opposite directions.

The MRW model (Lecture 3a, Chapter 6)

Add human capital as a second accumulated factor. Three new structural facts: ($i$) inseparability of $h$ from workers (so the wage compensates both raw labour and embedded human capital); ($ii$) the production function is now $Y = K^{\alpha}H^{\phi}(AL)^{1-\alpha-\phi}$ with $\alpha+\phi < 1$; ($iii$) the dynamics are governed by a coupled pair of difference equations in $\tilde k, \tilde h$, with a phase diagram instead of a 1D diagram. The model dramatically improves the empirical fit of basic Solow: convergence rate moves from $\sim 5\%$ down to $\sim 2.5\%$ (matching the data); the elasticities of $y^*$ with respect to $s_K, s_H, n$ go from $0.5, 0.5, -0.5$ to $1, 1, -2$ (also matching the data); the Golden Rule prescribes $s_K^{**} = \alpha$, $s_H^{**} = \phi$, justifying significant public investment in education.

The Solow-with-land/oil model (Lecture 3b, Chapter 7)

Add a fixed factor (land $X$) and/or an exhaustible factor (oil $R$). The new dynamics are summarised by the capital-output ratio $z = K/Y$, which converges to a constant $z^*$. In balanced growth, output per worker grows at $g_y = (\beta g - (\kappa+\varepsilon)n - \varepsilon s_E)/(\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon)$. Three growth drags: technology is less effective (factor $\beta/(\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon) < 1$); population growth pushes $g_y$ down; oil extraction pushes $g_y$ further down. Western calibrations (Nordhaus: $\beta=0.6, \kappa=\varepsilon=0.1, s_E = 0.005, g=0.024, n=0.01$) deliver $g_y \approx 1.5\%$ — sustainable positive growth. High-fertility scenarios can push $g_y$ negative — the classical pessimism returns. Everything hinges on Cobb-Douglas's strong substitutability assumption.

Three takeaways

1. One extra accumulated factor closes both basic-Solow empirical puzzles. Adding human capital fixes convergence speed (down from 5% to 2.5%) AND amplifies the s-effect (elasticity from 0.5 to 1) simultaneously. That's the brilliance of the MRW idea — a single structural change addresses two seemingly-distinct empirical failures.

2. The classical pessimism is conditional, not refuted. Land and oil DO create growth drags. The drags are quantifiable and depend on factor shares and population growth. For modern Western economies with low $n$ and steady technological progress, the drags are small enough that sustained per-capita growth is feasible. For high-$n$ developing economies, the same model can predict ZERO or negative long-run growth in per-capita income. Don't take the model's optimism as universal.

3. The rate of technological progress $g$ is still a black box. Module 3 takes $g$ as exogenous everywhere. Cross-country variation in $g$ — or in the institutions that underpin it — explains a substantial residual portion of income gaps that the structural model alone cannot account for. Module 4 picks up exactly here: WHY do some countries have higher $g$ than others? That requires endogenising technology.
