Macroeconomics III — Module 2 — 2026-05-26

1. The Big Picture

So far we have spent a lot of effort on a single object: a closed economy where every dollar saved at home is invested at home. That model gave us the basic Solow story — diminishing returns, a finite steady state $k^*$, conditional convergence. But it leaves two big things unexplained.

The first thing it leaves out is the rest of the world. Look at any small European economy: Denmark, Switzerland, Ireland. A firm in Zurich does not borrow only from Swiss savers, and a Swiss saver does not have to invest only in Swiss capital. Capital flows across borders, and the Swiss real interest rate is tied — more or less mechanically — to the world rate. If we are going to take the Solow model seriously for any country smaller than China, we have to let capital move.

The second thing it leaves out is sustained growth in income per worker. The basic Solow model, in steady state, has $\dot y / y = 0$. But the most basic empirical fact about advanced economies — what Cozzi calls stylized fact #5 — is that GDP per person has been growing at roughly 1.5-2% per year for two centuries. Zero is wrong. We need a model whose steady state has positive growth in $y$.

Module 2 takes both extensions seriously. Lecture 2a opens up the capital account and asks: what does free international capital mobility do to long-run national income, wealth, factor prices, and welfare? Lecture 2b reintroduces a closed economy but lets total factor productivity grow exogenously at rate $g$ — and shows that the resulting general Solow model has a steady state with balanced growth and a positive per-worker growth rate equal to $g$.

These look like two unrelated extensions, but they share a structural lesson: the Solow framework is flexible enough that we can swap in a different binding constraint — the exogenous world rate $\bar r$ in 2a, the exogenous growth rate $g$ in 2b — and re-derive the model's steady state from the same set of building blocks (capital accumulation, profit-maximizing factor prices, a constant savings rate, a Cobb-Douglas production function). The mathematical machinery is the same; what changes is which variable is endogenous and which is exogenous.

The exam payoff is concrete. The mock exam's Question 2 asks you to explain the Solow model with land and natural resources (Module 3) and to sketch a numerical simulation — and the simulation skill is taught here, in Bolboaca's exercise session 2. Question 3 asks you to derive Cozzi's hybrid model (Module 5) and discuss policy implications — the derive-and-discuss style is exactly what 2a does for the open-economy welfare result. If you can write down the wealth transition equation, derive the open-economy steady state, and explain in plain language why opening the capital account raises average income but can hurt domestic workers, you have rehearsed the exact moves an exam answer requires.

2. Object Definitions

Before we can run the model, we need to be precise about which symbols mean what — especially because some of them (like $Y$ vs $Y^n$, or $k$ vs $\tilde k$) look almost identical but track different real-world objects. Let's introduce them in the order we'll need them.

Capital, wealth, and net foreign assets

In a closed economy there was one variable that captured both stuff installed in factories and stuff owned by the country's citizens — these were the same thing, because foreigners couldn't own domestic capital and domestic citizens couldn't own foreign capital. Once the borders open, we have to split them apart.

Domestic capital $K_t$ is the capital stock physically located inside the country and used for domestic production. It enters the production function. National wealth $V_t$ is the total value of capital owned by the country's residents — wherever in the world it sits. The difference is net foreign assets $F_t$:

$$V_t = K_t + F_t.$$

Reading this: $V_t$ is what the citizens of the country own, $K_t$ is the share of $V_t$ that happens to be invested at home, and $F_t$ is the share invested abroad. $F_t$ can be negative — that just means foreigners own more domestic capital than domestic residents own foreign capital, so the country is a net debtor.

What about the current account? Start from the open-economy intertemporal budget constraint. Setting depreciation to zero for now (we'll relax this in §3.2), national wealth next period equals current wealth plus saving:

$$V_{t+1} - V_t = S_t.$$

But $V = K + F$, and gross domestic investment is $I_t = K_{t+1} - K_t$ (still with $\delta = 0$). Subtracting:

$$F_{t+1} - F_t = (V_{t+1} - V_t) - (K_{t+1} - K_t) = S_t - I_t.$$

Current account identity: $$CA_t \;\equiv\; F_{t+1} - F_t \;=\; S_t - I_t.$$ The current account balance is just the savings-investment gap. A country that saves more than it invests at home is exporting capital ($CA > 0$) and building up claims on the rest of the world.

Figure 1 visualizes the flow. In the closed economy, all saving must equal all investment — the only downstream use of $S$ is $I$. In the open economy, the same $S$ splits into two streams: some goes into domestic capital $I$, and the rest goes abroad as net foreign asset accumulation $CA$.

Two interest rates, two income measures

In the closed economy the real interest rate $r$ was determined endogenously — it adjusted so that the marginal product of capital cleared the domestic capital market. In the small open economy the country is a price-taker on the world capital market. We write $\bar r$ for the world real interest rate, and we treat it as exogenously given. The bar reminds us: this is set by the rest of the world; the small economy cannot move it.

This single change — making $r$ exogenous instead of endogenous — is the entire structural difference between the closed and open Solow models. Capital still earns its marginal product, but now the marginal product is pinned down by an outside number.

Because residents can earn $\bar r$ on whatever foreign assets they hold, the country's total income is no longer just GDP. Gross domestic product (GDP) $Y_t$ is what is produced inside the country: $Y_t = B K_t^{\alpha} L_t^{1-\alpha}$. Gross national product (GNP), or national income $Y^n_t$ is what the country's residents earn — production at home plus interest on their net foreign assets:

$$Y^n_t = Y_t + \bar r \, F_t.$$

If the country is a net creditor, $F_t > 0$, so $Y^n_t > Y_t$. If it is a net debtor, $F_t < 0$, so $Y^n_t < Y_t$ — part of domestic production is being shipped abroad to service the foreign-owned capital. Saving is now defined as a constant fraction of national income (not GDP): $S_t = s\, Y^n_t$. Residents save out of total income, including what their foreign assets pay them.

Labour-augmenting technology

For the second half of the module we'll close the borders again and turn on the engine the basic Solow model was missing: exogenous technical progress. The cleanest way to write this with a Cobb-Douglas production function is to multiply the labour input by a productivity coefficient $A_t$:

$$Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}\,(A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha},$$

where $A_t$ — labour-augmenting technology — grows at a constant exogenous rate $g$, so $A_{t+1} = (1+g) A_t$. The product $A_t L_t$ is called effective labour and is denoted $\tilde L_t = A_t L_t$. The idea is that a unit of raw labour in 2020 is more productive than a unit in 1900 by a factor of $A_{2020}/A_{1900}$ — so what matters for production is not the raw headcount $L_t$ but the technology-weighted headcount $A_t L_t$.

Hicks-neutral vs labour-augmenting. An alternative way to write technical progress is to put it out front as a TFP coefficient, $Y_t = B_t K_t^{\alpha} L_t^{1-\alpha}$, which is called Hicks-neutral. With Cobb-Douglas, the two formulations are equivalent: if $B_t = A_t^{1-\alpha}$, the two production functions are identical (raise the labour-augmenting form to the appropriate power and you get the Hicks form). So when Lecture 2a writes $Y = B K^{\alpha} L^{1-\alpha}$ with constant $B$, and Lecture 2b writes $Y = K^{\alpha}(A_t L)^{1-\alpha}$ with growing $A_t$, the connection is $B_t = A_t^{1-\alpha}$. We use the labour-augmenting form because it makes the algebra of effective workers cleaner.

Beyond exam scope, but useful: for non-Cobb-Douglas production functions, Hicks-neutral, labour-augmenting (Harrod-neutral), and capital-augmenting (Solow-neutral) progress are genuinely distinct ways of modelling technology. Only with Cobb-Douglas does the form not matter.

To analyse the model we'll use per-effective-worker variables:

$$\tilde k_t \;\equiv\; \frac{K_t}{A_t L_t}, \qquad \tilde y_t \;\equiv\; \frac{Y_t}{A_t L_t}.$$

Suppose we double both $K$ and $L$ in the production function $Y = K^{\alpha}(A L)^{1-\alpha}$. Try it: $(2K)^{\alpha}(A \cdot 2L)^{1-\alpha} = 2^{\alpha}\cdot 2^{1-\alpha}\cdot K^{\alpha}(AL)^{1-\alpha} = 2 Y$. So $Y$ is homogeneous of degree 1 in $(K, L)$ at any fixed $A_t$ — and that's what lets us collapse to per-effective-worker form. Dividing $Y = K^{\alpha}(A L)^{1-\alpha}$ on both sides by $A_t L_t$:

$$\tilde y_t \;=\; \frac{Y_t}{A_t L_t} \;=\; \frac{K_t^{\alpha}(A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}}{A_t L_t} \;=\; \left(\frac{K_t}{A_t L_t}\right)^{\!\alpha} \;=\; \tilde k_t^{\alpha}.$$

So in per-effective-worker form the production function is exactly the same Cobb-Douglas relationship $\tilde y = \tilde k^{\alpha}$ that we had in the basic Solow model — only now the $y$ and $k$ are divided by $A_t L_t$ rather than $L_t$. This is the move that makes the general Solow model tractable: in tilde-units, everything looks like the basic Solow model again, and we can re-use machinery we already know.

3. Core Mechanics

We have all the objects. Now: how does the model actually evolve? The two halves of the module have different state variables (wealth $v$ in 2a, technology-adjusted capital $\tilde k$ in 2b), so we'll do them in sequence, with an explicit transition between them.

§3.1 Switching to the open-economy variant

We now move to the open-economy variant of the Solow model. The key difference vs the closed economy: $\bar r$ is set by world capital markets, not by domestic MPK. So instead of $r$ adjusting to clear the domestic capital market, the capital stock $K$ adjusts to satisfy the profit-maximization condition at the given $\bar r$. Throughout §3.1 we assume $\delta = 0$ (a simplification that drops out of all qualitative results — it just keeps algebra cleaner), and capital is perfectly mobile across borders. The country is small, so its own choices don't move $\bar r$.

The complete model has seven equations. Most are familiar from Module 1:

Open-economy Solow model (Lecture 2a, with $\delta = 0$):

$$V_t = K_t + F_t, \qquad V_{t+1} - V_t = S_t,$$

$$Y^n_t = Y_t + \bar r F_t, \qquad Y_t = B K_t^{\alpha} L_t^{1-\alpha},$$

$$L_{t+1} = (1+n) L_t, \qquad S_t = s Y^n_t,$$

$$\bar r = \alpha B \left(\tfrac{K_t}{L_t}\right)^{\!\alpha-1}, \qquad w_t = (1-\alpha) B \left(\tfrac{K_t}{L_t}\right)^{\!\alpha}.$$

The last two equations are the factor-price equations from profit maximization. We've seen them before in Module 1 for the closed-economy case, but they deserve a re-derivation because the way we read them changes.

Derivation of factor prices. The representative firm solves $\max_{K, L} \; B K^{\alpha} L^{1-\alpha} - r K - w L$. First-order conditions: differentiate with respect to $K$ and $L$:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial K}\bigl[B K^{\alpha} L^{1-\alpha}\bigr] = \alpha B K^{\alpha-1} L^{1-\alpha} = r,$$

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial L}\bigl[B K^{\alpha} L^{1-\alpha}\bigr] = (1-\alpha) B K^{\alpha} L^{-\alpha} = w.$$

To write these in per-worker form, notice that $K^{\alpha-1} L^{1-\alpha} = K^{\alpha-1} L^{-(\alpha-1)} = (K/L)^{\alpha-1}$, and likewise $K^{\alpha} L^{-\alpha} = (K/L)^{\alpha}$. So:

$$r = \alpha B (K/L)^{\alpha-1}, \qquad w = (1-\alpha) B (K/L)^{\alpha}.$$

In the closed economy these two equations determined $r$ and $w$ from the predetermined value of $K/L$. In the open economy, the first equation is read differently: $r = \bar r$ is given exogenously, so the equation now determines $K/L$. Algebraically we solve for $k_t = K_t/L_t$:

$$\bar r = \alpha B \, k_t^{\alpha-1} \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; k_t^{\,1-\alpha} = \frac{\alpha B}{\bar r} \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; k_t = \left(\frac{\alpha B}{\bar r}\right)^{\!1/(1-\alpha)}.$$

This is striking: $k_t$ is not a predetermined state variable any more. It has no time subscript in the answer — it's the same in every period. The economic story is that capital flows freely across borders, so if domestic MPK were below $\bar r$, capital would leave instantly until MPK rose back to $\bar r$; if domestic MPK were above $\bar r$, capital would pour in instantly until MPK fell back. So:

Immediate adjustment of $k$: in every period, $$k_t = k^* = \left(\frac{\alpha B}{\bar r}\right)^{\!1/(1-\alpha)}.$$

Domestic GDP per worker and the wage rate inherit the same jump-to-steady-state property. Substituting $k^*$ into $y_t = B k_t^{\alpha}$ and $w_t = (1-\alpha) B k_t^{\alpha}$:

$$y^* = B^{\,1/(1-\alpha)} \left(\tfrac{\alpha}{\bar r}\right)^{\!\alpha/(1-\alpha)}, \qquad w^* = (1-\alpha)\, B^{\,1/(1-\alpha)} \left(\tfrac{\alpha}{\bar r}\right)^{\!\alpha/(1-\alpha)}.$$

These are constants — they depend on parameters $B, \alpha, \bar r$, but not on time, not on the savings rate, not on population growth. They jump to their steady-state levels in period zero and stay there. The savings rate matters for wealth, not for capital, output, or wages.

The wealth law of motion

So what does evolve over time in this model? National wealth $V_t$. Citizens save out of national income, and savings accumulate as wealth. Recall the intertemporal budget constraint, $V_{t+1} - V_t = S_t$, equivalently $V_{t+1} = V_t + S_t$. Using $S_t = s Y^n_t$:

$$V_{t+1} = V_t + s Y^n_t.$$

Now we need an expression for $Y^n_t$ in terms of $V_t$. Start from $Y^n_t = Y_t + \bar r F_t$ and substitute $F_t = V_t - K_t$:

$$Y^n_t = Y_t + \bar r (V_t - K_t) = (Y_t - \bar r K_t) + \bar r V_t.$$

And $Y_t - \bar r K_t = w_t L_t$: the production function exhausts its output in factor payments, $Y = rK + wL$ with $r = \bar r$. So $y^n_t = w_t + \bar r v_t = w^* + \bar r v_t$, since $w_t$ has already jumped to $w^*$. Substituting back into the wealth recursion:

$$V_{t+1} = V_t + s L_t (w^* + \bar r v_t).$$

Divide both sides by $L_{t+1} = (1+n) L_t$ to get per-worker wealth next period:

$$v_{t+1} = \frac{V_{t+1}}{L_{t+1}} = \frac{V_t + s L_t(w^* + \bar r v_t)}{(1+n) L_t} = \frac{v_t + s w^* + s \bar r v_t}{1+n}.$$

Collecting terms in $v_t$:

Wealth transition equation (open economy): $$v_{t+1} = \frac{1 + s\bar r}{1 + n}\, v_t + \frac{s w^*}{1 + n}.$$

This is a first-order linear difference equation in $v_t$ — exactly the kind whose stability we can read off the slope. Two pieces. The first, $\frac{1+s\bar r}{1+n} v_t$, is inherited-wealth growth: existing wealth $v_t$ earns the interest rate $\bar r$ (so per unit of wealth, total resources become $1 + s\bar r$ after saving fraction $s$ of interest income), then everything gets diluted by population growth $1+n$. The second, $\frac{s w^*}{1+n}$, is the contribution from wage savings: each worker earns $w^*$, saves $sw^*$ of it, and that gets diluted across $1+n$ workers next period.

Figure 2 shows the transition diagram — a straight line in $v_{t+1}$ vs $v_t$ space, intersecting the $45°$ line at the steady state $v^*$. Convergence happens staircase-style exactly when the transition line is flatter than the $45°$ line.

The stability condition $s\bar r < n$

The transition line has slope $(1 + s\bar r)/(1 + n)$. For convergence, we need this slope below 1, which simplifies to:

$$\frac{1 + s\bar r}{1 + n} < 1 \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; s \bar r < n.$$

Is this reasonable? Plug in numbers. Saving rate around 0.2, world real rate around 0.03, so $s\bar r \approx 0.006$. Population growth in OECD countries is often 0.01 or below — so naively, the condition seems tight. But there's a clever sanity check using the closed-economy result.

From Module 1, the basic Solow steady-state interest rate in the closed economy is $r^*_c = \alpha n / s$. Quick re-derivation in this notation: in the closed economy with $\delta = 0$, the steady-state condition is $s B k^{*\,\alpha} = n k^*$, giving $k^* = (sB/n)^{1/(1-\alpha)}$; then $r^*_c = \alpha B (k^*)^{\alpha-1} = \alpha B \cdot (sB/n)^{(\alpha-1)/(1-\alpha)} = \alpha B \cdot (n/(sB)) = \alpha n / s$ after simplifying.

Now the world as a whole is closed, so the world interest rate must satisfy $\bar r = \alpha \bar n / \bar s$, where $\bar n, \bar s$ are world averages. Substituting into the stability condition:

$$s \bar r < n \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; s \cdot \frac{\alpha \bar n}{\bar s} < n \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; \alpha \cdot \frac{s}{n} < \frac{\bar s}{\bar n}.$$

With $\alpha \approx 1/3$ and $s/n$ comparable to the world average $\bar s / \bar n$, this becomes $\tfrac{1}{3} \cdot 1 < 1$, which is comfortably satisfied. So $s\bar r < n$ is benign as long as the small economy isn't structurally bizarre relative to the world average.

§3.2 Switching to the closed economy with technological progress

We now move to the general Solow model. The key difference vs the open-economy variant we just finished: we close the borders again (so $\bar r$ becomes irrelevant) and instead introduce exogenous labour-augmenting technological progress at rate $g$. Throughout §3.2 we assume the country is closed (so all of national wealth is invested at home, $V = K$, $F = 0$); depreciation is $\delta > 0$; technology follows $A_{t+1} = (1+g)A_t$. Production is $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}(A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}$.

The complete model:

General Solow model (Lecture 2b):

$$Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}(A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}, \qquad K_{t+1} = (1-\delta)K_t + s Y_t,$$

$$L_{t+1} = (1+n) L_t, \qquad A_{t+1} = (1+g) A_t,$$

$$r_t = \alpha A_t^{\,1-\alpha}(K_t/L_t)^{\alpha-1}, \qquad w_t = (1-\alpha) A_t^{\,1-\alpha}(K_t/L_t)^{\alpha}.$$

The whole point of the per-effective-worker change of variables is that the law of motion becomes formally identical to the basic Solow model. Starting from $K_{t+1} = (1-\delta)K_t + s Y_t$, divide both sides by $A_{t+1} L_{t+1} = (1+g)(1+n) A_t L_t$ to convert into $\tilde k$:

$$\tilde k_{t+1} = \frac{K_{t+1}}{A_{t+1} L_{t+1}} = \frac{(1-\delta)K_t + s Y_t}{(1+g)(1+n) A_t L_t} = \frac{(1-\delta)\, \tilde k_t + s\, \tilde y_t}{(1+g)(1+n)}.$$

Now substitute $\tilde y_t = \tilde k_t^{\alpha}$:

General Solow transition equation: $$\tilde k_{t+1} = \frac{s\, \tilde k_t^{\alpha} + (1-\delta)\, \tilde k_t}{(1+n)(1+g)}.$$

Subtracting $\tilde k_t$ from both sides gives the Solow equation in efficiency units:

$$\tilde k_{t+1} - \tilde k_t = \frac{s\, \tilde k_t^{\alpha} - (n + g + \delta + ng)\, \tilde k_t}{(1+n)(1+g)}.$$

The right-hand side is positive (so $\tilde k$ is rising) whenever $s\, \tilde k^{\alpha} > (n + g + \delta + ng)\, \tilde k$. This is exactly the geometry of the Solow diagram — saving curve above break-even, capital rises; below, capital falls. The extra $ng$ term (product of two small rates) is negligible in practice and is sometimes dropped to write $n + g + \delta$.

The stability condition here is just that the break-even slope eventually exceeds the saving slope, which requires:

$$n + g + \delta + ng > 0,$$

plainly satisfied for any economy with positive population growth, technology growth, or depreciation. Once stability holds, $\tilde k_t$ converges monotonically to a unique steady-state value $\tilde k^*$ — which we compute next.

4. Properties & Proofs

With the laws of motion in hand, we can extract the long-run behavior of both variants. Two questions structure §4: (a) what does the steady state actually look like, and (b) does opening the capital account — or turning on technology — make residents better off in the long run?

§4.1 Steady state of the open economy

Set $v_{t+1} = v_t = v^*$ in the wealth transition equation $v_{t+1} = \frac{1 + s\bar r}{1 + n}\, v_t + \frac{s w^*}{1 + n}$. Multiply both sides by $1 + n$ to clear the denominator:

$$(1 + n) v^* = (1 + s\bar r) v^* + s w^*.$$

Subtract $(1 + s\bar r) v^*$ from both sides:

$$(n - s\bar r) v^* = s w^* \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; v^* = \frac{s w^*}{n - s \bar r} = \frac{s/n}{1 - s\bar r/n}\, w^*.$$

This is positive precisely because the stability condition $s\bar r < n$ holds. Higher $s/n$ → higher long-run wealth (more saving and less population dilution), exactly as in the closed economy.

What about the net foreign asset position $f^* = v^* - k^*$? We have $v^*$ above and $k^* = (\alpha B / \bar r)^{1/(1-\alpha)}$. A useful intermediate relation comes from rewriting $w^* = (1-\alpha) B (k^*)^{\alpha}$: dividing $k^*$ by $w^*$,

$$\frac{k^*}{w^*} = \frac{k^*}{(1-\alpha) B (k^*)^{\alpha}} = \frac{(k^*)^{1-\alpha}}{(1-\alpha) B} = \frac{1}{(1-\alpha) B} \cdot \frac{\alpha B}{\bar r} = \frac{\alpha}{(1-\alpha)\bar r}.$$

So $k^* = \frac{\alpha}{(1-\alpha)\bar r}\, w^*$. Now:

$$f^* = v^* - k^* = \frac{s w^*}{n - s\bar r} - \frac{\alpha w^*}{(1-\alpha)\bar r}.$$

The sign of $f^*$ depends entirely on whether $\bar r$ sits above or below the closed-economy autarky rate $r^*_c = \alpha n / s$ (which we re-derived in §3.1). After algebra (the textbook does the rearrangement; the key step is collecting over a common denominator and using $r^*_c = \alpha n/s$), the sign reduces to:

Net creditor condition: $$f^* > 0 \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; \bar r > r^*_c \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; \frac{s}{n} > \frac{\bar s}{\bar n}.$$ A country becomes a net creditor abroad ($f^* > 0$) if and only if its closed-economy interest rate would have been below the world rate — i.e., it would have saved too much relative to the world's needs in autarky.

The intuition is exactly the Feldstein-Horioka one. A country with a relatively strong propensity to save ($s/n > \bar s / \bar n$) would, in autarky, drive its domestic rate of return down ($r^*_c < \bar r$). Opening the borders lets it earn the higher world rate on its excess saving — and so it accumulates foreign assets.

The welfare gain from opening

Now the most important comparative-statics question of Lecture 2a: does opening the capital account leave national income higher than under autarky? Denote steady-state national income per worker under free capital mobility as $y^{n*}$ and under autarky (closed) as $y^*_c$. Define the ratio

$$x \;\equiv\; \frac{y^{n*}}{y^*_c}.$$

If $x > 1$, opening helps; if $x < 1$, opening hurts; if $x = 1$, opening is neutral. From our formulas (using $y^{n*} = w^* + \bar r v^*$ and substituting our expression for $v^*$):

$$y^{n*} = w^* \cdot \frac{1}{1 - s\bar r/n} = (1 - \alpha)\, B^{\,1/(1-\alpha)} \!\left(\tfrac{\alpha}{\bar r}\right)^{\!\alpha/(1-\alpha)} \!\cdot \frac{1}{1 - s\bar r/n}, \qquad y^*_c = B^{\,1/(1-\alpha)} \!\left(\tfrac{s}{n}\right)^{\!\alpha/(1-\alpha)}.$$

Taking the ratio and using $s/n = \alpha / r^*_c$ (from $r^*_c = \alpha n / s$):

$$x = (1-\alpha) \left(\frac{r^*_c}{\bar r}\right)^{\!\alpha/(1-\alpha)} \!\cdot \frac{1}{1 - \alpha\, \bar r / r^*_c}.$$

Define $\tilde r \equiv \bar r / r^*_c$ — the world rate relative to the autarky rate. Then:

$$x(\tilde r) = (1-\alpha) \left(\frac{1}{\tilde r}\right)^{\!\alpha/(1-\alpha)} \!\cdot \frac{1}{1 - \alpha \tilde r}.$$

Three properties of this function settle the welfare question:

  1. At $\tilde r = 1$ (knife-edge where opening would change nothing): $x = (1-\alpha) \cdot 1 \cdot \frac{1}{1-\alpha} = 1$. National income is exactly the same.
  2. The derivative of $\ln x$ with respect to $\tilde r$ is zero at $\tilde r = 1$: differentiating, $\frac{d \ln x}{d \tilde r} = -\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} \cdot \frac{1}{\tilde r} + \frac{\alpha}{1 - \alpha \tilde r}$. At $\tilde r = 1$, this is $-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} + \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} = 0$.
  3. The same derivative is strictly increasing in $\tilde r$ (the second derivative of $\ln x$ is positive on the admissible range $\alpha \tilde r < 1$). So $\tilde r = 1$ is a global minimum of $x$.

Conclusion: $x(\tilde r) \geq 1$ for every admissible $\tilde r$, with equality only at $\tilde r = 1$. Algebraically — and unconditionally — opening the capital account weakly raises long-run national income, strictly if $\bar r \neq r^*_c$. Notice how strong this result is: it's an always-good claim about a structural reform, and the proof is just calculus on a single function. That said, the result is about average income — the next subsection shows that domestic workers can lose even as the average gains.

What happens to the wage?

From $w^* = (1-\alpha) B^{1/(1-\alpha)} (\alpha/\bar r)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$ and the closed-economy autarky wage $w^*_c = (1-\alpha) B^{1/(1-\alpha)} (s/n)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$:

$$\frac{w^*}{w^*_c} = \left(\frac{\alpha n / s}{\bar r}\right)^{\!\alpha/(1-\alpha)} = \left(\frac{r^*_c}{\bar r}\right)^{\!\alpha/(1-\alpha)}.$$

So $w^* > w^*_c$ if and only if $r^*_c > \bar r$ — i.e., the country had a higher autarky rate than the world. That means it was capital-scarce, a relatively weak saver. In that case, opening pours capital in, raises $k$, raises MPL, raises wages. The converse: a strong-saver country has $r^*_c < \bar r$, so opening sends capital out, which lowers $k$, lowers MPL, lowers domestic wages — and this can be a meaningful drop (textbook calibration: a 10-20% wage cut is plausible for a rich, high-saving economy).

Either way, average income $y^{n*}$ rises — but the rise comes from capital-income gains in the strong-saver case, and from labour-income gains in the weak-saver case. The distributional politics around capital-account liberalization fall right out of this asymmetry: workers in a high-saving developed economy may rationally oppose opening even as the country's average income goes up.

§4.2 Steady state of the general Solow model

Back to the closed economy with technology growing at rate $g$. Set $\tilde k_{t+1} = \tilde k_t = \tilde k^*$ in the transition equation. Multiplying both sides by $(1+n)(1+g)$:

$$(1+n)(1+g)\, \tilde k^* = s (\tilde k^*)^{\alpha} + (1-\delta)\, \tilde k^*.$$

Bringing $\tilde k^*$ terms to one side: $\bigl[(1+n)(1+g) - (1-\delta)\bigr]\, \tilde k^* = s (\tilde k^*)^{\alpha}$. And $(1+n)(1+g) - (1-\delta) = n + g + ng + \delta$, so:

$$(n + g + \delta + ng)\, \tilde k^* = s (\tilde k^*)^{\alpha} \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; (\tilde k^*)^{1-\alpha} = \frac{s}{n + g + \delta + ng}.$$

Raising both sides to power $1/(1-\alpha)$:

General Solow steady states: $$\tilde k^* = \left(\frac{s}{n + g + \delta + ng}\right)^{\!1/(1-\alpha)}, \qquad \tilde y^* = (\tilde k^*)^{\alpha} = \left(\frac{s}{n + g + \delta + ng}\right)^{\!\alpha/(1-\alpha)}.$$

These look exactly like the basic Solow steady states — only with $n + g + \delta + ng$ in the denominator instead of $n + \delta$. The extra $g + ng$ is the tech-dilution term: technology growth means we need more capital just to keep capital per effective worker constant, the same way population growth does.

Now translate back to per-worker (un-tilded) variables. Since $\tilde k = k/A$ and $\tilde k^*$ is constant in steady state, $k^*_t = A_t \tilde k^*$ — capital per worker grows at the same rate as $A_t$, namely $g$. Similarly $y^*_t = A_t \tilde y^*$ grows at $g$. Consumption per worker $c^*_t = (1-s) y^*_t$ also grows at $g$. The real interest rate, plugging $\tilde k^*$ into the FOC, simplifies to:

$$r^* = \alpha (\tilde k^*)^{\alpha-1} = \alpha \cdot \frac{n + g + \delta + ng}{s},$$

a constant — and the real wage $w_t = (1-\alpha) A_t \tilde y^*$ grows at rate $g$.

This is balanced growth in all the respects we'd want it: per-worker quantities ($y, k, w, c$) all grow at the same constant rate $g$, factor prices ($r$ constant, $w$ growing) match this, and the capital-output ratio $k/y = \tilde k^* / \tilde y^* = (\tilde k^*)^{1-\alpha}$ is constant. Stylized fact #5 — sustained positive per-worker growth — is reproduced, and the source is the exogenous $g$.

Golden rule and comparative statics

Per-worker consumption in steady state is $c^*_t = (1-s) A_t \tilde y^* = (1-s) A_t \bigl(\frac{s}{n+g+\delta+ng}\bigr)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$. To find the saving rate that maximizes long-run consumption (the Golden Rule), the $A_t$ factor doesn't matter — it just shifts the whole consumption path up by the same multiplicative factor. So we maximize the $s$-dependent part. Taking logs:

$$\ln c^* = \ln(1-s) + \tfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\, \ln s + \text{(terms not in }s).$$

$\frac{d \ln c^*}{ds} = -\frac{1}{1-s} + \frac{\alpha}{(1-\alpha) s} = 0$ gives $\frac{\alpha}{(1-\alpha) s} = \frac{1}{1-s}$, i.e. $\alpha(1-s) = (1-\alpha)s$, hence $\alpha - \alpha s = s - \alpha s$, so $\alpha = s$. Therefore:

$$s^{**} = \alpha,$$

the same answer as in basic Solow. Technology shows up nowhere in the optimal savings rate, because it just shifts the whole consumption path up by the same factor regardless of $s$.

The rate of convergence

How fast does the model approach steady state? This matters for two reasons: empirics (we'll compare predicted to observed convergence speeds in §5 and §6), and intuition (it tells us how long the model thinks it takes to recover from a shock). The trick is to linearize the transition equation around $\tilde k^*$.

Write the transition equation as $\tilde k_{t+1} = G(\tilde k_t)$, where $G(\tilde k) = [s\, \tilde k^{\alpha} + (1-\delta)\, \tilde k]/[(1+n)(1+g)]$. By definition, $G(\tilde k^*) = \tilde k^*$. First-order Taylor expansion around $\tilde k^*$:

$$\tilde k_{t+1} - \tilde k^* \approx G'(\tilde k^*)\, (\tilde k_t - \tilde k^*),$$

so deviations from steady state decay by a factor $G'(\tilde k^*)$ each period. The rate of convergence is conventionally defined as $\lambda \equiv 1 - G'(\tilde k^*)$ — the fraction of the remaining gap closed per period.

Differentiating $G$:

$$G'(\tilde k) = \frac{s \alpha\, \tilde k^{\alpha - 1} + (1-\delta)}{(1+n)(1+g)}.$$

At $\tilde k = \tilde k^*$, use the steady-state condition $s(\tilde k^*)^{\alpha} = (n + g + \delta + ng)\, \tilde k^*$, which gives $s(\tilde k^*)^{\alpha - 1} = n + g + \delta + ng$. So:

$$G'(\tilde k^*) = \frac{\alpha (n + g + \delta + ng) + (1-\delta)}{(1+n)(1+g)}.$$

Now compute $1 - G'(\tilde k^*)$. Expanding $(1+n)(1+g) = 1 + n + g + ng$:

$$1 - G'(\tilde k^*) = \frac{(1 + n + g + ng) - \alpha(n + g + \delta + ng) - (1-\delta)}{(1+n)(1+g)} = \frac{(1-\alpha)(n + g + ng) + (1-\alpha)\delta}{(1+n)(1+g)}.$$

For small $n, g, \delta$, the denominator is close to 1 and $ng$ is negligible:

Rate of convergence: $$\lambda \approx (1-\alpha)(n + g + \delta).$$ With $\alpha = 1/3$ and $n + g + \delta \approx 0.075$, the model predicts $\lambda \approx 0.05$ — about 5% of the gap closed per year. The data say more like 0.7% (see §6).

5. Worked Examples

Three numerical exercises bring the algebra down to ground. The first two replicate Bolboaca's Exercise Sessions; the third is a convergence-speed calculation that previews the empirical gap we discuss in §6.

§5.1 Autarky vs free capital mobility (Bolboaca Exercise 2.1)

Take a small open economy with parameters $\alpha = 0.3$, $s = 0.15$, $n = 0.03$, $\bar r = 0.03$, $B = 1$. The autarky benchmark uses the closed-economy formula $r^*_c = \alpha n / s = 0.3 \cdot 0.03 / 0.15 = 0.06$. So $r^*_c = 0.06 > 0.03 = \bar r$: the country has a relatively weak propensity to save and would, in autarky, have a higher rate of return than the world. Opening the capital account should bring capital in.

Closed-economy (autarky) steady state (basic Solow with $\delta = 0$):

  • $k^*_c = (sB/n)^{1/(1-\alpha)} = (0.15/0.03)^{1/0.7} = 5^{1.4286} \approx 11.18$.
  • $y^*_c = B (k^*_c)^{\alpha} = 11.18^{0.3} \approx 2.24$.
  • Wage $w^*_c = (1-\alpha) y^*_c = 0.7 \cdot 2.24 \approx 1.57$. (The slides round this to $1.49$ — small differences come from intermediate-step rounding.)
  • Consumption $c^*_c = (1-s) y^*_c = 0.85 \cdot 2.24 \approx 1.90$.
  • Investment $i^*_c = s y^*_c = 0.15 \cdot 2.24 \approx 0.34$.
  • Net foreign assets $f^*_c = 0$, National income $y^{n*}_c = y^*_c \approx 2.24$.

Open-economy steady state:

  • $k^* = (\alpha B / \bar r)^{1/(1-\alpha)} = (0.3 / 0.03)^{1/0.7} = 10^{1.4286} \approx 37.04$.
  • $y^* = B(k^*)^{\alpha} = 37.04^{0.3} \approx 3.33$.
  • $w^* = (1-\alpha) y^* = 0.7 \cdot 3.33 \approx 2.33$. (Slides: $2.22$ with rounding.)
  • $v^* = sw^*/(n - s\bar r) = 0.15 \cdot 2.33 / (0.03 - 0.15 \cdot 0.03) = 0.3495 / 0.0255 \approx 13.71$. (Slides: $13.07$ from $w^* = 2.22$.)
  • $f^* = v^* - k^* \approx 13.07 - 37.04 = -23.97$: the country is a massive net debtor, as predicted because $\bar r < r^*_c$.

National income per worker: $y^{n*} = w^* + \bar r v^* \approx 2.22 + 0.03 \cdot 13.07 \approx 2.61$, up from $y^*_c \approx 2.24$. That's a 16-17% increase in long-run income per worker just from opening the capital account. Where does the increase come from? From the arbitrage opportunity: domestic residents borrow at $\bar r = 0.03$ and earn $r^*_c = 0.06$ at the margin on the additional capital they install.

What about the interest the country pays on its foreign debt? $\bar r f^* = 0.03 \cdot (-23.97) \approx -0.72$. So out of GDP $y^* \approx 3.33$, residents pay $0.72$ in interest to foreign capital owners, leaving national income $y^{n*} \approx 3.33 - 0.72 = 2.61$. Numbers tie.

Sanity check on stability: $s\bar r = 0.15 \cdot 0.03 = 0.0045 < 0.03 = n$. ✓

Now the subtle question Bolboaca asks: which economy has higher consumption per effective worker? Reading the table: Economy 2 (lower $s$) consumes more in steady state.

Why? Because both economies are saving above the Golden Rule rate $s^{**} = \alpha = 1/3$. Economy 1, by saving 0.45 of income, is over-accumulating: an extra unit of saving raises output less than it raises the cost of replacing capital and equipping new effective workers. Economy 2 at $s = 0.35$ is closer to the Golden Rule and therefore enjoys higher consumption.

So the policy lesson reverses: if you're past the Golden Rule, cutting the savings rate raises long-run consumption. This is a steady-state observation. Along the transition, cutting $s$ initially raises consumption even further (less of an unchanged $y$ goes into capital) before $y$ eventually falls as capital depreciates faster than it's replaced. Whether the long-run consumption gain is worth the temporary disinvestment depends on how impatient society is — Bolboaca's slide 34 puts this exact question to the student.

§5.3 How fast does the model think convergence happens?

Take a country with $\alpha = 1/3$, $n = 0.01$, $g = 0.02$, $\delta = 0.05$. The model's predicted convergence rate is:

$$\lambda \approx (1-\alpha)(n + g + \delta) = \tfrac{2}{3} \cdot 0.08 \approx 0.053.$$

That's 5.3% of the remaining gap closed each year. To translate to a half-life — how many years to cover half the distance to steady state — solve $(1 - \lambda)^T = 0.5$: $T = \ln(0.5) / \ln(0.947) \approx 12.7$ years. So if Germany is 50% below its steady-state output per worker, the model says it gets to 25% below in about 13 years, 12.5% below in 26 years, and so on.

Is that realistic? The textbook's cross-country regression (Lecture 2b p24, textbook Fig 5.7) gives an estimated $\hat\lambda \approx 0.007$ — a half-life of about 100 years. So the model overpredicts convergence speed by roughly a factor of 7. We'll come back to this gap in §6 — it's one of the key empirical failures that the next module (Mankiw-Romer-Weil's human-capital extension) addresses by adding a second slow-moving accumulable factor.

6. Connections & Common Traps

Five things worth noting as we wrap up the module: one empirical puzzle, two failure modes of the open-economy result, one limitation of the general Solow model, and the connection to growth accounting (which previews Module 3 and beyond).

The Feldstein-Horioka puzzle

One way to measure international capital mobility is to ask: in a panel of countries, how tightly correlated are domestic saving rates $S_t/Y_t$ and domestic investment rates $I_t/Y_t$? In a perfectly closed world, $S = I$ in every country, so the correlation is 1. In a perfectly integrated world, $S$ and $I$ are decoupled — a country can finance domestic investment by importing capital from abroad — so the correlation can be near zero.

Feldstein and Horioka (1980) regressed

$$\left(\frac{I_t}{Y_t}\right)^{\!i} = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 \left(\frac{S_t}{Y_t}\right)^{\!i} + \varepsilon^i$$

across 16 OECD countries for 1960-74. Their estimate: $\hat\alpha_1 \approx 0.89$. That's very close to one — implying capital was mostly trapped at home, in stark contrast to the apparently liberalized policy regime. This became known as the Feldstein-Horioka puzzle.

By 1990-2004 the estimated $\hat\alpha_1$ had dropped well below 0.5 — capital mobility had visibly increased. In the euro area, post-2000 estimates are around 0.14. So the puzzle has eased substantially but not vanished, and why is capital not even more mobile remains an active research question (transaction costs, home bias in portfolios, exchange-rate risk, sovereign-default risk).

Interest rate shocks

A closed economy is shielded from world interest-rate shocks. An open economy is not. Suppose $\bar r$ rises permanently. The new steady-state $k^*$ falls (since $k^* = (\alpha B/\bar r)^{1/(1-\alpha)}$), so $y^*$ and $w^*$ both fall regardless of asset position — wages depend only on domestic $k^*$, which is pinned by $\bar r$. What happens to national income depends on the country's net foreign asset position:

Net creditor ($f^* > 0$)Net debtor ($f^* < 0$)
$\bar r$ rises$y^{n*}$ rises (higher return on wealth); $w^*$ falls$y^{n*}$ falls (higher debt service); $w^*$ falls
$\bar r$ falls$y^{n*}$ falls; $w^*$ rises$y^{n*}$ rises; $w^*$ rises

Real-world example: when world rates rise, debtor countries (typically poor) suffer a national-income hit even if their domestic production is unchanged in volume — debt service costs more. Workers in both creditor and debtor countries lose from a rise in $\bar r$, regardless of which side of the balance sheet the country is on. This is one of the reasons the textbook is more guarded about capital-account liberalization than the welfare theorem alone would suggest.

No meaningful Golden Rule in the open economy

One quiet result from §4.1 deserves more attention: in the open-economy steady state, $y^{n*} = w^* + \bar r v^* = w^* \cdot \frac{1}{1 - s\bar r/n}$. National income is strictly increasing in $s$ over the entire admissible range $s\bar r < n$. Consumption per worker $c^* = (1-s) y^{n*}$ then has two competing effects of raising $s$ — direct loss $(1-s)$ down, indirect gain $y^{n*}$ up — and the textbook shows these never cancel inside the stability region. There is no interior maximum: optimal $s$ is just as close to $n/\bar r$ as the stability condition allows.

The intuition is that in an open economy, the marginal product of capital is not driven down by extra saving — it's pinned at $\bar r$. So saving doesn't run into diminishing returns the way it does in autarky. Every extra dollar saved earns $\bar r$ forever, and the only check is the stability condition itself. This is a meaningful theoretical observation: it tells us that the Golden Rule logic of save just enough is tied to the closed-economy MPK adjustment, not to anything fundamental about welfare.

The empirical Solow underperformances

The general Solow model passes two basic empirical tests cleanly: positive long-run growth in $y$, and conditional convergence (countries below their steady state grow faster). But it fails two quantitative tests.

(1) The steady-state regression overstates the slope. Cross-country, regressing $\ln y$ on $\ln s - \ln(n + g + \delta)$ should — under the model — produce a slope of $\alpha / (1-\alpha) \approx 0.5$ for $\alpha = 1/3$. The actual estimated slope (textbook Fig 5.7) is about $1.5$. The data say the savings rate matters about three times more for long-run income than the basic Solow model predicts.

(2) The convergence regression understates the speed. Iterating the linearized law of motion from §4.2 forward $T$ periods and converting back to per-worker variables yields the model's convergence equation:

$$\frac{1}{T}\bigl(\ln y_T - \ln y_0\bigr) \;=\; g \;-\; \frac{1 - (1-\lambda)^T}{T}\, \ln y_0 \;+\; \frac{1 - (1-\lambda)^T}{T}\, \ln y^*_0,$$

where $y^*_0$ is the (initial) steady-state growth path and depends on $s$, $n$, $g$, $\delta$, and $A_0$. Substituting the formula for $y^*_0$ and rearranging gives the empirical regression form, with $\beta_2 \equiv (1-(1-\lambda)^T)/T$ — a coefficient on $\ln y_0^i$ that pins down $\lambda$:

$$\bigl(\tfrac{1}{T}(\ln y_T^i - \ln y_0^i)\bigr) \;=\; \beta_0 \;+\; \beta_1 \bigl[\ln s^i - \ln(n^i + g + \delta)\bigr] \;-\; \beta_2\, \ln y_0^i \;+\; \varepsilon^i.$$

We computed the model's predicted $\lambda \approx 0.05$ above. The textbook's OLS estimate across 65 countries (1960-2003) gives $\hat\beta_2 \approx 0.006$ with $T = 43$, which inverts to $\hat\lambda \approx 0.007$ — the data say convergence is 7× slower than the basic Solow model predicts. That factor is human capital, and adding it is exactly what Mankiw-Romer-Weil did in 1992 — the central derivation of Module 3.

The Solow residual (preview of Module 3 and beyond)

From $\ln y_t = \ln B_t + \alpha \ln k_t$ (per-worker, Hicks-neutral form), take time differences:

$$g_y \approx g_B + \alpha g_k.$$

If we observe $g_y$ and $g_k$ and we assume $\alpha = 1/3$ (the standard calibration), we can back out the Solow residual $g_B = g_y - \alpha g_k$ — the part of growth not explained by capital accumulation. Empirically this residual is positive and substantial (often 1-3% per year for advanced economies over long horizons), accounting for the bulk of long-run growth in living standards. Solow himself called the residual a measure of our ignorance: we know it's there but the model doesn't tell us where it comes from. Modules 4 and 5 (endogenous growth) attempt to open the black box.

7. Summary

The module's bottom line is two re-derivations of the basic Solow machinery under different exogeneity choices: opening the capital account makes the world rate $\bar r$ exogenous and the domestic capital intensity an immediate-adjustment variable; turning on technical progress at rate $g$ leaves the closed structure intact but converts the steady-state per-worker growth rate from zero to $g$. Three takeaways:

  1. Capital-account opening always weakly raises long-run national income — proven from the algebra of $x(\tilde r) = y^{n*}/y^*_c$, which attains its minimum of 1 at the knife-edge $\tilde r = 1$. But the wage can fall (for strong savers) or rise (for weak savers), so the distributional politics of liberalization are real.
  2. Exogenous tech growth at rate $g$ gives the basic Solow story balanced positive per-worker growth. Per-worker quantities all grow at $g$, factor prices satisfy $r^*$ constant and $w^*$ growing at $g$, and the Golden Rule $s^{**} = \alpha$ is unchanged. The model's steady state finally matches stylized fact #5.
  3. Quantitative failure modes survive both extensions. The model overpredicts the rate of convergence by ~7× and underpredicts the cross-country sensitivity of $y$ to $s$ by ~3×. Both gaps motivate Module 3's human-capital extension.
