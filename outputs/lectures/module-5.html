Lecture: Endogenous Growth without Scale Effects

Macroeconomics III — Module 5 (Cozzi's lecture notes) — 2026-05-30

1. The Big Picture: A Prediction That Was Too Good

Here is a question that should nag at you after the R&D models of the previous module. If ideas are what drive growth, and ideas come from people, then a bigger economy — more potential inventors — should grow faster. More heads, more ideas, faster progress. The first generation of R&D-based growth models (Romer 1990, and the Chapter 9 model you have seen) say exactly this. The prediction even has a name: the scale effect. It is one of the cleanest, most intuitive predictions in all of growth theory.

It is also flatly wrong.

Think about what actually happened. The number of scientists and engineers doing R&D in the rich world exploded after the Second World War — in the United States the research workforce rose roughly five-fold between 1950 and 1990. If the growth rate were proportional to the number of researchers, productivity growth should have rocketed upward over those decades. It did not. Measured TFP growth was flat, and arguably declined. Charles Jones made this point sharply in 1995, and it became the single most damaging piece of evidence against first-generation endogenous growth theory.

So the field had a problem, and it found two different ways out. Both kill the scale effect; they disagree about almost everything else.

The two escape routes (the heart of this module):
• Semi-endogenous growth (Jones 1995, Kortum 1997, Segerstrom 1998): assume ideas get harder to find as the stock of knowledge grows. Then the long-run growth rate ends up pinned to the population growth rate $n$, not the population level — no scale effect. The catch: if growth is set by $n$ alone, then policy (subsidies, patents, research funding) cannot change long-run growth at all.
• Truly endogenous growth without scale effects (Smulders & van de Klundert 1995, Dinopoulos & Thompson 1998, Peretto 1998, Young 1998, Howitt 1999): assume that as population grows it adds not just researchers but new products, firms, and sectors, so R&D is spread ever more thinly. Then what matters is the fraction of the labour force in R&D, not the level — again no scale effect. But because that fraction is something policy can move, here policy changes long-run growth permanently.

Two escapes, opposite verdicts on policy. Semi-endogenous: policy is impotent in the long run. Truly endogenous: policy is king. And here is the uncomfortable part — both theories fit the available data about equally well. The empirical estimates that would let us tell them apart are frustratingly close to the knife-edge. So which one is right?

Cozzi's (2017) answer — the climax of this module, and the reason it has its own lecture — is: maybe both. Build a model that is a genuine blend of the two mechanisms, and ask which one wins in the long run. The startling result, the one the exam keeps asking about, is that the answer does not depend on how much weight you put on each theory. It depends on demography. If population grows fast enough, the semi-endogenous mechanism takes over and policy is impotent; if population grows slowly, stagnates, or shrinks, the truly-endogenous mechanism takes over and policy is powerful. And since the threshold that separates these two worlds itself depends on policy, a government can in principle flip the entire economy from one regime to the other. In Cozzi's words: "policy could make policy itself more effective for growth."

That is where we are going. This module has no textbook chapter behind it — it is Cozzi's own research programme, and it is mock-exam-load-bearing (the recurring "derive and discuss the policy implications of the Cozzi (2017) hybrid model" question). Everything we need we will build from scratch. Let us start by pinning down the objects.

2. The Objects: Two Knobs That Generate Three Theories

Every model in this module shares the same skeleton, and almost all the disagreement lives in one equation. So let us lay down the shared skeleton first, then zoom in on the equation that does all the work.

The shared skeleton

Output is produced from capital and technology-augmented labour, exactly as in the technology-augmented Solow world:

$$Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}\,(A_t L_{Yt})^{1-\alpha}$$

Reading the symbols: $Y_t$ is output; $K_t$ is the capital stock; $A_t$ is the level of labour-augmenting technology (knowledge — bigger $A_t$ means each worker is effectively worth more); $L_{Yt}$ is the labour employed in goods production; and $\alpha\in(0,1)$ is the capital share (the Cobb-Douglas exponent on capital). The combination $A_t L_{Yt}$ is "effective labour" in the goods sector.

Why $L_{Yt}$ and not just $L_t$? Because in these models the labour force is split between two jobs: making goods, and making ideas. Total labour $L_t$ divides as

$$L_{Yt} + L_{At} = L_t, \qquad L_{At} = s_R\,L_t.$$

Here $L_{At}$ is the labour employed in the R&D sector, and $s_R$ is the fraction of the workforce doing research. This fraction has a name we will use constantly:

R&D share $s_R$: the fraction of the total labour force employed in research and development, $s_R \equiv L_{At}/L_t$. It is a number between 0 and 1 (empirically a percent or two). Crucially, it is a policy variable — subsidies, patent protection, public labs, and graduate funding all push on $s_R$.

Capital accumulates out of saving, and population grows at a constant rate, the usual way:

$$K_{t+1} = s\,Y_t + (1-\delta)K_t, \qquad L_{t+1} = (1+n)L_t,$$

where $s$ is the saving rate, $\delta$ the depreciation rate, and $n$ the population growth rate. So far this is just Solow with a labour split. Nothing here distinguishes the three theories. The distinction lives entirely in how the R&D sector turns researchers into new ideas.

The one equation that matters: the R&D production function

New technology is produced by the research sector. The generic form is

$$\underbrace{A_{t+1}-A_t}_{\text{new ideas this period}} = \rho\; \underbrace{A_t^{\varphi}}_{\text{knowledge you stand on}}\;\underbrace{(\text{R\&D input})^{\lambda}}_{\text{research effort}}$$

Let us read every piece, because the whole module is an argument about two of these symbols. $A_{t+1}-A_t$ is the flow of new technology — the direct output of the R&D sector. $\rho>0$ is a productivity constant (how good researchers are). The term $A_t^{\varphi}$ says that today's stock of knowledge feeds back into the production of new knowledge — researchers "stand on the shoulders" of existing ideas. And the last term is the research effort, raised to a power $\lambda\in(0,1]$ that captures duplication ("stepping on toes": two labs racing to the same patent partly waste each other's effort, so doubling researchers less-than-doubles output).

Now the two knobs. Everything in this module is generated by two independent choices in this single equation.

Knob 1 — the knowledge-returns exponent $\varphi$. Does the existing stock of knowledge make new discovery easier without limit ($\varphi=1$, constant returns to the knowledge stock) or does it eventually get harder ($\varphi<1$, diminishing returns — the "fishing-out" idea: the easy fish get caught first)?

Knob 2 — the R&D input: level or share? Is the research effort measured by the level of R&D labour $L_{At}$ (so a bigger economy means more total research) or by the share $s_R$ (so only the fraction in research matters, regardless of size)?

(One notation warning, because it bites students every year: the $\varphi$ here is the R&D knowledge-returns exponent — it has nothing to do with human capital. This module has no human-capital model, so we are free to use $\varphi$ for this and only this.)

Turn the two knobs and you get exactly the three theories from the big picture. Figure 1 lays them out as a two-by-two map — keep it in your head for the rest of the lecture.

With the map in hand, here are the three R&D equations explicitly. Notice they differ only in the two knobs — same $\rho$, same $\lambda$, same skeleton.

Theory$\varphi$inputR&D equation
Semi-endogenous (Jones)$\varphi<1$level $L_{At}$$A_{t+1}-A_t=\rho A_t^{\varphi} L_{At}^{\lambda}$

Before we start turning these into growth rates, three more definitions we will lean on — stated plainly so they never sneak in as a parenthetical later.

Scale effect: the property that the long-run growth rate rises with the size (level) of the population or economy. A model has a scale effect if a bigger country, holding all rates fixed, grows faster forever.

Semi-endogenous growth: a regime in which the long-run growth rate is determined by exogenous demographics (the population growth rate $n$) and policy can shift growth only temporarily, during the transition.

Truly (fully) endogenous growth: a regime in which the long-run growth rate is determined by choices that policy can move — here the R&D share $s_R$ — so policy changes growth permanently.

Balanced growth path (BGP): a long-run state in which every "per-effective-worker" variable is constant and every per-capita variable grows at the same constant rate. It is the steady state of these models.

3. The Core Mechanics: Where Each Growth Rate Comes From

We now have three R&D equations that differ only in two knobs. The job of this section is to extract the long-run growth rate of technology from each, and to see — concretely — why $\varphi$ and the level-vs-share choice have the dramatic consequences the map promised. Let us take them one at a time.

3.1 The dilution story: why the share, not the level

Start with the intuition behind the truly-endogenous model, because it is the genuinely new idea. Why on earth would only the fraction of researchers matter, and not how many there are?

The picture is this. An economy is not one big lab working on one big problem. It is thousands of sectors, each with its own products and its own technical frontier. R&D done in the semiconductor industry mostly helps semiconductors; it does little for, say, agriculture. So what raises aggregate productivity is R&D per sector. Now ask what happens when population doubles. You get twice as many researchers, yes — but you also get twice as many products, firms, and sectors for them to spread across. R&D per sector is unchanged. Figure 2 traces the chain.

That single substitution — level $L_{At}\rightarrow$ share $s_R$ — is knob 2, and Figure 2 is the entire economic justification for it. Now let us see what it does to the growth rate, alongside knob 1.

3.2 Semi-endogenous: growth chained to demography

Take the semi-endogenous R&D equation, $A_{t+1}-A_t=\rho A_t^{\varphi}L_{At}^{\lambda}$ with $\varphi<1$. The technology growth rate is the flow divided by the stock:

$$g_t \equiv \frac{A_{t+1}-A_t}{A_t} = \rho\,A_t^{\varphi}L_{At}^{\lambda}\,/\,A_t = \rho\,A_t^{\varphi-1}L_{At}^{\lambda}.$$

Read that middle step: we divided $\rho A_t^{\varphi}L_{At}^{\lambda}$ by $A_t$, and $A_t^{\varphi}/A_t = A_t^{\varphi-1}$. Now stare at the exponent $\varphi-1$. Since $\varphi<1$, we have $\varphi-1<0$, so $A_t^{\varphi-1}=1/A_t^{1-\varphi}$ is shrinking as knowledge $A_t$ accumulates. In words: as the economy gets more advanced, each existing idea contributes less to making the next one — discovery gets harder. Left alone, this force drives the growth rate to zero.

What can offset it? Only a rising research input $L_{At}$. So a constant growth rate requires the two forces — the shrinking $A_t^{\varphi-1}$ and the (hopefully) growing $L_{At}^{\lambda}$ — to exactly cancel. Let us impose that. On a balanced growth path $g_t$ is constant, which means $A_t^{\varphi-1}L_{At}^{\lambda}$ must be constant, i.e. its value at $t+1$ equals its value at $t$. Take the ratio and set it to 1:

$$\frac{A_{t+1}^{\varphi-1}L_{A,t+1}^{\lambda}}{A_t^{\varphi-1}L_{At}^{\lambda}} = 1.$$

Now we substitute the growth factors. Because $A$ grows at the (constant, to-be-found) rate $g^{se}$, we have $A_{t+1}/A_t = 1+g^{se}$, so $A_{t+1}^{\varphi-1}/A_t^{\varphi-1} = (1+g^{se})^{\varphi-1}$. And R&D labour $L_{At}=s_R L_t$ grows at the population rate $n$, so $L_{A,t+1}/L_{At}=1+n$ and $L_{A,t+1}^{\lambda}/L_{At}^{\lambda}=(1+n)^{\lambda}$. The condition becomes

$$(1+g^{se})^{\varphi-1}(1+n)^{\lambda}=1.$$

We want $g^{se}$, so isolate the term containing it. Multiply both sides by $(1+g^{se})^{1-\varphi}$ (that flips the negative exponent to positive):

$$(1+n)^{\lambda}=(1+g^{se})^{1-\varphi}.$$

Raise both sides to the power $1/(1-\varphi)$ to free $1+g^{se}$, then subtract 1:

$$g^{se} = (1+n)^{\frac{\lambda}{1-\varphi}} - 1 \;\simeq\; \frac{\lambda\,n}{1-\varphi}.$$

The approximation on the right uses $\ln(1+x)\approx x$ for small $x$: taking logs of $1+g^{se}=(1+n)^{\lambda/(1-\varphi)}$ gives $g^{se}\approx \frac{\lambda}{1-\varphi}\,n$. Now read the result, because it is the soul of semi-endogenous growth: the long-run growth rate is proportional to the population growth rate $n$. Set $n=0$ and $g^{se}=0$ — growth dies. Notice what is absent: $s_R$ does not appear. Doubling the research share raises the level of $L_{At}$ once, but the growth rate depends on how fast $L_{At}$ grows, which is $n$ — untouched by $s_R$. That is precisely why, in this world, policy cannot move long-run growth: only demographics can.

3.3 Truly endogenous: growth you can buy

We now switch to the truly-endogenous model. What changed: both knobs move at once — we set $\varphi=1$ (constant returns to knowledge) and replace the level $L_{At}$ with the share $s_{Rt}$. Throughout we take the share constant, $s_{Rt}=s_R$. The R&D equation becomes $A_{t+1}-A_t=\rho A_t s_R^{\lambda}$. Divide by $A_t$ to get the growth rate:

$$g_t = \frac{A_{t+1}-A_t}{A_t} = \frac{\rho A_t s_R^{\lambda}}{A_t} = \rho\,s_R^{\lambda} \equiv g^{e}.$$

Watch what just happened: with $\varphi=1$ the stock $A_t$ in the numerator and the $A_t$ we divide by cancel exactly. There is no leftover $A_t^{\varphi-1}$ term to erode growth. So the growth rate is immediately constant — no balanced-growth-path argument needed — and equal to $g^{e}=\rho s_R^{\lambda}$.

Truly-endogenous growth rate: $\;g^{e}=\rho\,s_R^{\lambda}.$ Reading it: growth rises with the R&D productivity $\rho$ and, decisively, with the level of the research share $s_R$. It does not contain $n$ at all.

Two payoffs fall out at once. First, $g^e$ depends on the level of $s_R$, and $s_R$ is a policy lever — so a permanent rise in the research share permanently raises growth. Second, $g^e$ contains no population term, so the size of the economy is irrelevant: no scale effect. Figure 3 shows the policy lever directly.

But the curve always slopes up: more research share, more growth, forever. That upward slope is exactly what was missing from $g^{se}$, where $s_R$ did not appear at all.

3.4 The foil: keep $\varphi=1$ but keep the level — and the scale effect returns

It is worth seeing the rejected model explicitly, because it shows that both knobs are doing work. Suppose we set $\varphi=1$ (good) but stubbornly keep the level $L_{At}$ (knob 2 unturned). This is the Chapter 9 / Romer model, $A_{t+1}-A_t=\rho A_t L_{At}^{\lambda}$. Dividing by $A_t$:

$$g_t = \rho\,L_{At}^{\lambda}.$$

Now the growth rate depends on the level of R&D labour. As population grows, $L_{At}=s_R L_t$ grows without bound, and so does growth — faster and faster, forever. That is the scale effect, and it is the prediction Jones showed the data reject. The fix in the truly-endogenous model is precisely to divide that runaway level by the dilution it causes, leaving the bounded share $s_R$.

3.6 Closing the truly-endogenous model: capital and the per-effective-worker form

The R&D block gave us $g^{e}$. To get output per person we still need the capital side. The complete truly-endogenous model is the skeleton from §2 with the truly-endogenous R&D equation plugged in:

$$Y_t=K_t^{\alpha}(A_tL_{Yt})^{1-\alpha},\quad A_{t+1}-A_t=\rho A_t s_R^{\lambda},\quad K_{t+1}=sY_t+(1-\delta)K_t,$$ $$L_{t+1}=(1+n)L_t,\quad L_{Yt}+L_{At}=L_t,\quad L_{At}=s_RL_t.$$

This looks like a lot of moving parts, but the trick — the same one used for technology-augmented Solow — tames it: measure everything per effective worker. Define $\tilde{k}_t\equiv K_t/(A_tL_t)$ and $\tilde{y}_t\equiv Y_t/(A_tL_t)$. Let us reduce output first. Divide $Y_t=K_t^{\alpha}(A_tL_{Yt})^{1-\alpha}$ by $A_tL_t$. Since $L_{Yt}=(1-s_R)L_t$ (the goods-sector share of labour is what is left after R&D takes $s_R$):

$$\tilde{y}_t = \frac{K_t^{\alpha}(A_tL_{Yt})^{1-\alpha}}{A_tL_t}.$$

Write $K_t^{\alpha}=(A_tL_t)^{\alpha}\tilde{k}_t^{\alpha}$ (just multiplying and dividing by $(A_tL_t)^{\alpha}$, using $\tilde k_t=K_t/(A_tL_t)$), and $L_{Yt}=(1-s_R)L_t$, so $(A_tL_{Yt})^{1-\alpha}=(A_tL_t)^{1-\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}$. The numerator becomes $(A_tL_t)^{\alpha}\tilde{k}_t^{\alpha}\cdot(A_tL_t)^{1-\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}=(A_tL_t)\,\tilde{k}_t^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}$. Dividing by $A_tL_t$ cancels the $(A_tL_t)$:

$$\tilde{y}_t=\tilde{k}_t^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}.$$

Output per effective worker is just the usual $\tilde k^{\alpha}$, shrunk by the factor $(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}$ because a slice $s_R$ of labour is off doing research instead of making goods. Now the law of motion for $\tilde{k}$. Start from $K_{t+1}=sY_t+(1-\delta)K_t$ and divide by $A_{t+1}L_{t+1}=(1+g^{e})(1+n)A_tL_t$ — that is the level of effective labour next period, since $A$ grows at $g^{e}$ and $L$ at $n$:

$$\tilde{k}_{t+1}=\frac{K_{t+1}}{A_{t+1}L_{t+1}}=\frac{sY_t+(1-\delta)K_t}{(1+g^{e})(1+n)A_tL_t}.$$

Split the right-hand side over its two terms and recognise $Y_t/(A_tL_t)=\tilde{y}_t=\tilde{k}_t^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}$ and $K_t/(A_tL_t)=\tilde{k}_t$:

$$\tilde{k}_{t+1}=\frac{1}{(1+n)(1+g^{e})}\Big[s\,\tilde{k}_t^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}+(1-\delta)\tilde{k}_t\Big].$$

This is the familiar Solow transition, with one new face: the denominator carries $(1+g^{e})$ alongside $(1+n)$. Technological progress dilutes capital per effective worker just as population growth does — every period, the same capital must be spread over an effective-labour pool that grew by both $n$ and $g^{e}$. That extra dilution term is the only mark the R&D sector leaves on the capital dynamics. In §4 we find where this settles down.

4. Properties & Proofs: Steady States and the Hybrid Theorem

Two tasks here. First, finish the truly-endogenous model by finding its steady state — a quick, satisfying payoff. Then the main event: build Cozzi's hybrid and prove the regime-switching theorem that the exam is built around.

4.1 The steady state of the truly-endogenous model

A balanced growth path is where $\tilde{k}$ stops moving: $\tilde{k}_{t+1}=\tilde{k}_t\equiv\tilde{k}^{*}$. Let us impose that on the transition we just derived. Restating it so it is in front of us:

$$\tilde{k}_{t+1}=\frac{1}{(1+n)(1+g^{e})}\Big[s\,\tilde{k}_t^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}+(1-\delta)\tilde{k}_t\Big].$$

Set both $\tilde{k}$'s to $\tilde{k}^{*}$ and multiply through by $(1+n)(1+g^{e})$:

$$(1+n)(1+g^{e})\,\tilde{k}^{*}=s\,(\tilde{k}^{*})^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}+(1-\delta)\tilde{k}^{*}.$$

Collect the $\tilde{k}^{*}$ terms on the left. Subtract $(1-\delta)\tilde{k}^{*}$:

$$\big[(1+n)(1+g^{e})-(1-\delta)\big]\tilde{k}^{*}=s\,(\tilde{k}^{*})^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}.$$

The bracket is worth expanding, because it is the "effective break-even" rate. $(1+n)(1+g^{e})-(1-\delta)=1+n+g^{e}+ng^{e}-1+\delta=n+g^{e}+\delta+ng^{e}$. So

$$\big(n+g^{e}+\delta+ng^{e}\big)\tilde{k}^{*}=s\,(\tilde{k}^{*})^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}.$$

Now divide both sides by $(\tilde{k}^{*})^{\alpha}$ and by $(n+g^{e}+\delta+ng^{e})$ to gather the powers of $\tilde k^*$. Using $(\tilde k^*)/(\tilde k^*)^{\alpha}=(\tilde k^*)^{1-\alpha}$:

$$(\tilde{k}^{*})^{1-\alpha}=\frac{s\,(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}}{n+g^{e}+\delta+ng^{e}}.$$

Finally raise to the power $1/(1-\alpha)$. Note $\big[(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}\big]^{1/(1-\alpha)}=(1-s_R)$:

$$\tilde{k}^{*}=\left(\frac{s}{n+g^{e}+\delta+ng^{e}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}(1-s_R),\qquad \tilde{y}^{*}=\left(\frac{s}{n+g^{e}+\delta+ng^{e}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}(1-s_R).$$

(The $\tilde y^{*}$ formula is just $\tilde y^{*}=(\tilde k^{*})^{\alpha}(1-s_R)^{1-\alpha}$ with $\tilde k^*$ substituted — same algebra, one fewer power.) Reading $\tilde k^*$: more saving $s$ pushes it up; more dilution ($n,g^{e},\delta$) pushes it down; and the $(1-s_R)$ factor knocks it down because researchers are not making goods to invest. What about actual income per person? Once $\tilde{k}$ has settled at $\tilde{k}^{*}$, the ratio $\tilde y=Y/(AL)$ is constant, so $y=Y/L=\tilde{y}A$ grows at exactly the rate of $A$. Formally, in the long run

$$\frac{y_{t+1}-y_t}{y_t}\;\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}\;\frac{A_{t+1}-A_t}{A_t}=\rho s_R^{\lambda}=g^{e}.$$

So per-capita growth is ultimately technology growth — capital just rides along. Everything funnels back to the R&D sector and the growth rate it produces. This is why the entire hybrid analysis below is conducted on the technology growth rate alone: solve the R&D block and you have solved the model.

4.2 The hybrid model: what if both mechanisms are real?

We now move to Cozzi's (2017) hybrid model. What changed: instead of choosing one R&D equation, we assume the true law of technological progress is a weighted sum of the semi-endogenous and the truly-endogenous mechanisms. What we assume: a fixed weight $\alpha_{sem}\in(0,1)$ on the semi-endogenous piece, a constant research share $s_{Rt}=s_R$, and otherwise the identical skeleton (production, capital, population) from §2. The R&D equation is now

$$A_{t+1}-A_t=\underbrace{\alpha_{sem}\,\rho A_t^{\varphi}L_{At}^{\lambda}}_{\text{semi-endogenous part}}+\underbrace{(1-\alpha_{sem})\,\rho A_t s_R^{\lambda}}_{\text{truly-endogenous part}}.$$

The weight $\alpha_{sem}$ is how much of reality is the diminishing-returns/level mechanism; $1-\alpha_{sem}$ is how much is the constant-returns/share mechanism. Warning, every year on the exam: this $\alpha_{sem}$ is not the capital share $\alpha$ from the production function — it just happens to share a Greek letter. Dividing by $A_t$ gives the hybrid growth rate (using $A_t^{\varphi}/A_t=A_t^{\varphi-1}$ in the first term and the cancellation $A_t/A_t=1$ in the second):

$$g_t=\alpha_{sem}\,\rho A_t^{\varphi-1}L_{At}^{\lambda}+(1-\alpha_{sem})\,\rho s_R^{\lambda}.$$

It nests both theories cleanly: set $\alpha_{sem}=1$ and you have pure semi-endogenous; set $\alpha_{sem}=0$ and you have pure truly-endogenous; in between sits an entire class of models. Cozzi's point is that one of these intermediate models might be the true one — so any conclusion robust across the whole class is worth taking seriously.

The tempting wrong answer. Surely, you think, the long-run growth rate is just the weighted average of the two ingredient rates: $g^{h}\stackrel{?}{=}\alpha_{sem}\,g^{se}+(1-\alpha_{sem})\,g^{e}$. This feels obvious. It is wrong, and seeing why is the whole insight of the model.

4.3 The three-case theorem

The trick is to ask not "what is the average?" but "which term survives in the long run?" Look again at $g_t=\alpha_{sem}\rho A_t^{\varphi-1}L_{At}^{\lambda}+(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}$. The second term is a constant, $(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}$. The first term carries the factor $A_t^{\varphi-1}=1/A_t^{1-\varphi}$, which (since $\varphi<1$) shrinks as knowledge grows — unless the rising $L_{At}^{\lambda}$ holds it up. Whether the first term lives or dies is the entire question, and it turns on how fast population grows.

Case 1 — constant population ($n=0$). Then $L_{At}=s_RL$ is constant; call it $L_A$. So $g_t=\alpha_{sem}\rho A_t^{\varphi-1}L_A^{\lambda}+(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}$. Notice $g_t\ge(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}>0$ always — growth never stops, so $A_t\to\infty$. But then $A_t^{\varphi-1}=1/A_t^{1-\varphi}\to 0$: with $L_A$ pinned down and $A_t$ exploding, the first term is crushed to nothing. In the limit

$$g^{h}=0+(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}=(1-\alpha_{sem})\,g^{e}.$$

Growth is fully endogenous (just scaled by $1-\alpha_{sem}$). The semi-endogenous mechanism, starved of population growth, vanishes.

Case 2 — shrinking population ($n<0$). Now $L_{At}=s_RL_t\to 0$, so the first term's $L_{At}^{\lambda}\to 0$ and $A_t^{\varphi-1}\to 0$ — it dies even faster. Same conclusion: $g^{h}=(1-\alpha_{sem})g^{e}$. Fully endogenous again. Strikingly, the model predicts ongoing growth even as the population dwindles.

Case 3 — growing population ($n>0$). This is the interesting one, and it needs the ratio trick. Move the constant term to the left so the surviving-or-dying term is isolated:

$$g_t-(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}=\alpha_{sem}\rho A_t^{\varphi-1}L_{At}^{\lambda}.$$

Write the same equation one period later (just advance every $t$ to $t+1$):

$$g_{t+1}-(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}=\alpha_{sem}\rho A_{t+1}^{\varphi-1}L_{A,t+1}^{\lambda}.$$

Divide the second equation by the first. The constants $\alpha_{sem}\rho$ cancel, and we use $A_{t+1}/A_t=1+g_t$ and $L_{A,t+1}/L_{At}=1+n$:

$$\frac{g_{t+1}-(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}}{g_t-(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}}=(1+g_t)^{\varphi-1}(1+n)^{\lambda}.$$

On a balanced growth path the growth rate is constant, $g_{t+1}=g_t=g^{h}$, so the entire left-hand side is just $1$. That leaves

$$1=(1+g^{h})^{\varphi-1}(1+n)^{\lambda}.$$

But this is exactly the semi-endogenous balanced-growth condition from §3.2! Solving it the same way — multiply by $(1+g^h)^{1-\varphi}$, raise to $1/(1-\varphi)$, subtract 1 — gives

$$g^{h}=(1+n)^{\frac{\lambda}{1-\varphi}}-1=g^{se}.$$

So with a growing population the hybrid grows at the pure semi-endogenous rate. The truly-endogenous piece, despite its weight $1-\alpha_{sem}$, does not show up in the growth rate at all. But — and this is the catch that creates the threshold — this BGP only makes sense if the first (semi-endogenous) term is actually the larger one, i.e. if $g^{se}>(1-\alpha_{sem})g^{e}$. If instead the constant endogenous term is bigger, then $A_t$ grows faster than $L_{At}^{\lambda/(1-\varphi)}$, the first term collapses to zero after all, and we are back in the fully-endogenous outcome $g^{h}=(1-\alpha_{sem})g^{e}$.

4.4 The threshold $\bar{n}$

So everything hinges on the contest $g^{se}(n)$ versus $(1-\alpha_{sem})g^{e}$. The semi-endogenous rate wins — and prevails — exactly when $n$ is large enough that $g^{se}(n)$ overtakes the endogenous floor. The break-even population growth rate $\bar{n}$ is where they are equal:

$$(1+\bar{n})^{\frac{\lambda}{1-\varphi}}-1=(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}.$$

Solve for $\bar{n}$: add 1 to both sides, raise both sides to the power $(1-\varphi)/\lambda$ (the inverse of the exponent on the left), then subtract 1:

$$\bar{n}=\Big[(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}+1\Big]^{\frac{1-\varphi}{\lambda}}-1.$$ Regime rule: if $n>\bar n$ the economy grows at $g^{h}=g^{se}$ (semi-endogenous wins); if $n<\bar n$ it grows at $g^{h}=(1-\alpha_{sem})g^{e}$ (truly-endogenous wins).

Figure 6 draws the whole verdict. The steady-state growth rate $g^{h}$ is the upper envelope of the flat endogenous floor and the rising semi-endogenous curve — flat for $n<\bar n$, climbing for $n>\bar n$, with a kink exactly at $\bar n$.

Now we can say why the naive weighted average was wrong. The long-run growth rate is not a blend of the two mechanisms — it is a winner-take-all outcome in which one mechanism's term survives the limit and the other's is annihilated. Which one survives is decided by a race between $A_t^{1-\varphi}$ (knowledge accumulation, set by growth itself) and $L_{At}^{\lambda}$ (research labour, set by population growth $n$). Demography is the referee. And — read it off the formula — $\bar n$ depends on $s_R$: the threshold itself is a policy variable. That is the hinge for everything in §6.

5. Worked Examples: Two Economies and a Simulation

Abstract theorems land better once you have pushed real numbers through them. We use the exact calibration from Bolboaca's exercise session, so the numbers are the ones you would meet in a problem set.

5.1 Two economies that differ only in $n$

Take the parameters $\alpha=0.33$, $\alpha_{sem}=0.40$, $\varphi=0.40$, $\lambda=0.80$, $\delta=0.10$, $s=0.25$, $s_R=0.04$, $\rho=1$. Two economies share all of these; they differ only in population growth: Economy 1 has $n=0.04$, Economy 2 has $n=0.02$. Which regime is each in? We need the threshold $\bar n$.

Step 1 — the endogenous floor. $g^{e}=\rho s_R^{\lambda}=1\times 0.04^{0.8}$. Since $0.04^{0.8}=e^{0.8\ln 0.04}=e^{0.8\times(-3.2189)}=e^{-2.575}=0.0761$, we get $g^{e}=0.0761$. The floor is $(1-\alpha_{sem})g^{e}=0.60\times0.0761=0.0457$.

Step 2 — the threshold. $\bar n=[(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}+1]^{(1-\varphi)/\lambda}-1=[0.0457+1]^{0.6/0.8}-1=1.0457^{0.75}-1$. Now $1.0457^{0.75}=e^{0.75\ln 1.0457}=e^{0.75\times0.04467}=e^{0.03350}=1.03407$, so $\bar n=0.0341$, i.e. about $3.41\%$.

Step 3 — classify. Economy 1 has $n=4.0\%>\bar n=3.41\%$ ⇒ semi-endogenous regime. Economy 2 has $n=2.0\%<3.41\%$ ⇒ fully-endogenous regime.

Step 4 — the growth rates. Economy 1: $g^{h}=g^{se}=(1.04)^{0.8/0.6}-1=(1.04)^{1.3333}-1=e^{1.3333\times0.03922}-1=e^{0.05229}-1=0.0537\approx 0.054$. Economy 2: $g^{h}=(1-\alpha_{sem})g^{e}=0.0457\approx0.046$.

Pause on how counter-intuitive this is. Economy 2 has the lower population growth, yet it is the one where policy works — it sits in the fully-endogenous regime, so a rise in $s_R$ there would lift growth permanently. Economy 1, growing its population faster, is trapped in the semi-endogenous regime where only demography moves long-run growth. Slower population growth is what unlocks the policy lever. These are the two economies plotted as the markers on Figure 6 — Economy 2 on the flat part, Economy 1 up on the rising part. Their full steady states differ accordingly:

5.2 Sketching a numerical simulation

The exam very often asks you to "explain how you would numerically simulate" a model — not to run it, just to lay out the recipe. Here is the recipe for the response of growth to a fall in population growth, the experiment in the slides.

Simulation recipe.
1. Fix the parameters ($\alpha,\varphi,\lambda,\delta,s,s_R,\rho$) and the initial population $L_0$.
2. Choose the initial technology $A_0$ so the economy starts exactly on its BGP (otherwise you confound the shock with start-up transients). On the semi-endogenous BGP $g^{se}=\rho A_t^{\varphi-1}L_{At}^{\lambda}$ holds, and solving that for the level gives $$A_0=\left(\frac{\rho\,(s_RL_0)^{\lambda}}{g^{se}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\varphi}}.$$ With $s_R=0.04,L_0=1,\lambda=0.8,\varphi=0.4,\rho=1$ and $g^{se}=0.0537$: $A_0=(0.0761/0.0537)^{1/0.6}=1.418^{1.667}=1.79$.
3. Set $\tilde k_0=\tilde k^{*}$ likewise, so capital also starts on the BGP.
4. Iterate the system period by period: each period compute $Y_t$, update $A_{t+1}$ from the R&D equation, $K_{t+1}$ from the capital equation, $L_{t+1}$ from population growth, then form $y_t=Y_t/L_t$ and its growth rate $g_{y,t}$.
5. At $t=10$, drop $n$ permanently (say $0.04\to0.02$) and keep iterating. Plot $g_{y,t}$.

What does the plot look like? Figure 7 sketches it. The drop in $n$ lowers the long-run technology growth (fewer new researchers each period), so the economy must converge down to a new, lower BGP growth rate. But on the way there is a twist: with population growing more slowly, the existing capital is shared among fewer new workers, so $\tilde k$ and $\tilde y$ rise toward higher steady-state values. That transitional rise in $\tilde y$ temporarily pushes per-worker growth up — above its old level — for a handful of periods, before the lower technology growth wins out.

6. Connections, Policy, and Common Traps

This section does double duty: it collects the mistakes that cost marks, and it develops the policy discussion that the mock exam explicitly demands ("derive and discuss the policy implications of the Cozzi 2017 hybrid model"). Lead with the traps, because avoiding them is free marks.

6.2 Why the scale effect was rejected — the empirical backbone

The argument, due to Jones (1995): the number of researchers in the advanced economies rose enormously over the twentieth century — in the US, the R&D workforce grew several-fold in the postwar decades. A model with a scale effect (the red-cell model of Figure 1) predicts that productivity growth should have accelerated in step (the rising blue line in Figure 8). Instead, measured TFP growth was roughly flat, even drifting down (the red line). The prediction and the data diverge wildly — so the scale effect is rejected. Both surviving theories were built to pass this test, and they do.

On the supportive side, the evidence is kinder to the R&D-share logic. Across firms and over the long run, higher R&D intensity — research spending or research employment as a share of value added — is robustly associated with higher productivity growth. The truly-endogenous model can be recast in terms of R&D as a share of GDP rather than of employment and delivers the same long-run conclusions. So the share-based mechanism has real empirical footing. The honest summary is the one Cozzi gives: estimates of $\varphi$ come out below but close to 1, which is maddeningly inconclusive — it rules neither theory out. That inconclusiveness is the entire motivation for building a hybrid.

6.3 Policy: the part the exam grades hardest

Here is the chain of policy implications, from the simplest to Cozzi's punchline.

(a) Semi-endogenous vs truly-endogenous: temporary vs permanent. In a semi-endogenous world, an R&D subsidy that raises $s_R$ lifts growth only during the transition — long-run growth snaps back to $\lambda n/(1-\varphi)$, set by demography. In a truly-endogenous world, the same subsidy raises growth permanently, to $\rho s_R^{\lambda}$. Same policy, opposite long-run verdict — and which verdict holds depends on which regime you are in.

(b) The hybrid's twist: policy can move the threshold. Because $\bar n=[(1-\alpha_{sem})\rho s_R^{\lambda}+1]^{(1-\varphi)/\lambda}-1$ rises with $s_R$, a government that raises the research share pushes the threshold up. If it pushes $\bar n$ above the actual $n$, the economy flips from the semi-endogenous regime (policy impotent) into the fully-endogenous regime (policy potent). Figure 9 plots $g^{h}$ against the research share $s_R$, holding $n$ fixed. At low $s_R$ the curve is flat — you are in the semi-endogenous regime, where pouring money into R&D does nothing for long-run growth (only $n$ matters). Push $s_R$ past the threshold and the curve lifts off: now you are in the endogenous regime and growth rises with every extra point of research share. The flat-to-rising kink is the regime flip. Use Cozzi's own calibration ($\rho=1,\varphi=0.5,\lambda=0.9,\alpha_{sem}=0.5,n=0.01$): raising $s_R$ from $1\%$ to $3\%$ — the European Union's old Lisbon-Strategy target — moves the threshold $\bar n$ from $0.44\%$ to $1.18\%$. Since actual $n=1\%$, that change carries the economy from $n>\bar n$ (semi-endogenous, where the policy itself was useless long-run) to $n<\bar n$ (fully endogenous, where it is potent). In Cozzi's striking phrase: "policy could make policy itself more effective for growth." The first dose of R&D policy changes the regime so that the next dose finally bites.

(c) Demography writes history. Step back and read $\bar n$ as a statement about eras. When world population growth is very slow — the Malthusian past, or a depopulating future — the economy sits below $\bar n$, in the fully-endogenous regime. When population growth is fast — the centuries after the Industrial Revolution — it sits above $\bar n$, in the semi-endogenous regime. So the model tells a long historical story: fully-endogenous growth dominant in antiquity, semi-endogenous taking over as population accelerated, and — as more countries pass through the demographic transition to low fertility — fully-endogenous growth re-emerging. The model's boldest long-run prediction: if humanity does not grow without bound, the fully-endogenous, policy-sensitive regime will rule the very long run. Strikingly, this holds whatever $\alpha_{sem}$ is — the world could be 99% semi-endogenous in its mechanics, and slow population growth would still hand the long run to the endogenous regime.

6.4 Where this sits in the course

Module 4 gave you the Chapter 9 R&D model with its scale effect — the red cell of Figure 1, with R&D equation $A_{t+1}-A_t=\rho A_t L_{At}^{\lambda}$ and growth rising in the population level. This module is the repair: dilution turns the level into the share and kills the scale effect, and the hybrid reconciles the two surviving theories. For the exam, the load-bearing target is the mock's recurring Question 3, "derive and discuss the policy implications of the Cozzi (2017) hybrid model" — which is exactly §4.2–4.4 (the derivation) plus §6.3 (the discussion). Structure such an answer the way Cozzi grades: (1) motivate — two strong theories, opposite policy verdicts; (2) the model — the linear-combination R&D equation; (3) the result — the three cases and the threshold $\bar n$, with Figure 6; (4) the economics — winner-take-all by demography, and policy moving the threshold. The diagram is graded content, not decoration.

Beyond exam scope, but useful: Cozzi's companion paper (2017b, Economics Letters 155) generalises the hybrid to continuous time and to non-linear combinations of the two mechanisms; the discrete-time version here is all the exam requires. And Cozzi (2007) microfounds knowledge creation through quality-ladder patent races — the machinery beneath the aggregate $\rho A^{\varphi}(\cdot)^{\lambda}$ shorthand we have used throughout.

7. Summary & Formula Reference

The module in four sentences. (1) The early R&D models predicted a scale effect — bigger populations grow faster — which the data flatly reject. (2) Two repairs survive: semi-endogenous growth ($\varphi<1$, level $L_A$), where growth is chained to population growth $n$ and policy is impotent long-run; and truly-endogenous growth without scale effects ($\varphi=1$, share $s_R$), where growth is $\rho s_R^{\lambda}$ and policy is permanent. (3) Cozzi's hybrid blends them, and the long-run growth rate is not the blend but a winner-take-all decided by demography: semi-endogenous if $n>\bar n$, fully-endogenous if $n<\bar n$. (4) Because $\bar n$ rises with the research share, policy can move the threshold — policy can make policy effective.

The one thing to remember: in the hybrid, you do not average the two growth rates — you ask which mechanism's term survives the long-run limit, and demography ($n$ relative to $\bar n$) is the referee.
