The Big Picture: Two Holes in the Basic Solow Model

Two things bother an economist staring at the basic Solow model from chapter 3. Both of them are why module 2 exists.

The first: along its balanced-growth path, the basic Solow model predicts zero growth in GDP per worker. The economy reaches a steady state, capital per worker sits at $k^*$, output per worker sits at $y^* = B(k^*)^\alpha$, and that's it — no more progress. But look at the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, Japan, France: every one of them has produced steadily more output per worker for over a century. That's stylized fact #5 from module 1. A model that misses the single most important empirical regularity in growth data is not a model we can take seriously as a description of long-run reality.

The second: the basic Solow model treats every country as a sealed box. Domestic saving equals domestic investment, full stop. That's not a bad approximation for the 1950s, when capital controls were tight and capital flows between countries were small. But since 1960 international capital mobility has exploded — by 2008 the world stock of foreign-owned assets had risen to historic highs (Lecture 2a p3). If we want our growth model to say something about why poor countries open their capital accounts, or what happens when a country runs a current-account deficit, the basic Solow model has no machinery for it.

Module 2 patches both holes with two extensions of the basic Solow setup. They sit on the same Cobb-Douglas-plus-savings scaffolding, and we'll treat them in one lecture because they share so much structure — almost every formula from chapter 3 carries over with a tweak.

The two extensions, in one sentence each:

Open-economy Solow (Lecture 2a / Ch 4): let capital flow freely across borders. Domestic saving may differ from domestic investment; the difference is the current account.

General Solow (Lecture 2b / Ch 5): let total factor productivity $B_t$ grow exogenously at rate $g$. The steady state now exhibits balanced growth with strictly positive growth in GDP per worker.

Here's where we're going. §2 builds new objects (national wealth $V$, net foreign assets $F$, effective labour $AL$). §3 works out the dynamics — and the punchline is that in the open economy, capital jumps to its long-run value immediately, while wealth crawls; in the general Solow model, things look just like basic Solow but in 'per effective worker' coordinates. §4 covers the big results: a theorem that opening the capital account always raises long-run national income (but can lower real wages in saving-heavy countries), and the balanced-growth properties of the technologically-progressing steady state. §5 puts numbers on it with Bolboaca's table. §6 lays out the empirical fit — directionally right, magnitude-wise off — which is what motivates module 3.

The New Objects: Wealth, Net Foreign Assets, Effective Labour

Module 2 introduces a fair amount of new notation. Before doing any dynamics, let's collect the objects in one place so they don't ambush us later.

The open economy: wealth is not the same thing as capital

In a closed economy the only place a citizen can park savings is the domestic capital stock. So 'national wealth' and 'domestic capital stock' coincide. In an open economy that equality breaks. Domestic citizens can hold foreign bonds; foreigners can own pieces of domestic firms. We need two stocks, not one.

National wealth identity: $$V_t = K_t + F_t$$ where $K_t$ is the domestic capital stock (machines and buildings physically located in the country, regardless of who owns them) and $F_t$ is net foreign assets (what domestic citizens own abroad minus what foreigners own here). $V_t$ is what the citizens of the country own; $K_t$ is what they have to work with.

Concrete example. An American family's claim on a French vineyard counts positively in $F^{USA}$ (an American owns it abroad) but not at all in $K^{USA}$ (the vineyard is in France, not America). Conversely it counts negatively in $F^{FR}$ (a foreigner owns part of France) but positively in $K^{FR}$ (the vines are growing on French soil).

Three accounting consequences fall out immediately.

Saving = change in national wealth. The intertemporal budget constraint, after the simplifying assumption $\delta = 0$ that the textbook uses throughout chapter 4 (it doesn't drive anything important — it just keeps the algebra clean), is:

$$V_{t+1} - V_t = S_t.$$

The country's wealth next period is its wealth now plus what it saves.

GDP is not the same as GNP. Capital invested abroad pays returns to its domestic owners — those returns count as income (they go in citizens' pockets) but not as domestic production:

$$Y_t^n = Y_t + \bar{r} F_t$$

where $\bar{r}$ is the world real interest rate, taken as an exogenous constant (no time subscript). $Y_t$ is GDP, produced inside the country; $Y_t^n$ is GNP / national income, earned by citizens. For a creditor country ($F_t > 0$) GNP exceeds GDP; for a debtor it falls short.

Saving is a fraction of national income, not of GDP. When citizens decide how much to save, they save out of what they actually receive:

$$S_t = s Y_t^n, \quad 0 < s < 1.$$

This is the only behavioural assumption — everything else in this section is accounting.

Combine the three and you get the central identity of open-economy macro, the one a finance journalist invokes any time they say 'the US is running a current-account deficit because it doesn't save enough':

Current account identity: $$\underbrace{F_{t+1} - F_t}_{\text{change in net foreign assets}} \;=\; \underbrace{S_t - I_t}_{\text{saving minus investment}} \;=\; \underbrace{CA_t}_{\text{current account balance}}.$$ If a country saves more than it invests at home, the surplus must flow abroad as a capital outflow, and equivalently the country runs a current-account surplus. Closed economy is the special case $F_{t+1} = F_t = 0$, which forces $S_t = I_t$.

Figure 1 lays out both sides of this identity side by side. In the closed economy on the left, the only thing households can do with $S$ is invest it in domestic $K$. In the open economy on the right, the saving stream forks — some goes into domestic capital (still $I$), and the rest leaves the country as a capital export, building up $F$.

What 'small open economy' means technically

'Open' and 'small' both have precise meanings in this model.

  • Open: goods and capital cross borders without friction (no tariffs, no capital controls). Labour does not cross borders — workers stay put. So we're explicitly modelling capital and trade liberalization, not migration.
  • Small: the country's economic activity has negligible influence on world prices or the world interest rate. Whatever the country does to its domestic savings or investment, $\bar{r}$ is unaffected.
  • Perfect capital mobility: domestic and foreign assets are perfect substitutes. By arbitrage, the domestic real interest rate equals the world real interest rate at every moment: $r_t = \bar{r}$ for all $t$.

This last point is the operational engine of the whole open-economy story. With production $Y_t = B K_t^\alpha L_t^{1-\alpha}$ and profit maximization, the firm sets the marginal product of capital equal to the rental rate. Since $\delta = 0$, the rental rate equals the real interest rate, which equals $\bar{r}$:

$$\bar{r} = \alpha B \left(\frac{K_t}{L_t}\right)^{\alpha - 1}, \qquad w_t = (1 - \alpha) B \left(\frac{K_t}{L_t}\right)^{\alpha}.$$

The first equation determines $K_t / L_t$ uniquely, given $\bar{r}$. This is the line we'll exploit in §3 — it forces the domestic capital intensity to jump immediately to a single value $k^*$.

The general Solow model: time-varying productivity

Swing back now to a closed economy and pivot to chapter 5's extension. The single change from basic Solow: total factor productivity $B$ is no longer constant — it grows at a constant rate $g > 0$, period after period, exogenously.

The production function in 'labour-augmenting' form is:

$$Y_t = K_t^\alpha \left(A_t L_t\right)^{1 - \alpha}, \qquad A_{t+1} = (1 + g)\, A_t.$$

Here $A_t$ is the productivity of one unit of labour; it grows at rate $g$. Under Cobb-Douglas this is fully equivalent to writing $Y_t = B_t K_t^\alpha L_t^{1-\alpha}$ with $B_t = A_t^{1-\alpha}$ — same model, two different ways of parametrizing technology. The labour-augmenting form is the convenient one.

Beyond exam scope, but useful for vocabulary: economists distinguish three flavours of technical progress depending on where the productivity multiplier sits in the production function — Hicks-neutral ($B_t$ multiplies the whole thing: $Y = B_t F(K,L)$), Harrod-neutral or labour-augmenting ($A_t$ multiplies $L$ only), and Solow-neutral or capital-augmenting ($D_t$ multiplies $K$ only). Under Cobb-Douglas these are interchangeable; under more general production functions only the Harrod-neutral form is consistent with balanced growth. Don't worry about this — Cozzi sticks with Cobb-Douglas throughout.

The key analytical move: define variables 'per effective worker'. The effective labour input is $\tilde{L}_t = A_t L_t$ — actual workers weighted by their productivity. Then:

Per-effective-worker variables: $$\tilde{k}_t \equiv \frac{k_t}{A_t} = \frac{K_t}{A_t L_t}, \qquad \tilde{y}_t \equiv \frac{y_t}{A_t} = \frac{Y_t}{A_t L_t}.$$ In these variables, the general Solow model is mathematically equivalent to the basic Solow model with $\tilde{L}_t$ in place of $L_t$, growing at rate $(1+n)(1+g) - 1 \approx n + g$. Every formula from chapter 3 carries over almost mechanically.

The Mechanics: Two Transition Equations

Two transition equations live in this lecture: one for the open-economy wealth dynamics (in $v_t$, slow), and one for the general Solow per-effective-worker dynamics (in $\tilde{k}_t$, also slow but now with technology growing in the background). They differ in their guts but share the same Solow-style logic. We'll do the open economy first, then the general Solow.

Open economy: capital jumps, wealth crawls

Here is the central organising insight about the open-economy Solow model. Two stocks move in this economy — capital $K_t$ and wealth $V_t$ — and they move on completely different timescales.

Capital jumps. Recall from §2 that profit maximization forces $\bar{r} = \alpha B (K_t / L_t)^{\alpha - 1}$. Since $\bar{r}$ is a constant pinned down by the world, this equation has only one possible solution for $K_t / L_t$:

$$k^* = B^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}} \left(\frac{\alpha}{\bar{r}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}.$$

The capital-labour ratio adjusts to $k^*$ within the period through capital imports or exports. Suppose for some reason domestic $k$ sat below $k^*$ for an instant; the marginal product of capital would then exceed $\bar{r}$, foreign investors would flood in (arbitrage), and $k$ would rise until the gap closed. Symmetrically, if $k$ were above $k^*$, capital would flow out. Figure 2 shows the picture.

Output per worker and the wage rate, both functions of $k$, jump alongside:

$$y^* = B^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}} \left(\frac{\alpha}{\bar{r}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}, \qquad w^* = (1-\alpha) B^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}} \left(\frac{\alpha}{\bar{r}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}.$$

These are not 'steady states the economy converges to' in the usual sense — they are the values $y$ and $w$ take in every period from $t = 0$ onward. There is no transitional dynamic in capital. Saving and population growth play no role at all in determining $k^*$, $y^*$, or $w^*$. The contrast with closed-economy Solow is sharp.

Wealth crawls. What does evolve slowly is national wealth $V_t$ — the part of the country's stocks that domestic citizens actually own. Start from $V_{t+1} - V_t = S_t$, divide both sides by $L_{t+1} = (1+n) L_t$:

$$v_{t+1} = \frac{1}{1+n} \left(v_t + s\, y_t^n\right).$$

Now use $y_t^n = w_t + \bar{r} v_t$ (national income per worker = wages per worker plus return on national wealth per worker). With wages stuck at $w^*$ from period zero:

Open-economy wealth transition equation: $$v_{t+1} = \frac{1 + s\bar{r}}{1 + n}\, v_t + \frac{s w^*}{1 + n}.$$ This is linear in $v_t$, not concave — that's the open-economy signature. Why linear? Because at the margin, additional wealth always earns the world rate $\bar{r}$ (you can invest it abroad), so there are no diminishing returns to extra wealth from the country's perspective.

Figure 3 plots the transition line against the $45°$ line. The slope is $(1 + s\bar{r})/(1 + n)$. For convergence to a positive intersection — i.e. for the economy to have a sensible long-run wealth level — we need this slope below 1, which translates to the stability condition $s\bar{r} < n$. We'll defend this assumption in §4. Granting it for now, setting $v_{t+1} = v_t = v^*$ and solving:

$$v^* = \frac{s/n}{1 - s\bar{r}/n}\; w^*.$$

Once $v^*$ is in hand, everything else falls out: net foreign assets $f^* = v^* - k^*$, GNP per worker $y^{n*} = w^* + \bar{r} v^*$, consumption per worker $c^* = (1-s) y^{n*}$. So the open-economy model has a clean two-stage solution: stage 1, jump $k$ to $k^*$ (and along with it $y$, $w$, $r$); stage 2, accumulate $v$ slowly toward $v^*$, with $f$ filling the gap between national wealth and what's actually invested at home.

General Solow: per-effective-worker dynamics

Now we go back to a closed economy with exogenous technological progress at rate $g$. The trick from §2 is to work in per-effective-worker variables $\tilde{k}_t = K_t / (A_t L_t)$.

Start from the standard capital accumulation equation $K_{t+1} = s Y_t + (1 - \delta) K_t$ (now with $\delta$ back in — we're not in the special $\delta = 0$ open-economy world anymore). Divide both sides by $A_{t+1} L_{t+1} = (1+n)(1+g) A_t L_t$:

$$\tilde{k}_{t+1} \;=\; \frac{s\, \tilde{k}_t^\alpha \;+\; (1-\delta)\, \tilde{k}_t}{(1+n)(1+g)}.$$

This is the transition equation in $\tilde{k}$. Subtracting $\tilde{k}_t$ from both sides gives the Solow-equation form:

$$\tilde{k}_{t+1} - \tilde{k}_t \;=\; \frac{s\, \tilde{k}_t^\alpha \;-\; (n + g + \delta + ng)\, \tilde{k}_t}{(1+n)(1+g)}.$$

The composite term $(n + g + \delta + ng)$ is the 'break-even' rate of investment. Enough saving to keep $\tilde{k}$ constant must cover (i) physical depreciation $\delta$, (ii) population dilution $n$ (more workers next period sharing the same capital), AND (iii) technology dilution $g$ — each period $A_t$ rises by factor $(1+g)$, so even keeping $K/(AL)$ constant requires the numerator $K$ to grow at rate $g$ on top of everything else. The $ng$ cross term is just the algebra of compounding two rates together; in practice it's tiny (a few hundredths of a percent) and often dropped.

Mathematically this transition equation is identical to the basic Solow transition equation with $n + g + \delta + ng$ in place of $n + \delta$. The transition curve $\tilde{k}_{t+1} = G(\tilde{k}_t)$ is concave (because of diminishing returns to $\tilde{k}$), starts at the origin with infinite slope, and asymptotes to slope $(1-\delta)/[(1+n)(1+g)] < 1$. So it crosses the $45°$ line exactly once at a positive value $\tilde{k}^*$. Figure 4 is the picture.

Setting $\tilde{k}_{t+1} = \tilde{k}_t = \tilde{k}^*$ and solving:

General Solow steady state (per effective worker): $$\tilde{k}^* = \left(\frac{s}{n + g + \delta + ng}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}, \qquad \tilde{y}^* = (\tilde{k}^*)^\alpha = \left(\frac{s}{n + g + \delta + ng}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}.$$ Compare to the basic Solow steady state: identical formula with $n + g + \delta + ng$ replacing $n + \delta$. The 'effective labour' trick really does pay off — almost no algebraic work.

The interesting move is back to actual per-worker variables. Since $k_t = A_t \tilde{k}_t$ and $\tilde{k}_t \to \tilde{k}^*$:

$$k_t^* = A_t\, \tilde{k}^* = A_0 (1+g)^t\, \tilde{k}^*, \qquad y_t^* = A_t\, \tilde{y}^* = A_0 (1+g)^t\, \tilde{y}^*.$$

Capital per worker and output per worker grow forever at rate $g$. The 'steady state' in the general Solow model is not a constant level — it is a steady balanced-growth path, with positive slope $g$ on a log scale. That's the payoff: a Solow-style model that finally matches stylized fact #5.

The Properties: Stability, the Opening Theorem, Golden Rule, Balanced Growth

With the dynamics in hand, four results matter. The stability conditions (and why they're plausible). The central comparative theorem of the open economy. The golden rule. And the balanced-growth characterization of the general Solow steady state.

Stability conditions — why they're not as fragile as they look

Both transition equations need a stability condition.

  • Open economy: $s\bar{r} < n$. The linear transition line must have slope below $45°$.
  • General Solow: $n + g + \delta + ng > 0$. The concave transition curve's asymptote must lie below $45°$.

The general-Solow condition is obvious in a healthy economy — population doesn't shrink fast, $g$ and $\delta$ are non-negative, so $n + g + \delta > 0$ is essentially automatic. It would only fail in an economy where population is collapsing faster than productivity grows. Set this one aside.

The open-economy condition is more subtle. With plausible OECD numbers $s \approx 0.2$, $\bar{r} \approx 0.03$, and $n \approx 0.005$ (annual population growth), we have $s\bar{r} \approx 0.006 > 0.005 \approx n$. The condition looks violated! What's going on?

Two arguments rescue it.

(1) Read $n$ as growth in efficiency units, not heads. Once we account for labour-augmenting technical progress (as in §3 of the general Solow), 'effective labour' grows at rate $n + g$ — population growth plus productivity growth. For advanced economies $n + g$ is around 2-3% per year, well above $s\bar{r}$. The chapter-4 model abstracts from $g$ for tractability, but the stability condition should really be read as $s\bar{r} < n + g$.

(2) Consistency with world equilibrium. The world as a whole is closed, so the world interest rate must equal the autarky rate of the average country: $\bar{r} = \alpha \bar{n}/\bar{s}$. Plug this into $s\bar{r} < n$ and rearrange:

$$s\bar{r} < n \;\Leftrightarrow\; \alpha \frac{s}{n} < \frac{\bar{s}}{\bar{n}}.$$

Since $\alpha \approx 1/3$ and most countries are not radically more saving-prone than the world average (i.e. $s/n$ is comparable to $\bar{s}/\bar{n}$), this holds with room to spare.

The stability question is interpretive, not just technical. If $s\bar{r} < n$ failed, wealth would grow so fast on its own interest income that it outpaced population growth without limit — wealth per worker would explode. The reason it doesn't fail in practice is that the world interest rate cannot stay above $\alpha \bar{n}/\bar{s}$ globally for very long: capital accumulation worldwide pushes $\bar{r}$ down toward that level. Cozzi reads this condition as 'highly plausible' (Lecture 2a p19).

The opening theorem: free capital mobility always raises national income

This is the most-cited result in chapter 4 and the cleanest theoretical statement in module 2. Compare steady-state national income per capita under autarky (closed Solow from chapter 3, with $\delta = 0$) to that under free capital mobility:

$$y^{n*}_{\text{open}} = (1-\alpha) B^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}} \left(\frac{\alpha}{\bar{r}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}} \cdot \frac{1}{1 - s\bar{r}/n}, \qquad y^{*}_{c} = B^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}} \left(\frac{s}{n}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}.$$

Take the ratio $x = y^{n*}_{\text{open}} / y^{*}_{c}$. Use the closed-economy interest rate $r_c^* = \alpha n / s$ and define $\tilde{r} = \bar{r}/r_c^*$ (the ratio of the world rate to the autarky rate). The ratio $x$ becomes:

$$x(\tilde{r}) = (1-\alpha) \left(\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}} \frac{1}{1 - \alpha \tilde{r}}.$$

Three properties of $x(\tilde{r})$:

  1. At $\tilde{r} = 1$ (the knife edge: $\bar{r} = r_c^*$), $x = 1$. Opening makes no difference.
  2. $d \ln x / d\tilde{r} = -\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{1}{\tilde{r}} + \frac{\alpha}{1 - \alpha\tilde{r}} = 0$ at $\tilde{r} = 1$.
  3. $d \ln x / d\tilde{r}$ is strictly increasing in $\tilde{r}$ (given the stability condition $\alpha\tilde{r} < 1$).

Combine: $x(\tilde{r})$ has a flat tangent at $\tilde{r} = 1$ with value 1, and the derivative is monotone increasing — so $\tilde{r} = 1$ is a minimum, and the minimum value is exactly 1. For ANY $\bar{r} \neq r_c^*$, $x > 1$. Opening strictly raises national income.

The opening theorem (Cozzi Lecture 2a p25, textbook ch4 p18): in the small open economy with perfect capital mobility, $y^{n*}_{\text{open}} \geq y^*_{c}$, with strict inequality except in the knife-edge case $\bar{r} = r_c^*$. Free capital mobility always makes long-run national income per capita at least as high as autarky — and strictly higher unless the country happens to have exactly the same saving capacity as the world average.

Intuition, without algebra: there are always two cases.

If $\bar{r} > r_c^*$, the country has a relatively low saving propensity. Under autarky its capital is scarce and MPK is high. Opening lets foreigners arbitrage by lending capital at the lower world rate; domestic $K$ rises, MPL rises, wages rise, and the country gains by using cheap foreign capital to expand domestic production.

If $\bar{r} < r_c^*$, the country has a relatively high saving propensity. Under autarky its abundant savings have bid the domestic rate down. Opening lets domestic owners earn a higher return on their wealth by investing abroad; the country exports capital, becomes a net creditor, and the foreign return rises GNP above what the closed economy could produce.

Either way, the country exploits the gap between $r_c^*$ and $\bar{r}$. The only time there's no gap to exploit is $\bar{r} = r_c^*$ exactly.

The wage caveat — opening can hurt workers

National income always rises. But the income distribution can shift against workers. From the open-economy $w^* = (1-\alpha) B^{1/(1-\alpha)} (\alpha/\bar{r})^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$ and the closed-economy counterpart:

$$\frac{w^*_{\text{open}}}{w^*_{c}} = \left(\frac{r_c^*}{\bar{r}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}.$$

Wages rise iff $r_c^* > \bar{r}$ — that is, iff the country was a low-saver under autarky. Strong savers see wages fall when they open up. Figure 5 captures the mechanism on each side.

Politically this is the part that matters. Aggregate income rises in every case — but workers in capital-exporting (strong-saver) countries can lose. That's a 'rational' reason for labour movements in rich, high-saving economies to be skeptical of capital-account liberalization. Cozzi's textbook is careful to add: since national income rises, a redistributive policy could in principle make everyone strictly better off — but only if such a policy is actually implemented.

Balanced growth in the general Solow steady state

Switch back to the general Solow setup. In steady state $\tilde{k}_t = \tilde{k}^*$ forever; what do the actual per-worker and aggregate variables do?

VariableSteady-state behaviour
$k_t, y_t, c_t, w_t$ (per worker)Grow at constant rate $g$
$K_t, Y_t, C_t, I_t$ (aggregates)Grow at rate $\approx g + n$
$L_t$Grows at rate $n$
$r_t$, labour share $w_t L_t / Y_t$, capital share $r_t K_t / Y_t$Constant
Capital-output ratio $K_t / Y_t$Constant

This is what 'balanced growth' means in the technical sense: a collection of variables grows at common constant rates, and the 'great ratios' (capital-output, labour share) are constant. Kaldor's stylized facts from 1961 are all hit. The chapter-3 Solow couldn't deliver this; chapter-5's general Solow does, with a steady positive growth rate of $y_t$ equal to $g$.

The intuition for why this works: $\tilde{k}_t \to \tilde{k}^*$ means $K_t / (A_t L_t)$ stabilizes. So $K_t$ must grow at the same rate as $A_t L_t$, which is $g + n$. And $Y_t = K_t^\alpha (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}$ — a weighted geometric mean of two things both growing at rate $g + n$ — therefore also grows at $g + n$. Divide by $L_t$ (growing at $n$) to get $y_t$ growing at $g$.

The golden rule, unchanged

What savings rate maximizes the consumption-per-worker growth path? Differentiating $\tilde{c}^* = (1-s) \tilde{y}^* = (1-s) [s/(n+g+\delta+ng)]^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$ with respect to $s$:

$$s^{**} = \alpha.$$

Same as in the basic Solow model. With $\alpha \approx 1/3$, advanced economies (saving rates around 0.2) are below the golden rule — they save too little, not too much. Saving more would temporarily depress consumption but raise the long-run path. Whether to do it is a question of how much we discount future utility relative to present — and Bolboaca's slides (p33-34) make exactly this point: 'are you willing to endure the short-run pain for the long-run gain?'

Comparative statics: ↑s raises levels, not long-run growth

An increase in $s$ shifts the $s \tilde{k}^\alpha$ curve up in the Solow diagram, as Figure 6 shows. The new intersection with $(n+g+\delta+ng) \tilde{k}$ is at a higher $\tilde{k}^{*\prime} > \tilde{k}^*$. So $\tilde{y}^*$ rises, and the level of the long-run growth path of $y_t$ shifts up.

But the slope of that path is still $g$. Saving more is a level effect, not a growth effect. During the transition, the growth rate of $y_t$ temporarily jumps above $g$, then monotonically falls back. Figure 7 plots $\ln y_t$ over time to make the level-vs-growth distinction unmissable.

That's the structural limitation. The general Solow model can explain levels of per-capita income across countries (a country with higher $s$ ends up richer), but the source of long-run growth — what determines $g$ — is left unexplained. Module 4 will try to endogenize $g$ via R&D and externalities. Modules 5 (no scale effects) and 6 (efficiency wages) build on that further.

Three observations are worth lingering on in the bar chart.

(1) The wage rises by exactly the same percentage as GDP. Both jump 49%. That's because under Cobb-Douglas the wage is the constant fraction $(1-\alpha) = 0.7$ of GDP per worker, so $w^*$ and $y^*$ have to move together one-for-one. In this weak-saver country, opening up means workers come out ahead — capital flowed in, $K/L$ rose, MPL rose, wages rose. Compare this to a strong-saver country, where the same logic would run in reverse: opening would cause capital to flow OUT, $K/L$ to fall, MPL to fall, and $w^*$ to fall by some percentage less than the inverse of the gain in $y^{n*}$.

(2) National income rises by much less than GDP. GDP jumps 49%, but national income jumps only 17%. The reason: a chunk of the extra domestic production is owed to foreign investors who financed the capital inflow. Look at $\bar{r} f^* = -0.72$ in the table — that's the country paying 0.72 units of interest abroad every period. Subtract that drag from the 1.09 unit rise in $y^*$ (from 2.24 to 3.33), and you almost exactly recover the 0.37 unit rise in $y^{n*}$ (from 2.24 to 2.61). The arithmetic checks out: $1.09 - 0.72 = 0.37$.

(3) The opening theorem is satisfied — but the gain is modest. National income rises (17%), and consumption rises proportionally. So yes, $y^{n*}_{\text{open}} > y^*_{c}$ as §4 promised. But the size of the welfare gain is meaningfully less than the headline GDP number suggests. A casual observer staring only at $y^*$ would think the country had nearly doubled its standard of living; the citizen actually consuming the output sees a much more modest 17% improvement.

If Bolboaca's calibration had instead been a strong-saver country ($r_c^* < \bar{r}$), three things would flip. First, $f^*$ would be positive — the country would be a net creditor abroad. Second, $w^*$ would fall from 1.49 to something lower (capital flowing OUT reduces domestic $K/L$). Third, while aggregate $y^{n*}$ would still rise (opening always helps in the aggregate), the distribution of that rise would tilt against workers and toward wealth-owners. Same model, reverse politics. That's the open-economy theorem in its full bite.

Connections and Empirical Traps

This section collects the empirical evidence — what works in the data, what doesn't — and the connections to other modules. Cozzi's exam profile flags closed-vs-open comparison, $CA = S - I$, and the general Solow steady state as the three hot-spots for module 2, so the traps here matter for exam purposes.

Feldstein-Horioka: did capital mobility actually rise?

If we believe the small-open-economy model with perfect capital mobility, then in steady state the domestic investment rate $I/Y = n(K/Y)^*$ should be independent of the domestic savings rate $s$ — investment is pinned by the world interest rate, not by domestic saving. So in a cross-country regression:

$$\left(\frac{I_t}{Y_t}\right)^i = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 \left(\frac{S_t}{Y_t}\right)^i$$

the coefficient $\alpha_1$ should be approximately zero. At the other extreme, in a closed economy $\alpha_1 = 1$ (saving equals investment by definition).

Feldstein and Horioka ran this regression in 1980 for 16 OECD countries over 1960-74 and got $\alpha_1 \approx 0.89$. Saving and investment were highly correlated within countries — almost as if there were no capital mobility at all. This became known as the 'Feldstein-Horioka puzzle': how can capital mobility be so 'perfect' in theory and so absent in practice?

Cozzi shows the same regression done on 1990-2004 data: the slope drops to about $0.4$. Table 4.2 in the textbook reports the euro-area slope falling to $\alpha_1 \approx 0.14$ by 1991-2001. Capital mobility IS rising over time, just as the 'globalization' narrative would predict — the puzzle was a snapshot of mid-20th-century reality, when capital controls were still pervasive. The trend since 1980 vindicates the small-open-economy framework as an increasingly good approximation. Figure 9 sketches the picture.

Interest rate shocks — the price of openness

The flip side of being plugged into the world capital market: when $\bar{r}$ moves, the country gets dragged along. Suppose $\bar{r}$ rises permanently (perhaps because a large country abroad expanded government consumption). Then $k^* = B^{1/(1-\alpha)}(\alpha/\bar{r})^{1/(1-\alpha)}$ falls (capital is now more expensive to hold), $y^*$ falls, $w^*$ falls. National income $y^{n*}$ may rise or fall depending on whether the country is a net creditor (gains from higher returns on its assets) or a net debtor (pays more on its debt). The textbook's table for this (Lecture 2a p29) is worth memorising for an exam:

ShockNet creditorNet debtor
$\bar{r}$ rises$y^n$ rises, $w$ falls$y^n$ falls, $w$ falls
$\bar{r}$ falls$y^n$ falls, $w$ rises$y^n$ rises, $w$ rises

A closed economy avoids all of this — at the cost of permanently lower $y^{n*}$ (by the opening theorem). So the meaningful trade-off the chapter raises: stability vs. average income.

The OLS slope problem in general Solow

Take logs of the general-Solow steady-state $y^* = A_t \tilde{y}^*$:

$$\ln y^*_t = \ln A_t + \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\left[\ln s - \ln(n + g + \delta + ng)\right].$$

Across countries, if we assume a common $A_t$ and a common technology, the slope of $\ln y^*$ on $[\ln s - \ln(n+g+\delta)]$ should be $\alpha/(1-\alpha) \approx 0.5$ for $\alpha = 1/3$.

Cozzi reports the actual OLS estimate (65 countries, 1960-2003): slope $\approx 1.52$, with 95% confidence interval $[1.24, 1.80]$ — a clean miss. The slope implies $\alpha \approx 0.6$, way above the capital share observed in national accounts ($\approx 1/3$).

What this tells us, and how module 3 fixes it: the general Solow model is directionally right (high $s$ and low $n$ do correlate with high $y$), but the magnitudes are too weak — the model underestimates the effect of $s$ and $n$ on long-run income. Mankiw, Romer, and Weil's 1992 paper, which is the foundation of module 3, fixes this by adding human capital to the production function. With both physical and human capital accumulating, the effective capital share rises to about $2/3$, so $\alpha/(1-\alpha) \to 2$, and the empirical slope of 1.52 stops looking like a refutation. The 'augmented Solow model' becomes empirically respectable.

The convergence-rate problem

The general Solow model also predicts the speed at which countries converge to their steady states. Linearizing the transition equation around $\tilde{k}^*$ gives the rate of convergence:

$$\lambda \approx (1 - \alpha)(n + g + \delta) \approx 0.05$$

for $\alpha = 1/3$, $n + g + \delta \approx 0.075$. So $5\%$ of the gap to steady state should be closed each year — roughly a 14-year half-life.

Empirical estimate from the same 65-country sample: $\lambda \approx 0.007$ — $0.7\%$ per year. A 100-year half-life. The model overpredicts the speed of convergence by an order of magnitude. Module 3's human-capital extension brings the predicted $\lambda$ down (because raising the effective capital share to $2/3$ slows convergence), again putting the model closer to the data.

Growth accounting and the Solow residual

One reference idea worth knowing because it appears throughout empirical macro. Take logs and differences of the Cobb-Douglas production function:

$$g^Y \approx g^B + \alpha g^K + (1 - \alpha) g^L.$$

Given data on $g^Y$, $g^K$, $g^L$ and an assumed $\alpha$, the residual $g^B = g^Y - \alpha g^K - (1-\alpha) g^L$ is the 'Solow residual' — the part of output growth not explained by inputs. Solow himself called it 'a measure of our ignorance'. It became the standard empirical proxy for TFP growth. Cozzi's modules 4 and 5 are basically attempts to explain what's inside this residual rather than just measure it.

None of the four mock-exam questions hit module 2 directly, but Cozzi's 'Describe + critique' template (Q1 in the mock: 'Describe the main empirical evidence supporting and contradicting the basic Solow model') extends cleanly to the general Solow model. If a future exam asks 'Describe the empirical evidence supporting and contradicting the general Solow model with technological progress', the answer should hit four points: (i) directional correlations of $y$ with $s, n$ are confirmed (positive); (ii) balanced growth in advanced economies matches steady-state prediction; (iii) BUT the OLS slope is too steep (1.52 vs theory's 0.5) — model underestimates; (iv) AND the empirical convergence rate (0.7%) is far below the theoretical 5%. For the open economy, an 'Explain + applied' question on 'Explain the open-economy Solow model. Summary and Formula Reference

Two extensions of basic Solow, two different patches.

Open-economy Solow (Lecture 2a / Ch 4)

  • What's different from closed Solow: capital can flow across borders; saving may differ from domestic investment by the current account; the domestic real interest rate is pinned by the world, not by domestic conditions.
  • Two-speed dynamics: $k$ jumps to $k^*$ immediately via capital flows; $v$ accumulates gradually via saving.
  • Opening theorem: $y^{n*}_{\text{open}} \geq y^*_{c}$ always; strict inequality unless $\bar{r} = r_c^*$.
  • Distributional caveat: a strong-saver country sees wages fall on opening; a weak-saver country sees wages rise. Net foreign assets sign: $f^* > 0$ iff $\bar{r} > r_c^*$.

General Solow with technological progress (Lecture 2b / Ch 5)

  • What's different from basic Solow: TFP grows at exogenous rate $g$; model is solved in per-effective-worker variables $\tilde{k} = K/(AL)$.
  • Balanced growth in steady state: $k, y, c, w$ all grow at rate $g$; aggregates $K, Y, C, I$ at rate $g + n$; $r$ and factor shares constant; $K/Y$ constant.
  • Golden rule: $s^{**} = \alpha$, unchanged from basic Solow.
  • Policy: raising $s$ shifts the level of the growth path up, but cannot raise long-run $g$, which is exogenous. Module 4 will try to endogenize it.

Empirical scorecard

  • ✓ Directionally right: high $s$ and low $n$ correlate with high $y$.
  • ✓ Balanced growth: Kaldor's stylized facts match.
  • ✓ Capital mobility rising over time (Feldstein-Horioka slope falling from 0.89 to 0.4).
  • ✗ Magnitude of level effect: regression slope on $\ln(s/(n+g+\delta))$ is 1.52, theory predicts ≈ 0.5.
  • ✗ Speed of convergence: empirical $\lambda \approx 0.7\%$, theoretical $\lambda \approx 5\%$.
  • ✗ Source of growth: $g$ is exogenous and unexplained.
