Lecture: Human Capital and Scarce Resources

Macroeconomics III — Module 3 — 2026-05-25

1. The Big Picture

Module 3 is where the basic Solow model gets two upgrades that pull it in opposite directions. The first upgrade adds something good that the basic model ignored — human capital, the accumulated education and training that workers carry around in their heads. The second adds something bad that the basic model also ignored — fixed natural resources like land and oil, which set a hard ceiling on how much one worker can ever produce. So this module is really two mini-extensions of the general Solow framework from Chapter 5, presented back-to-back. Both keep technological progress exogenous (the rate g is still handed to us from outside). Both still use the Solow saving-rate machinery rather than full optimisation. But each plugs a specific empirical or conceptual gap that the basic model leaves open.

Why human capital? Recall from Module 1 that the basic Solow model has two well-known empirical embarrassments. First, the model predicts convergence to steady state at a fast rate — about 5 percent per year — whereas cross-country data show convergence at more like 1.4 percent per year. Second, the model predicts that the long-run elasticity of GDP per worker with respect to the investment rate s is about $\alpha/(1-\alpha) \approx 0.5$, but the data persistently deliver a slope of roughly 1.5. Mankiw, Romer and Weil noticed in their famous 1992 paper that both of these problems disappear if you add a second accumulated factor — human capital — that behaves like physical capital but whose income share accrues to workers (because you cannot separate a person from their education the way you can separate a worker from their lathe). One change, two empirical wins. That is why this extension is so beloved.

Why scarce resources? The other half of the module reaches back to the founding generation of economists — Adam Smith, David Ricardo, Thomas Malthus. The classics took land as a vital, fixed, irreplaceable input. With a growing population pressing on a constant supply of land, they argued, diminishing returns must eventually drag wages down to subsistence and end growth in "misery and vice" (Malthus' own words, from 1798). For two hundred years that prediction has been spectacularly wrong in the West — incomes have grown at around 2 percent per year for many decades. The question is: were the classics simply mistaken, or did they miss a countervailing force? We will set up a Solow model that includes land as a fixed factor, then a richer version that adds exhaustible resources (oil), and see whether technological progress is enough to outrun the resource drag. The answer turns out to be a qualified "yes" for developed economies — but with the unsettling rider that for high-fertility developing countries the classical drag is large and biting.

Two extensions, then. One pulls steady-state income upward by adding a productive accumulated factor; the other pulls steady-state growth downward by adding a non-accumulated factor in fixed supply. In both cases the apparatus is the same: write a Cobb-Douglas production function with the new factor, work out the per-effective-worker form, derive a transition equation, find the steady state, and read off the implications. The arithmetic is more demanding than in basic Solow (the MRW model has a two-dimensional phase diagram instead of a one-dimensional Solow diagram; the land model uses the capital-output ratio z = K/Y as its analysis variable instead of capital per worker). But the spirit is unchanged. If you have internalised basic Solow, this module is mostly a redeployment of familiar machinery against more realistic environments. Where the mechanical work pays off is in policy implications — strong arguments for subsidising education in the human-capital model; strong arguments against complacency about resource depletion in the land/oil model. Those policy implications are exactly the kind of thing Cozzi tests, as we will see when we get to Mock Q2 in section 6.

2. The Objects on the Table

Before we touch any dynamics let us be precise about what each model looks like. Module 3 actually presents three separate Solow variants — the human-capital extension (Lecture 3a) plus the land model and the land-and-oil model (Lecture 3b). They share most of their structure with each other and with the general Solow model of Chapter 5, but their production functions differ in revealing ways. Figure 1 lays the four models out side by side so you can see the family resemblance.

Look at the production functions row by row. The basic and general Solow models give you $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}(A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha}$, a function of just capital and effective labour. The MRW extension splits the labour exponent: now $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha} H_t^{\varphi} (A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha-\varphi}$. Cozzi typically calibrates $\alpha \approx \varphi \approx 1/3$, so labour's share is also about 1/3 — consistent with the textbook stylised fact that two-thirds of GDP accrues to workers (one third for raw labour, one third for the return to human capital). The land model swaps the human-capital exponent for a land exponent: $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}(A_t L_t)^{\beta} X^{\kappa}$ with $\alpha + \beta + \kappa = 1$ and X a constant. The oil model just adds one more factor: $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}(A_t L_t)^{\beta} X^{\kappa} E_t^{\varepsilon}$ where $E_t$ is the energy/oil input used in period t. In all four models constant returns to scale must hold — the replication argument insists that doubling every input doubles output.

Human capital $h_t$. The accumulated stock of education, training, learning-by-doing, and health embedded in workers, measured per worker as $h_t = H_t / L_t$. The crucial property: human capital cannot be separated from its owner. You can rent out a hammer; you cannot rent out your knowledge of welding without showing up to weld. This is why the return to H is paid to workers, not to a separate "human-capital owner" class. In MRW's accounting, the labour income share (around 2/3) splits into compensation for raw labour (1/3) plus return to human capital (1/3).

What about behaviour? In MRW the consumer now has two savings decisions. Total saving is split into investment in physical capital, $I_t^K = s_K Y_t$, and investment in human capital, $I_t^H = s_H Y_t$, with both rates exogenous Solow-style and $s_K + s_H < 1$. The accumulation equations have the same form as before — $K_{t+1} - K_t = I_t^K - \delta K_t$ and $H_{t+1} - H_t = I_t^H - \delta H_t$ with the same depreciation rate $\delta$ for both. Figure 2 below shows the resulting circular flow. Output is what gets produced; saving and consumption are how households dispose of it; the two saving channels accumulate into the next period's K and H, which together with A and L feed back into next period's output.

For the resource models the consumer side is simpler — there is only one accumulated factor (physical capital) and saving has its old single rate s. What is new is the resource side. Land $X$ is just constant — owned by consumers, rented inelastically to firms, the rental rate $v_t$ adjusts each period so that all land is used. Oil $R_t$ behaves differently: it gets used up. The total remaining stock obeys $R_{t+1} = R_t - E_t$, where $E_t$ is the amount of energy/oil sold to firms as input in period t. Following Nordhaus (and Cozzi's lecture), we will close the model with a simple rule: a constant share $s_E$ of the remaining stock is extracted each period, so $E_t = s_E R_t$, which means $R_t = (1 - s_E)^t R_0$. The stock decays geometrically. We are deliberately ducking the deep micro-foundations of why $s_E$ would be constant — proper resource economics would derive it from optimising producers with rational expectations about future oil prices. For our purposes the constant-fraction rule is a transparent and adequate stand-in. The textbook (and Nordhaus) consider $s_E \approx 0.005$ realistic — about half a percent of remaining oil is extracted each year.

Beyond exam scope, but useful: The textbook distinguishes Hicks-neutral from Harrod-neutral technological progress. We are using Harrod-neutral here ($A_t$ multiplies labour). It is the only specification consistent with balanced growth and constant factor income shares. Cozzi does not test the distinction in the mock, but you might want to flag the assumption mentally — it is doing real work.

3. The Core Mechanics

The mechanics in this module follow the general Solow recipe — divide everything by effective labour to absorb the trend; derive a transition equation; find where it has a fixed point. What is new is that the MRW model has two coupled state variables (k-tilde and h-tilde) rather than one, and the land model uses a clever change-of-variable (the capital-output ratio z = K/Y) to keep the analysis one-dimensional. Let us walk through each piece carefully.

3.1 The MRW transition equations

Start with the per-effective-worker definitions: $\tilde{k}_t \equiv K_t/(A_t L_t)$, $\tilde{h}_t \equiv H_t/(A_t L_t)$, $\tilde{y}_t \equiv Y_t/(A_t L_t)$. These three variables stay constant on the balanced growth path, which is the whole point of working with them. Dividing the production function $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha} H_t^{\varphi}(A_t L_t)^{1-\alpha-\varphi}$ on both sides by $A_t L_t$ gives the elegant per-effective-worker version:

$$\tilde{y}_t = \tilde{k}_t^{\alpha} \tilde{h}_t^{\varphi}.$$ Output per effective worker depends multiplicatively on the two per-effective-worker capital stocks. The exponents are exactly the K and H income shares.

For the dynamics, take the standard capital-accumulation equations $K_{t+1} - K_t = s_K Y_t - \delta K_t$ and $H_{t+1} - H_t = s_H Y_t - \delta H_t$, rearrange to $K_{t+1} = s_K Y_t + (1-\delta) K_t$ and $H_{t+1} = s_H Y_t + (1-\delta) H_t$, and divide each by $A_{t+1} L_{t+1} = (1+g)(1+n) A_t L_t$. After a line of algebra you arrive at the transition equations:

$$\tilde{k}_{t+1} = \frac{1}{(1+n)(1+g)}\left[s_K \tilde{y}_t + (1-\delta)\tilde{k}_t\right]$$ $$\tilde{h}_{t+1} = \frac{1}{(1+n)(1+g)}\left[s_H \tilde{y}_t + (1-\delta)\tilde{h}_t\right]$$ with $\tilde{y}_t = \tilde{k}_t^{\alpha}\tilde{h}_t^{\varphi}$ substituted in. Reading these: next period's per-effective-worker capital equals (this period's gross investment plus undepreciated capital) divided by the growth in the "denominator" AL. Population growth and technology growth both dilute capital per effective worker.

This is a system of two coupled, first-order, non-linear difference equations. Given $(\tilde{k}_0, \tilde{h}_0)$ it determines the entire future. Unlike basic Solow there is no clean one-dimensional Solow diagram you can draw on a single axis — we need a two-dimensional phase plane.

3.2 The Solow equations and the phase diagram

Subtracting $\tilde{k}_t$ from both sides of the first transition equation and $\tilde{h}_t$ from both sides of the second, and approximating $(1+n)(1+g) - 1 \approx n + g + ng$, gives the Solow equations:

$$\tilde{k}_{t+1} - \tilde{k}_t \approx s_K \tilde{y}_t - (n+g+\delta+ng)\tilde{k}_t$$ $$\tilde{h}_{t+1} - \tilde{h}_t \approx s_H \tilde{y}_t - (n+g+\delta+ng)\tilde{h}_t$$

These have exactly the same structure as the Solow equation you know from Chapter 5 — gross saving minus break-even investment. The difference is there are now two of them, running in parallel. The clever next step (which is unique to two-dimensional systems) is to study where each of the two state variables is standing still. Setting $\tilde{k}_{t+1} = \tilde{k}_t$ defines a curve in the $(\tilde{k}, \tilde{h})$ plane along which physical capital per effective worker does not change. Solving $s_K \tilde{k}^{\alpha}\tilde{h}^{\varphi} = (n+g+\delta+ng)\tilde{k}$ for $\tilde{h}$:

$$\text{the } \Delta\tilde{k}=0 \text{ locus: } \quad \tilde{h} = \left(\frac{n+g+\delta+ng}{s_K}\right)^{1/\varphi} \tilde{k}^{(1-\alpha)/\varphi}.$$

And similarly, setting $\tilde{h}_{t+1} = \tilde{h}_t$ gives the $\Delta\tilde{h}=0$ locus:

$$\tilde{h} = \left(\frac{s_H}{n+g+\delta+ng}\right)^{1/(1-\varphi)} \tilde{k}^{\alpha/(1-\varphi)}.$$

For the standard calibration $\alpha = \varphi = 1/3$ the first locus has $\tilde{h} \propto \tilde{k}^{2}$ — steeply rising parabolic shape. The second has $\tilde{h} \propto \sqrt{\tilde{k}}$ — flatly rising square-root shape. They cross at exactly one positive point, which is the steady state $(\tilde{k}^*, \tilde{h}^*)$. We are taking this convergence as given in this lecture — Cozzi does not require a proof — but the textbook (Chapter 6, pp. 16-18) shows convergence formally via a linearisation that reduces the two-dimensional system to a one-dimensional difference equation in $\ln\tilde{y}_t - \ln\tilde{y}^*$.

3.3 The capital-output ratio trick for the land model

Now switch to the land model. We could in principle write a transition equation for $\tilde{k}_t$ here too, but the lecture takes a different and rather elegant route: analyse the dynamics in terms of the capital-output ratio $z_t \equiv K_t / Y_t = k_t / y_t$. Why? Because along any balanced growth path $z_t$ is constant — so finding where $z$ stops moving will pin down the steady state in one variable rather than two, even though the model now has the awkward feature that "land per worker" $x_t = X/L_t$ is mechanically falling at rate $-n$ as the population grows.

Starting from the per-capita production function $y_t = k_t^{\alpha} A_t^{\beta} x_t^{\kappa}$ (where $\alpha + \beta + \kappa = 1$), divide both sides by $y_t^{\alpha}$ and use $z = k/y$:

$$z_t = k_t / y_t = k_t^{1-\alpha} A_t^{-\beta} x_t^{-\kappa}.$$

Lead this forward one period (use the capital-accumulation equation $K_{t+1} = sY_t + (1-\delta)K_t$ in the form $k_{t+1} = (s y_t + (1-\delta)k_t)/(1+n)$, plug in $A_{t+1} = (1+g)A_t$ and $x_{t+1} = x_t/(1+n)$), and after rearranging — which exploits the identity $\alpha + \beta + \kappa = 1$ — you reach the surprisingly clean transition curve from Lecture 3b p11:

$$z_{t+1} = \left[\frac{1}{(1+n)(1+g)}\right]^{\beta} \left[s + (1-\delta)z_t\right]^{1-\alpha} z_t^{\alpha}.$$ A single one-dimensional difference equation in z, even though the underlying model has K, L, A, X all evolving differently. The reduction works because the capital-output ratio is the right scaling variable when there is a fixed factor.

3.4 Four properties of the transition curve

You should be able to convince yourself of the following four properties — the lecture asks them as an exercise (Lecture 3b p11, textbook ch7 p5). Each tells you something about the shape of the curve in Figure 4 below.

4. Properties and Steady States

Now we cash in. The mechanics gave us transition equations and convergence to a steady state. The point of this section is to extract concrete formulas for what the economy looks like at that steady state, what makes it shift, and what its growth rate is. The MRW model and the land/oil models have very different things to say, so we will take them in turn.

4.1 MRW: steady-state stocks, output, and elasticities

Setting both Solow equations to zero simultaneously and solving the resulting two-equation system gives the steady state values (Lecture 3a p18; textbook ch6 p7):

$$\tilde{k}^* = \left[\frac{s_K^{1-\varphi}\, s_H^{\varphi}}{n + g + \delta + ng}\right]^{1/(1-\alpha-\varphi)}, \qquad \tilde{h}^* = \left[\frac{s_K^{\alpha}\, s_H^{1-\alpha}}{n + g + \delta + ng}\right]^{1/(1-\alpha-\varphi)}.$$ Inserting into $\tilde{y} = \tilde{k}^{\alpha}\tilde{h}^{\varphi}$ gives the steady-state output per effective worker: $$\tilde{y}^* = \left(\frac{s_K}{n+g+\delta+ng}\right)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha-\varphi)} \left(\frac{s_H}{n+g+\delta+ng}\right)^{\varphi/(1-\alpha-\varphi)}.$$

Now the empirically interesting part. Take logs and look at the elasticities. The elasticity of $\tilde{y}^*$ with respect to $s_K$ (holding $s_H$ fixed) is $\alpha/(1-\alpha-\varphi)$. With the calibration $\alpha = \varphi = 1/3$, that comes to $(1/3)/(1/3) = 1$. Compare that to the basic Solow elasticity of $\alpha/(1-\alpha) = 1/2$. The MRW elasticity is twice as large. Same goes for the elasticity with respect to $s_H$. And the elasticity with respect to $n+g+\delta$ is the much-stronger $-(\alpha+\varphi)/(1-\alpha-\varphi) = -2$, whereas the basic Solow elasticity is only $-\alpha/(1-\alpha) = -1/2$. Both of these adjustments push the model towards the empirically observed regression coefficients of roughly 0.6 and 0.9 instead of the basic Solow's predicted 0.5. That is exactly the "both empirical problems solved" payoff MRW were after.

Why is the elasticity so much larger? Geometric intuition: in basic Solow, raising $s$ accumulates more $k$, but the higher $y$ this generates only feeds back into more $k$ (one channel). In MRW, raising $s_K$ accumulates more $k$, the higher $y$ then drives both more $k$ and more $h$, the higher $h$ further raises $y$, and so on. The feedback loop has an extra link. The same loop is what slows convergence: the model takes longer to settle because adjustments in $k$ and $h$ must mutually accommodate each other.

4.2 Comparative statics: the s_H shock

What happens when a country permanently raises its human-capital investment rate? Figure 5 traces the shift. Before the policy: the economy sits at the old intersection E. The $\Delta\tilde{h}=0$ locus shifts upward (raising $s_H$ allows a higher $\tilde{h}$ at any $\tilde{k}$ to be self-sustaining). The $\Delta\tilde{k}=0$ locus stays put. After the shift, the old steady state E is no longer a steady state — at E we now have $\tilde{h}_{t+1} > \tilde{h}_t$, so human capital starts to accumulate. Once $\tilde{h}$ has risen, the higher productivity it generates pulls $\tilde{k}$ up too. The trajectory curves up and rightward into the new intersection E' (Lecture 3a p26 covers this).

4.3 The MRW Golden Rule

Along the steady-state path, consumption per worker grows at rate g. Its level is $c_t = (1 - s_K - s_H)\, y_t$, and with the formula for $\tilde{y}^*$ above the steady-state level becomes a function of $s_K$ and $s_H$. Maximising consumption with respect to both saving rates is now a two-variable problem, but the first-order conditions come out beautifully (Slides_Session3 p18):

MRW Golden Rule: $$s_K^{**} = \alpha, \qquad s_H^{**} = \varphi.$$ Each saving rate should equal the corresponding income-share exponent. With $\alpha = \varphi = 1/3$, the golden-rule saving rates are 1/3 each — a combined saving of 2/3 of output. Most real economies have $s_K \approx 0.2$ and $s_H \approx 0.15$, well below golden rule, so subsidising more saving (especially in education) tends to raise long-run consumption.

The intuition is clean: each factor's optimal saving rate equals its share. This is the natural generalisation of the basic-Solow golden rule $s^{**} = \alpha$. If you remember nothing else from this subsection, remember the pattern: golden-rule savings rate equals factor income share.

4.4 Land model: the two growth drags

Switch to the land model now. The capital-output ratio converges to a constant $z^*$, which means K and Y grow at the same rate, which (combined with the labour-augmenting accounting) pins down the steady-state growth rate of y. The result (Lecture 3b p13, textbook ch7 p6):

$$g^y = \frac{\beta\, g - \kappa\, n}{\beta + \kappa}.$$ Steady-state growth of GDP per worker. Two terms: a positive contribution from technological progress (scaled down by the share $\beta/(\beta+\kappa) < 1$), and a negative contribution from population growth (scaled by $\kappa/(\beta+\kappa)$).

The two negative effects are what the textbook calls "growth drags". Drag #1: technology is less effective than it would be without a fixed factor, because the share of output that has to be allocated to fixed land cannot be augmented by better technology. Drag #2: population growth eats into per-worker income, because adding workers without adding land triggers diminishing returns. Both drags vanish if $\kappa = 0$ (back to basic Solow) and both intensify the larger $\kappa$ is.

The structure of $g^y$ gives the classical economists their day in court. Consider two extreme cases:

CaseAssumptiong^y reduces toVerdict
Classicalg = 0, n > 0$-\kappa n /(\beta+\kappa) < 0$Smith/Ricardo/Malthus correct. Without technological progress, growing population on fixed land implies declining income per worker — "misery and vice" at the end.
Moderng > 0, n = 0$\beta g/(\beta+\kappa) \in (0, g)$Positive growth, but slower than g. Technology rules, but land still drags.

4.5 Adding oil: the depletion drag

Now bring in the exhaustible resource. The land-and-oil production function is $Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}(A_t L_t)^{\beta} X^{\kappa} E_t^{\varepsilon}$ with $\alpha+\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon = 1$. Energy input is $E_t = s_E R_t$ where $R_t$ is the remaining stock decaying at rate $s_E$. Going through the same balanced-growth analysis (Lecture 3b p20) gives the steady-state growth rate:

$$g^y = \frac{\beta}{\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon}\, g - \frac{\kappa+\varepsilon}{\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon}\, n - \frac{\varepsilon}{\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon}\, s_E.$$ Three terms. Positive technology contribution (now scaled down even more), negative population drag, and a brand-new depletion drag $-\varepsilon s_E/(\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon)$ — a constant subtraction proportional to how fast we are using up oil.

The depletion drag is what makes oil different from land. Even at $n = 0$ and $g = 0$, an economy with exhaustible resources is losing ground every year, because the oil input is mechanically shrinking. With a relatively modest extraction rate $s_E = 0.005$ (the textbook's Nordhaus-inspired calibration) and an oil share $\varepsilon = 0.1$, the depletion drag is about 0.06 percentage points per year — not huge in isolation. But combined with $\kappa = 0.1$ for land, the population drag is bigger than in the land-only model: $(\kappa+\varepsilon)/(\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon) = 0.2/0.8 = 1/4$ multiplies n. 5. Worked Examples

Three numerical examples, then the simulation sketch that the mock exam explicitly asks for, then a quick look at how all this performs against real-world data. The numbers in this section are the exact ones from Cozzi's slides and the textbook so you can recognise them on the exam.

5.1 MRW with Cozzi's baseline calibration

Take the parameter set from Lecture 3a p24 and Slides_Session3 p19:

$\alpha = \varphi = 1/3$, $s_K = 0.2$, $s_H = 0.15$, $\delta = 0.06$, $n = 0$, $g = 0.015$.

Plug into the steady-state formula for $\tilde{k}^*$:

$$\tilde{k}^* = \left[\frac{(0.2)^{2/3}(0.15)^{1/3}}{0 + 0.015 + 0.06 + 0}\right]^{3} = \left[\frac{0.342 \cdot 0.531}{0.075}\right]^{3} = \left[\frac{0.1817}{0.075}\right]^{3} = (2.423)^3 \approx 14.22.$$

And symmetrically:

$$\tilde{h}^* = \left[\frac{(0.2)^{1/3}(0.15)^{2/3}}{0.075}\right]^{3} = (2.20)^3 \approx 10.67.$$

These match the lecture's claimed steady-state values to two decimal places. Steady-state output per effective worker is then $\tilde{y}^* = (14.22)^{1/3}(10.67)^{1/3} \approx 2.42 \cdot 2.20 \approx 5.32$, and steady-state consumption per effective worker is $(1 - 0.2 - 0.15)\cdot 5.32 \approx 3.46$. The lecture's simulation starts from $\tilde{k}_0 = 16$ (above $\tilde{k}^*$) and $\tilde{h}_0 = 2$ (far below $\tilde{h}^*$), creating an interesting transition where physical capital initially falls while human capital climbs — a path that visibly hooks back toward (14.22, 10.67) in the phase diagram.

Most economies are saving less in physical capital than the golden rule would prescribe, so policies that raise $s_K$ would (in this model) raise long-run consumption. The same logic applies to $s_H$: try setting $s_H = \varphi = 1/3$ and the whole hill jumps upward.

5.2 Solow with land — the Western parameter set

Take Cozzi's calibration for the land model (Lecture 3b p14): $\alpha = \kappa = 0.2$, $\beta = 0.6$. The growth-rate formula gives:

$$g^y = \frac{0.6 \, g - 0.2 \, n}{0.6 + 0.2} = \frac{3}{4} g - \frac{1}{4} n.$$

For typical Western numbers $g = 2\%$, $n = 1\%$ per year:

$$g^y = 0.75 \cdot 0.02 - 0.25 \cdot 0.01 = 0.015 - 0.0025 = 0.0125 = 1.25\%.$$

The land drag reduces growth from 2% (basic Solow) to 1.25% — a noticeable but bearable bite. Now flip to a developing-country case: $g = 0.5\%$, $n = 3\%$:

$$g^y = 0.75 \cdot 0.005 - 0.25 \cdot 0.03 = 0.00375 - 0.0075 = -0.00375 = -0.375\%.$$

Negative. The classical Malthusian prediction comes alive. The threshold condition for positive growth in this case is $g > n/3$, easily met when $n = 1\%$ but not when $n = 3\%$. This is one reason demographic transition (reducing $n$) is so important for poor countries' long-run prospects.

5.3 Solow with land and oil — adding the depletion drag

The textbook and Lecture 3b p21 use: $\alpha = 0.2$, $\beta = 0.6$, $\kappa = \varepsilon = 0.1$. So $\beta + \kappa + \varepsilon = 0.8$, and:

$$g^y = \frac{0.6}{0.8} g - \frac{0.2}{0.8} n - \frac{0.1}{0.8} s_E = 0.75\, g - 0.25\, n - 0.125\, s_E.$$

With the textbook's $n = 0.01$ and $s_E = 0.005$, the constant subtraction is $0.25 \cdot 0.01 + 0.125 \cdot 0.005 = 0.0025 + 0.000625 = 0.003125$, or roughly 0.3 percentage points. So:

$$g^y = 0.75\, g - 0.3\%.$$

If $g = 2.4\%$, then $g^y = 0.018 - 0.003 = 1.5\%$. Still healthy. Figure 8 illustrates the oil-side of the story directly: the stock $R_t$ decaying geometrically at rate $s_E$, and the extraction $E_t = s_E R_t$ tracking it.

If oil becomes prohibitively expensive long before it actually runs out, alternative energy sources or technologies need to replace it. Otherwise the model breaks down at the limit.

5.4 How to numerically simulate the transition equations (Mock Q2 part 2)

This is what Mock Exam Q2 explicitly asks for: "Sketch how you would numerically simulate the transition equations with a software of your choice (Excel, or other)." A clean answer should be a pseudo-algorithmic walk-through, not actual runnable code. Here is the template, applied to the MRW model:

Algorithm for MRW transition simulation in Excel (or Python, or anything that does loops).

  1. Choose parameters. Fix $\alpha, \varphi, s_K, s_H, \delta, n, g$. Use the lecture calibration $\alpha = \varphi = 1/3$, $s_K = 0.2$, $s_H = 0.15$, $\delta = 0.06$, $n = 0$, $g = 0.015$ as a starting point.
  2. Compute steady-state targets. Apply the closed-form expressions: $\tilde{k}^* = 14.22$, $\tilde{h}^* = 10.67$. This gives you a checkpoint — the simulation should converge to these.
  3. Pick initial conditions. Choose $\tilde{k}_0$ and $\tilde{h}_0$ different from steady state. E.g. $\tilde{k}_0 = 16$, $\tilde{h}_0 = 2$.
  4. In each row t = 0, 1, 2, ..., T (say T = 300):
    • Compute $\tilde{y}_t = \tilde{k}_t^{\alpha}\tilde{h}_t^{\varphi}$ using values from row t.
    • Compute $\tilde{k}_{t+1} = [s_K \tilde{y}_t + (1-\delta)\tilde{k}_t]/((1+n)(1+g))$.
    • Compute $\tilde{h}_{t+1} = [s_H \tilde{y}_t + (1-\delta)\tilde{h}_t]/((1+n)(1+g))$.
  5. Plot. Two useful graphs: (a) a phase diagram with $\tilde{k}_t$ on the x-axis and $\tilde{h}_t$ on the y-axis (and the two Δ=0 loci overlaid as reference); (b) time paths $\tilde{k}_t$, $\tilde{h}_t$, $\tilde{y}_t$ versus t.
  6. Check convergence. Confirm $\tilde{k}_T \approx 14.22$, $\tilde{h}_T \approx 10.67$ after enough periods.
  7. Vary one parameter and re-run. Bump $s_H$ from 0.15 to 0.20 and watch the new steady state appear at a higher $\tilde{h}^*$. This isolates the comparative-static effect from the transition.

For the land model the same template applies, except (i) the state variable is $z_t = K_t/Y_t$ instead of two separate stocks; (ii) the transition is $z_{t+1} = [1/((1+n)(1+g))]^{\beta}[s + (1-\delta)z_t]^{1-\alpha}z_t^{\alpha}$; (iii) compute the level $y_t$ separately by also tracking $A_t = (1+g)^t A_0$ and $X/L_t = (X/L_0)/(1+n)^t$, then using the per-capita production function.

Cozzi will give you full credit for the steps above plus a brief sentence saying what the simulation is for (verifying convergence visually, exploring comparative statics without solving algebra). You do not need to write actual code — "sketch" in his wording means exactly what we did above.

5.5 Cross-country empirics

Finally, the empirical test. The textbook (ch6 pp10-12) estimates the steady-state equation derived from the MRW model:

$$\ln y^i_{03} = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1\big[\ln s_K^i - \ln(n^i + g + \delta)\big] + \gamma_2\big[\ln s_H^i - \ln(n^i + g + \delta)\big] + \text{error}.$$

OLS across 65 countries gives $\hat{\gamma}_1 = 0.59$ and $\hat{\gamma}_2 = 0.93$. Both close to the theoretical value of 1 (under the calibration $\alpha = \varphi = 1/3$). Adjusted $R^2$ rises from 0.63 (basic Solow) to 0.79 (MRW). About four-fifths of the cross-country variation in income per worker is explained by saving rates and population growth, with the coefficients in line with the theory. This is the empirical payoff. Compare against basic Solow's empirical slope of $\hat{\gamma} = 1.52$ (way off from the predicted 0.5), and you see what MRW achieved with one production-function modification. Section 6 picks up the policy implications.

6. Connections and Common Traps

6.1 Exam hot-spot: Mock Q2

The single most exam-relevant thing in this module is Mock Exam Question 2: "Explain the Solow model with land and natural resources. Sketch how you would numerically simulate the transition equations with a software of your choice (Excel, or other)." Two components, exactly matching the "technical + applied" pattern documented in EXAM_PROFILE. A good answer needs:

  1. The technical part (about 60% of the words): Set up the production function $Y = K^{\alpha}(AL)^{\beta}X^{\kappa}E^{\varepsilon}$ with $\alpha + \beta + \kappa + \varepsilon = 1$, explain that X is fixed and $E_t = s_E R_t$ depletes the oil stock. Define $z = K/Y$. State the transition equation. State the four properties that give convergence to $z^*$. Give the steady-state growth rate $g^y = (\beta g - (\kappa+\varepsilon)n - \varepsilon s_E)/(\beta + \kappa + \varepsilon)$ and label the three terms (technology contribution, population drag, depletion drag).
  2. The applied part (about 40%): The pseudo-algorithm in section 5.4 above. Six to eight bullet points, including: choose parameters, compute the steady-state target $z^*$, iterate the transition equation row by row, plot the path, verify convergence, optionally re-run with a changed parameter. Cozzi explicitly wrote "Excel, or other" so don't waste words on programming language choice.

6.2 Education subsidies: why and when

The MRW model has very specific policy implications. The structural-policy section of Lecture 3a (pp 21-22) lists four reasons why governments are right to subsidise education even though the model itself just treats $s_H$ as a parameter:

Four market imperfections that justify education subsidies:
  1. Imperfect credit markets. Education is an investment whose payoff is the future income of the educated person. Banks cannot perfectly distinguish able borrowers from unable ones (adverse selection), so they lend too little against future labour income. Public subsidies plug the gap.
  2. Imperfect insurance markets. Education sometimes fails (a student drops out, a career change doesn't pan out). Private insurance against failed education is rare because of moral hazard — insured students would work less hard. Subsidies socialise some of the risk.
  3. Positive externalities. An engineer is more productive surrounded by literate colleagues. Part of the social return on each person's education accrues to others, so private investment is too low.
  4. Distributional concerns. If inequality is high, the poor cannot afford education. The rich cannot compensate by over-educating themselves (decreasing returns: one rich person cannot study for 100 years to make up for five poor ones each not studying for 20 years).

These are microeconomic arguments, not part of the MRW model proper. But the strong empirical effect of $s_H$ on output that MRW documents suggests they are quantitatively serious — the size of the policy lever is real, even if the model itself does not justify pulling it.

7. Summary and Formula Reference

One-sentence summary

Module 3 plugs two empirical gaps in basic Solow — by adding human capital as a second accumulated factor (MRW raises the long-run elasticity of $y$ with respect to investment from 0.5 to about 1) and by recognising that fixed and depleting natural resources put predictable drags on the long-run growth rate.

Key results to recognise on the exam

  • MRW production function: $Y = K^{\alpha}H^{\varphi}(AL)^{1-\alpha-\varphi}$, with calibration $\alpha = \varphi = 1/3$.
  • MRW steady state: $\tilde{k}^* = [s_K^{1-\varphi}s_H^{\varphi}/(n+g+\delta+ng)]^{1/(1-\alpha-\varphi)}$, and analogous for $\tilde{h}^*$.
  • MRW golden rule: $s_K = \alpha$ and $s_H = \varphi$ — "saving rate equals factor share".
  • Solow with land growth rate: $g^y = (\beta g - \kappa n)/(\beta + \kappa)$. Two drags.
  • Solow with land + oil growth rate: $g^y = [\beta g - (\kappa+\varepsilon)n - \varepsilon s_E]/(\beta+\kappa+\varepsilon)$. Three drags.
  • Classical case ($g=0, n>0$): negative steady-state growth. Modern case ($g>0, n=0$): positive but smaller than g.
  • Empirical fit (MRW): $\hat{\gamma}_1 \approx 0.59, \hat{\gamma}_2 \approx 0.93$, $R^2$ rises 0.63 → 0.79 across 65 countries.

What is not in this lecture

A few things you should know exist but that we did not derive here. The textbook proves convergence in MRW via a linearisation that reduces the two-dimensional system to a one-dimensional equation in $\ln\tilde{y}_t - \ln\tilde{y}^*$ (Ch 6 pp 16-18). The same chapter argues that the implied rate of convergence in MRW is much closer to the empirically observed 1-2% than basic Solow's 5%. The textbook also tests the land model empirically by regressing log GDP per worker on log land per worker (across 79 countries), and the oil model by using World Bank "subsoil assets per capita" data — both deliver positive and statistically significant coefficients on the resource term, consistent with the model's prediction that more resources per worker means a higher level (not growth rate) of income.
