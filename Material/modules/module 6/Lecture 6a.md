- Involuntary unemployment is by definition almost the same as excess supply of labour. Consider a labour market:
- Unemployment: the wage is stuck at a 'too high' level. Shouldn't this imply a downward pressure on wages which would in itself eliminate unemployment? This is the key question in all theory of unemployment.

## Page 5

- Under perfectly competitive wage flexibility (by definition meaning that the nominal wage rate always adjusts to create a real wage rate that equates supply and demand of labour), there can be no unemployment.
- In other words: For unemployment to exist, some factor must have prevented the real wage from adjusting to a level that would equate (wage taking) supply and demand of labour.
- Such a factor is called a 'wage rigidity'.
- For unemployment to prevail, there must be wage rigidity.
- Why and how does wage rigidity arise?

## LONG-RUN REAL WAGE RIGIDITY AND STRUCTURAL UNEMPLOYMENT

- Assume that there were no new shocks and that the time horizon is long enough to make nominal wage rigidities irrelevant. How can there be unemployment?
- Only if unemployment for some reason does not imply a downward pressure on the real wage rate. Such a reason is called a long-run, real wage rigidity.
- Long-run, real wage rigidity implies that the economy's general equilibrium system establishing the relative/real prices cannot be characterized by perfect competition.
- Unemployment arising from long-run, real wage rigidity is called long-run, structural or natural unemployment .
- Long-run, real wage rigidity is a factor that can explain how unemployment and fully adjusted wages and prices can coexist.
